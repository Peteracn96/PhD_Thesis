\chapter{Outlook and Conclusions}
\label{ch:Outlook}
\glsresetall
As a final say in this dissertation, after all the concepts from the literature reviewed throughout the thesis, and applying them for studying light--matter interactions in semiconducting and insulating \bd~materials, there is pertinence in providing an overview of everything that was discussed.
Throughout the course of the PhD studies, and as a byproduct of the research undertaken, many novel ideas naturally arose.
However, there was not enough time to pursue them, but we provide a brief discussion.

\noindent
\textbf{\hyperref[ch:classical_electrodynamics]{Chapter 2\,} \textcolor{black}{|} Classical Electrodynamics of Continuous Media}

This chapter had the purpose of providing a simple, but complete, technical introduction for the reader, consisting of the classical framework for electrodynamics in solids and continuous media.
Several key concepts in this work were introduced, specifically those that can quantify light--matter interactions through material response functions.
The physics related to electromagnetic phenomena at planar interfaces between different media was also discussed, with the intent of contextualizing the topic of \bd~materials, the focus of this thesis.
The dielectric function, optical conductivity, and their interrelation in optically responsive systems were specially emphasized and particularized for \bd~materials.
As these materials present many phenomena of interest at nanometric scales coming from quantum effects, the tools herein introduced were deemed insufficient.
The necessary tools were provided in the two chapters that followed.

\noindent
\textbf{\hyperref[ch:quantum_mechanics_solids]{Chapter 3\,}  \textcolor{black}{|} Quantum Mechanics of Electrons in Crystalline Solids}

Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_mechanics_solids} dwelled on the basic principles of the quantum mechanics machinery, and applied to solids, allowing for describing physical properties at length scales of the order of the nanometer, or even smaller.
Central concepts in the quantum theory of solids were mentioned, such as the Bloch theorem and Bloch states, first-principles and second-principles approaches for band structure calculations, and many-body perturbation theory.
This last one offered a different point of view on the dielectric function.
Within the formalism of quantum many-particle physics, one could describe response functions at the microscopic level, shedding light on the concepts introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:classical_electrodynamics}.
Nonetheless, an approximation had to be introduced for the dielectric function, the \gls{rpa}, which permits computing the dielectric function in practice, albeit at the noninteracting level.
Doing so was postponed for the end of the thesis, only after introducing and studying excitons in the subsequent chapters.

\noindent
\textbf{\hyperref[ch:Excitons]{Chapter 4\,} \textcolor{black}{|}  Excitonic effects in Two-dimensional Semiconductors}

The purpose of the previous chapter was actually twofold; provide the required concepts of quantum mechanics for a later stage in the thesis, and to set a background to prepare the reader for this chapter.
It dealt with the exciton---a composite particle formed by a tightly bound electron--hole pair.
With the tools from the previous chapter, we could describe excitons at different degrees of complexity.
The simplest description was that of the Wannier equation, which is nothing more than the Schr\"odinger equation for the electron--hole pair.
Although used later in this thesis, we already had in mind that this approach is not accurate enough for more complicated systems.
Therefore, we introduced the \gls{bse} formalism.
Two different degrees of approximation for it were given, both informed by the Hamiltonian of the material: first, using a low-energy description of the Hamiltonian, that allowed for recovering the Wannier equation; second, we introduced the exact diagonalization method, which is informed by the precise details of the Hamiltonian.
In this way, the reader was well-informed to understand the research included in the next two chapters.

\noindent
\textbf{\hyperref[ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice]{Chapter 5\,} \textcolor{black}{|} Optical Properties of Nanostructured hBN}

In this chapter, we investigated the optical and excitonic properties of a nanostructured \gls{hbn} monolayer, a \bd~material simple enough so that we can easily describe excitons in it, but also interesting in its properties in bulk or single-layer form as the active element in experiments.
This material has already been widely studied in the literature, both in theory and in experiments.
It is so simple that we can infer its excitonic properties by using semi-analytical calculations.
We proposed a way of taking advantage of its strong optical resonances by engineering the electronic dispersion, so that \gls{hbn} can be made useful in a wide range of frequencies of interest.
By applying an electrostatic periodic potential on \gls{hbn}, we could induce simultaneously an increasing anisotropy in the low-energy dispersion and a diminished bandgap when one increases the strength of the potential.
This could be predicted with an easy analytical expression for the renormalized low-energy Hamiltonian.
Using the variational principle to get the excitonic energies and states with the Wannier equation, we saw that the excitons become less and less tightly bound as the strength of the potential grows.
As a consequence for the optical conductivity, the oscillator strength for the excitonic resonances is enhanced with the increasing anisotropy.
At the same time, the energies of the excitonic resonances in the optical absorption spectrum of \gls{hbn} red-shift.
Lastly, we could make predictions on the sub-gap photonic dispersion for the modified \gls{hbn} monolayer, resultant from the strong coupling of the exciton with the \gls{em} field---the exciton--polariton.
Interestingly, we saw that this light--matter hybrid presents a dispersion quite different from that of free light.
The field confinement is much higher, of the order of the nanometer, and in the case of extreme anisotropy, the dispersion changes qualitatively, backbending at the resonance corresponding to the first excited state.
This is in agreement with theoretical predictions in the existing literature.
We concluded that by nanostructuring \gls{hbn}, we can tailor the nanophotonic properties of this material, making it more versatile for practical applications.

\noindent
\textbf{\hyperref[ch:screening]{Chapter 6\,} \textcolor{black}{|} Electrostatic Screening in Two-dimensional materials}

In this chapter, we started by revisiting the dielectric function from Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_mechanics_solids}, but in two dimensions.
We explained in detail how to compute it in practice.
This had to be done numerically, since the problem of calculating the dielectric function cannot be done analytically, apart from some simple cases.
In this thesis, we work with semiconducting and insulating materials, where local-field effects have to be taken into account.
That makes this problem challenging.
The dielectric function is now an infinite matrix, which has to be truncated and inverted to compute the screened Coulomb potential.
Although these issues are not trivial, we found that the reduced dimensionality of the implementation allowed for obtaining results comparable with those from the literature in \abinit~methods, much more established in the literature, but for a fraction of the computational effort.
Noticeably, the agreement between the static macroscopic dielectric function from our \bd~approach agreed very well with the \abinit~one in the long-wavelength limit.
By extending the \bd~model to a quasi-2D one, where the thickness of the layer is accounted for in the dielectric function by adopting a mixed momentum-space representation, the results agree even better with \abinit~ones.
The nonlocal behavior of the \bd~effective dielectric function agrees with the Rytova--Keldysh one in the low momentum limit, and is accurately captured beyond that limit.
These results were applied to the calculation of the excitons with the code, which was extended to incorporate the numerical screening in the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian.
The excitons did not completely agree with the literature, which reports ambiguous results coming from models and/or methods.
Nonetheless, the results were still reasonable, and not very distant from any available experimental ones.

We investigated interesting problems that have natural extensions that sparked our curiosity.
As always, we think more could have been done if we had more time.
One question we wonder ourselves with is related to the interaction of the exciton with light in the quantum picture.
The electron--hole complex eventually decouples after a certain time, and the exciton decays while emitting a photon.
This could have been studied in the context of Chapter~\ref{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice}~\cite{Ninhos_Tserkezis_Mortensen_Peres_2024}, similarly to how it was done in Refs.~\cite{Ventura_2019,PhysRevB.111.075411}.
The radiative decay time can be calculated using Fermi's golden rule~\cite{Griffiths_2023,Sakurai,Zettili_2009}, which involves a matrix element of the dipole operator as seen in Chapter~\ref{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice}, where the dipole approximation was assumed.
However, in the literature there is an interest in the strong-coupling regime of light--matter interactions in cavities~\cite{cavity_2023,cavity_2024}.
In a cavity, the exciton radiative decay time depends on the exciton center-of-mass momentum~\cite{PhysRevB.111.075411}, and we need to go beyond the dipole approximation.
This has been done previously in Ref.~\cite{PhysRevB.86.085304}, albeit for finite-size systems.
As far as we know, this has never been done for extended systems.
In that case, one has to use the velocity gauge, and the matrix element reads $\langle X |\exp{\ii \bQ \vdot \br} \mathbf{v}|1_{\mathrm{photon}} \rangle$.
With our code we could compute it, by inserting a resolution of the identity in a Bloch basis between $\exp{\ii \bQ \vdot \br}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, because we have the matrix elements of each factor.
In turn, this idea also allows for computing the noninteracting and excitonic transverse optical conductivities for finite momentum, something we have never seen as well.

Naturally, with a quasi-2D approach for the dielectric function, we could extend the calculation of the screening and the excitons to the multilayer case.
How to do that is explained in Appendix~\ref{app:Q2D_multilayer}.
One can also implement the screening in real space with the noninteracting polarizability in real space as per Eq.~\eqref{eq:polarizability_RPA_def}, and compute the screened Coulomb potential directly in real space.
This idea is discussed in detail in the PhD thesis of A.~J.~Ur\'ia-\'Alvarez in Ref.~\cite{Alex_PhD}, Section 2.2.3 therein.
At last but not the least, it is also possible to extend the code to compute the dielectric function in the dynamic limit and at finite temperatures, for both metallic and insulating \bd~materials.
A striking application could be graphene, studied extensively in the literature~\cite{Andre_Goncalves_2020,GoncalvesPeres:2016,Cosme_2025}, a good material to compare with the results already known within plasmonics.
We hope our research has sparked the curiosity of the reader on these topics.
Moreover, we hope that it can serve as an inspiration for the community in general and reignite the research on \bd~materials to push further the boundaries of knowledge.
