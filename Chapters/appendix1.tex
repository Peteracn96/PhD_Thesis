\chapter{The Fourier Transform: definitions and identities}
\label{app:fourier_transform}

In this appendix, we summarize the definitions used for the Fourier transform and its inverse used throughout this thesis.
The discussion presented here is adapted from Appendix A of Ref~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004}.
In the sense that at the end we consider as well the case of discrete translation symmetry, this discussion is also a generalization of the content presented by H.~Bruus and K.~Flensbergin~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004}.

\section{Fourier transforms in time and frequency domains}

We define the direct and inverse Fourier transforms between time and frequency domains as

\begin{equation}
    f(t) = \mathcal{F}^{-1} \{f(\omega)\} (t) =\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\dd \omega}{2\pi}f(\omega)\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}, \qq{and} f(\omega) = \mathcal{F} \{f(t)\} (\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd t\,f(t)\,\ee^{\ii\omega t},
\end{equation}

\noindent respectively, where we used the symbols $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{F}^{-1}$ to denote Fourier transform and its inverse, respectively.
The following relations involving the Dirac delta function are also valid:

\begin{equation}
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\dd \omega}{2\pi}f(\omega)\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}=\delta(t),\qq{and} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd t\,f(t)\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}=\delta(\omega)\,.
\end{equation}

A two-point function in time $f(t,t')$ can be written in terms of its Fourier components as

\begin{equation}
    f(t,t')=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd \omega\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd \omega'\,f(\omega,\omega')\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\ee^{\ii\omega't'}.
\end{equation}

\noindent If this function enjoys continuous time translation symmetry such that $f(t,t')=f(t-t')$, which will always be the case in this work, then one can show that the Fourier transformed function can only be nonzero if $\omega=\omega'$.
More than that, it is of the form $f(\omega,\omega')=\widetilde{f}(\omega)\delta(\omega-\omega')$, and $\widetilde{f}(\omega)$ is the Fourier transform of $f(t-t')$ when we consider the time difference $t-t'$ as a single variable.
Therefore, we are allowed to write

\begin{equation}
    f(t-t')=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dd \omega\,\widetilde{f}(\omega)\,\ee^{-\ii\omega(t-t')}.
\end{equation}


\section{Fourier transforms in real and reciprocal spaces}

\subsection{One-point functions}

Let us consider first a system of finite volume $\vol$ in real space with periodic, or Born--von Karman~\cite{Simon_Oxford_2013}, boundary conditions.
Then, any function $f(\br)$ can be written in a Fourier series as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:inverse_Fourier_discrete}
    f(\br)=\frac{1}{\vol}\sum_{\bk}f_{\bk}\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br},
\end{equation}

\noindent with the momentum $\bk$ assuming discrete values, and

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:direct_Fourier_discrete}
    f_{\bk}=\int_{\vol}\dr\,f(\br)\,\ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br}.
\end{equation}

\noindent The following relations hold

\begin{equation}
    \int_{\vol}\dr\,f(\br)\,\ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br}=\vol\delta_{\bk,\bzero} \qq{and} \frac{1}{\vol}\sum_{\bk}\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br}=\delta(\br)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\delta_{\bk,\mathbf{q}}$ is the Kronecker delta.

If we let the volume of the region $\vol \to \infty$ tend to infinity, then $\bk$ assumes continuous values, and the sums over momentum transform to integrals by the rule $\sum_{\bk}\to\frac{\vol}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dd \bk$.
In this way, relations~\eqref{eq:inverse_Fourier_discrete} and~\eqref{eq:direct_Fourier_discrete} can be written as

\begin{equation}
    f(\br)=\int\frac{\dd \bk}{(2\pi)^{3}}f(\bk)\,\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br} \qq{and} f(\bk)=\int \dr f(\br)\,\ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br},
\end{equation}

\noindent respectively, and both relations below

\begin{equation}
    \int\frac{\dd \bk}{(2\pi)^{3}}\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br}=\delta(\br), \qq{and} \int \dd \br\, \ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br} = (2\pi)^{3}\delta(\bk)\,,
\end{equation}
 
\noindent hold as well.
If the system under consideration is \bd~and not \gls{3d}, then instead of volume $\vol$ we have area $A$, the momentum $\bk$ is a \bd~vector, the integrals over volume become integrals over area, and whenever we have $(2\pi)^{3}$ we would have $(2\pi)^{2}$ instead.

\subsection{Two-point functions}

Just like in the time domain example, there can also be real space resolved two-point functions.
For a general function $f(\br,\br')$ where the position is \gls{3d} and defined in a region of infinite volume, its inverse Fourier transform writes

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:inverse_Fourier_two_point}
    f(\br,\br')=\int\frac{\dd \bk}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{\dd \bk'}{(2\pi)^{3}}\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br}\ee^{-\ii \bk' \vdot \br'}f(\bk,\bk')=\int\frac{\dd \bk}{(2\pi)^{3}}\widetilde{f}(\bk)\,\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot (\br-\br')},
\end{equation}

\noindent where the second equality holds for translation invariant systems, and imposing $f(\bk,\bk')=\delta(\bk-\bk')\widetilde{f}(\bk)$.
The direct Fourier transform then writes

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{f}(\bk)=\int\dr\,\ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br}f(\br)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent by choosing $\br'=\bzero$ without loss of generality.
Something similar for the finite volume case writes

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:finite_volume_two_point_inverse_Fourier}
    f(\br,\br')=\frac{1}{\vol^{2}}\sum_{\bk,\bk'}\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br}\ee^{-\ii \bk' \vdot \br'}f(\bk,\bk')=\frac{1}{\vol}\sum_{\bk}\widetilde{f}(\bk)\,\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot (\br-\br')}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the second equality is for systems with translation invariance, and identifying $f(\bk,\bk')=\vol\delta_{\bk,\bk'}\widetilde{f}(\bk)$.

We give a typical, and very important example; the Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential.
The derivations are skipped, and only the final results will be stated.
The Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential $v_{c}$, that in electron-Volt and in real space reads

\begin{equation}
    v_{c}(\br-\br')=\frac{1}{4\pi \epsz }\frac{e}{|\br-\br'|}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent has different expressions in reciprocal space, depending on the dimensionality of the system.
Designating by $k = |\bk|$ the length of the vector $\bk$, in \gls{3d}, the momentum resolved Coulomb potential is given by

\begin{equation}
    v_{c}(\bk)=\int\dd^{3}\br\,\frac{e}{4\pi \epsz |\br-\br'|}\ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br}=\frac{e}{ \epsz k^{2}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent while in \bd~it assumes the form

\begin{equation}
    v_{c}(\bk)=\int\dd^{2}\br\,\frac{e}{4\pi \epsz |\br-\br'|}\ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br}=\frac{e}{2 \epsz k}\,.
\end{equation}

We remark that due to the definitions used in this work, the Coulomb potential, unlike in real space, does not have units of energy in reciprocal space.
Nonetheless, the final quantities to compute will always have the right units.

If the system enjoys discrete translation symmetry instead of continuous translation symmetry, when we want to describe solids microscopically, an expression similar to~\eqref{eq:finite_volume_two_point_inverse_Fourier} can still be worked out.
But for that, we need to use the concepts from chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_mechanics_solids}, namely that of \acrlong{bz} and reciprocal lattice vectors.
If the system enjoys discrete translational symmetry, one can prove that $f(\bk,\bk')\neq0$ only if $\bk'=\bk+\bG$, where $\bG$ is a vector of the reciprocal lattice.
Besides that, using the fact that any momentum $\bq$ in the reciprocal lattice can be written as $\bq=\bk+\bG$, where $\bk$ is a vector in the \gls{bz}, we can rewrite the sums over momentum in~\eqref{eq:finite_volume_two_point_inverse_Fourier} as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    f(\br,\br') &= \frac{1}{\vol^2}\sum_{\bk,\bk' \in \BZ} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} f(\bk+\bG,\bk'+\bG')\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot (\br-\br')}\ee^{\ii \bG \vdot \br} \ee^{-\ii \bG' \vdot \br'} = \\
                &= \frac{1}{\vol}\sum_{\bk \in \BZ}\sum_{\bG,\bG'}f(\bk+\bG,\bk+\bG')\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot (\br-\br')}\ee^{\ii \bG \vdot \br} \ee^{-\ii \bG' \vdot \br'},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent if we indentify $f(\bk+\bG,\bk'+\bG')=\vol\delta_{\bk,\bk'}f(\bk+\bG,\bk+\bG')$.
