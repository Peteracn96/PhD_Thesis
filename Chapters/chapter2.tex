\chapter{Classical Electrodynamics of Continuous Media}
\label{ch:classical_electrodynamics}
\todo[inline]{Decide final name of the chapter}

In this chapter we give an overview of concepts from classical electrodynamics that will serve as a basis for the physical phenomena we will study later on, mainly those pertaining to classical effects observable at macroscopic scales in solids.
We give particular emphasis to \bd~materials at the interface between two different media, as \bd~materials have been shown to present enticing physics and peculiar \gls{em} properties.
Even though with a solely classical description of matter we cannot grasp the fine details of \bd~matter, classical electrodynamics provides insightful tools to describe the propagation of light in matter and how these two interact.
We hope that this introduction to basic concepts in electromagnetism will suffice for the reader expecting a thesis as self-contained as possible.
Whenever further details or derivations could break the flow of the text, we refer to standard literature on this subject~\cite{Jackson:electrodynamics,Loureiro_Elementos_de_ECLA,Griffiths_2023}.\\

We start the chapter with the celebrated Maxwell's equations in continuous media, a set of equations governing, among others, the behavior of the \gls{em} field in space and time.
In the same section we present the boundary conditions of the \gls{em} field at the interface between two contiguous media.
Next, we describe how the \gls{em} field in matter relates to the external applied fields through the so-called constitutive relations.
The central quantity will be the dielectric function, a quantity characteristic to each material.
We consider different limits, the static limit and the local limit, in separate case studies, namely the \bd~dielectric and the Drude metal, respectively.
For the curious reader, other examples in different contexts are briefly mentioned.
The first two sections serve as the basis for what is to come in the third and final section of the chapter: the polariton.
To introduce the reader to the concept of polariton, we need first a framework for \gls{em} wave propagation in matter.
We take the opportunity to mention bulk plasmons, even though they are not the main topic of the thesis.
The concept of polariton is finally introduced, along with the derivation of the dispersion relation for these modes at a surface between two different materials, taking into account the surface optical response.
Again, we take the opportunity to mention plasmons, but now coupled to light to form the plasmon--polariton.
We finish the chapter with a discussion on the Fresnel coefficients with the same setup, in the situation where the incident radiation is normal to the planar surface and linearly polarized.
We generalize to the case where the interstitial surface, consisting of a \bd~material, has its own response.
These coefficients will allow us to estimate the capacity of the surface in absorbing, reflecting and transmitting radiation.

\section{Fundamentals of Classical Electrodynamics}
\label{sec:CED_fundaments}

\subsection{Maxwell's equations}
\label{subsec:maxwell_equations}

The four central equations of classical electrodynamics, which govern the dynamics of the \gls{em} field and all \gls{em} phenomena in continuous media, are called the macroscopic Maxwell equations, and in their local form they read~\cite{Jackson:electrodynamics,Loureiro_Elementos_de_ECLA}

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \div \bD \left(\br,t\right) &= \rho_\f\left(\br,t\right), &  &\text{Gauss' law}  \label{subeq:Gauss_law} \\
        \rot \bE \left(\br,t\right) &= -\pdt[\bB\left(\br,t\right)], & &\text{Faraday's law}\ \label{subeq:Faraday_law}\\
         \div \bB \left(\br,t\right) &= 0\,, & &\text{Inexistence of magnetic monopoles} \label{subeq:no_magnetic_monopoles}\\
         \rot \bH\left(\br,t\right) &= \bJ_\f\left(\br,t\right) + \pdt[\bD\left(\br,t\right)]\,. & &\text{Ampère--Maxwell's law} \label{subeq:Ampere_law}
    \end{align}
    \label{eq:Maxwells_equations}
\end{subequations}

\noindent In the previous system of equations, the vector fields $\bE$ and $\bB$ are called the electric, and magnetic induction fields, respectively.
We will simply denote the latter by magnetic field.
These two fields are the fundamental physical quantities in electromagnetism with a physical meaning, unlike the vector fields $\bD$ and $\bH$, called electric displacement and magnetic excitation fields, respectively, that are auxiliary identities with no physical interpretation.
%We will interchangeably use the term magnetic field for both $\bB$ and $\bH$, as in this work the relation between these two vectors is established by a simple proportionality factor.
The fields $\bD$ and $\bH$ are introduced to make implicit in Maxwell's equations the properties of the matter the \gls{em} field penetrates in.
Nonetheless, they are strictly related to the electric and magnetic fields, as we will see.
The last two quantities to define are the free charge (volume) density $\rho_\f$ and the free current (surface) density $\bJ_\f$, that together with the bound charge density and the bound current density form the total charge and current densities inside the material, respectively.\todo{not sure about the phrasing here}
The free charge and current densities are not independent, as they are related through the continuity equation

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:continuity_equation}
    \pdt[\rho_\f] + \div \bJ_\f = 0,
\end{equation}

\noindent encoding the conservation of free electric charge, which can be seen by taking the divergence of Ampère--Maxwell's law.
The free charge and current densities can in turn be split into an external part, which is the one externally applied on the material, and an induced part resulting from the material response to the external perturbation.
The continuity equation is also verified by the bound charge and bound current densities, and consequently, also by its counterpart total quantities.
As said above, the fundamental fields $\bE$ and $\bB$ are connected to the fields $\bD$ and $\bH$, respectively, through a set of two equations called constitutive relations given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:constitutive_relation_electric_field}
    \bD = \epsz \bE + \bP
\end{equation}

\noindent and\footnote{This asymmetry in the constitutive relations is in fact due to a historical mistake, as it was believed that it was actually the vector field $\bH$ that had physical meaning instead of the $\bB$ one.}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:constitutive_relation_magnetic_field}
    \bB = \mu_0 \qty(\bH + \bM)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\bP$ is the bound volume polarization density and $\bM$ the bound volume magnetization density, and the parameters $\epsz$ and $\mu_0$ are the vacuum electric permittivity and the vacuum magnetic permeability, respectively
Within the scope of this work, we study only materials with no magnetic response at optical frequencies nor with permanent magnetization, so that $\bM = \bzero$ and $\bB \qty(\br,t)  = \mu_0 \bH \qty(\br,t)$ will always hold.

With all these equations at hand, one can, in principle, solve any problem in electrodynamics in a continuous medium.
One would just need to know how in fact the material properties determine the polarization density, and also the magnetization density in case the material is magnetic.
However, before we dwell on material properties, we will see in the next section how the global form of Maxwell's equations can give insight into solving problems in electrodynamics where the system under study involves more than one material and their interfaces.
From a technical point of view, Maxwell's equations in their local form are not useful in these cases where one has material discontinuities, as taking spatial derivatives is not possible (or introduces unwished infinities).
In that situation, one then needs to set proper boundary conditions to find the \gls{em} field at all points in space and at all time instants.

\subsection{Boundary conditions at an interface}
\label{subsec:boundary_conditions}

In this section we consider an infinite planar interface between two different media, say medium 1 and medium 2.
This separation in space may introduce discontinuities in the vector fields $\bE$, $\bD$, $\bB$ and $\bH$, which can be determined by applying pill-box arguments to Maxwell's equations in their integral form~\cite{Jackson:electrodynamics,Griffiths_2023}.
By doing so, we can relate the fields $\bE_i,\bD_i,\bB_i$ and $\bH_i$ on each side $i=1,2$ of the interface by the following boundary conditions:

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:boundary_conditions_tangential}
    \begin{align}
        \bn_{12} \times (\bE_2 - \bE_1) &= \bzero \label{eq:boundary_condition_E} \qq{and} \\
        \bn_{12} \times (\bH_2 - \bH_1) &= \bj_s, \label{eq:boundary_condition_H}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}


\noindent for the tangential components of the electric and magnetic excitation fields, respectively, where $\bj_s$ is a sheet current density;

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:boundary_conditions_perpendicular}
    \begin{align}
        \bn_{12} \vdot (\bD_2 - \bD_1) &= \sigma_s \label{eq:boundary_condition_D} \qq{and}\\
        \bn_{12} \vdot (\bB_2 - \bB_1) &= 0, \label{eq:boundary_condition_B}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}


\noindent for the normal components of the electric displacement and magnetic fields, respectively, where $\sigma_s$ is a surface charge density, and $\bn_{12}$ is the versor normal to the interface pointing from medium 1 to medium 2.
The quantities $\sigma_s$ and $\bj_s$ account for any charge flow that might exist at the interface.
If it consists simply of a surface separating two different media, then in principle $\sigma_s = 0$ and $\bj_s = \bzero$.
However, if on the interface we have a material of arbitrary small thickness, or if the interface is itself the existing material separating two semi-infinite regions, then in general we have to account for the physics of its optical response and cannot ignore $\sigma_s$ nor $\bj_s$.
If surface charge and current densities are not null, then naturally they satisfy the continuity equation $\div \bj_s + \partial_t \sigma_s = 0$.
A striking example is that of graphene, either by itself or at a boundary between a dielectric and a metal~\cite{GoncalvesPeres:2016}.
In this work, we focus on dielectric \bd~materials, also termed insulators, and on their optical properties.
These can be quantified by the dielectric function, or as we shall explain in the next section, by the conductivity in an equivalent way.

\section{Material Properties: Classical Picture}
\label{sec:dielectric_function_classical}

The material properties that up until now we have been hiding in the fields $\bD$ and $\bP$, are encoded in the constitutive relation above between the total electric field $\bE$ and the response of the material to it quantified by $\bP$.
This polarization density results from the charges inside the material rearranging themselves to generate new local bound dipoles.
This relation is in the most general case functional and non-linear.
Notwithstanding this, herein we restrict ourselves to the linear response regime, where the polarization density $\bP$ depends linearly on the electric field $\bE$.
We will see how in this section.

\subsection{Dielectric function and conductivity}
\label{subsec:constitutive_relations_linear_response}

In the linear relation between the total electric field and the polarization density, the proportionality constant is given in terms of the electric susceptibility $\chi_e$ through

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:polarization_density}
    \bP = \epsz \rttensor{\chi}_{e} \bE ,
\end{equation}

\noindent assuming that the material can be anisotropic, such that the susceptibility is in fact a second-rank tensor (and presuming also that there is no permanent polarization $\bP_0$ contributing to $\bP$).
We admit that there is no magnetic response, so we can overlook the other constitutive relation for the magnetic field\footnote{Otherwise, we would have to account for the magnetic susceptibility $\chi_\mathrm{m}$}.
If the material is isotropic, then $\rttensor{\chi}_{e} = \chi_{e} \mathds{1}$, with $\mathds{1}$ being the identity matrix.
Then, for the electric displacement field within linear response approximation and for an anisotropic material we can write

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:displacement_field}
    \bD = \epsz \bE + \bP = \epsz \qty(\mathds{1} + \rttensor{\chi}_{e}) \bE \equiv \epsz \rttensor{\vareps} \bE\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we defined the relative permittivity tensor $\rttensor{\vareps} = \mathds{1} + \rttensor{\chi}_{e}$.
The proportionality constant $\rttensor{\vareps}$ is a characteristic quantity of the material, and it is in general a tensorial response function.
This means that the field at the point $\br$ and at the time $t$  depends on all the other points $\br'$ in space and all the other previous instants of time $t'<t$.
%This means that the field at the point $\br$ depends on all the other points $\br'$ in space, and at the time $t$ depends on all the other times $t'$.
One can readily see that the previous constitutive relation hides an intricate dependence of the displacement field on the electric field.
The operation between the permittivity (we omit from now onwards the word relative) tensor and the electric field is the convolution operation in both space and time coordinates

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:displacement_field_constitutive_relation}
    \bD\qty(\br,t) = \int_{\mathds{R}^3} \dr' \int_{-\infty}^t \dd{t'} \rttensor{\vareps}\qty(\br, \br'; t , t') \bE\qty(\br',t')\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the permittivity is now a (tensorial) response function, i.e., a two-point function in real space and a two-point function in time.
Without knowing any details about the permittivity, we can promptly impose causality which implies that $\vareps\qty(\br, \br'; t , t') = 0$ if $t' > t$ (let us omit the tensor nature of the permittivity for now).
The integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:displacement_field_constitutive_relation} above could then be taken up to $+\infty$ (this is useful to take the Fourier transform in time, as we shall do just now).
Furthermore, if the system enjoys \textit{continuous} time translational invariance\footnote{This assumption is only valid if we treat the external probe or driving field as a perturbation to the system, so small that the response is that of a material in (dynamic) equilibrium, and its intrinsic properties are time-independent. If the probe or field is strong enough, then it can drive the system to a non-equilibrium state, and in that case we have to account for the full dynamics of the composed system material+perturbation. Even if the external field is time periodic and the system still enjoys time translation invariance, albeit discrete, we must separate the two time dependences $t$ and $t'$. As stated above we work solely within a perturbative regime, henceforth this is not a big concern for us.}, then the permittivity depends only on the time difference $t - t'$.
This property is extremely useful, as per the convolution theorem, we can perform a Fourier transform in a single variable of time $t-t'$, so that the permittivity and the electric field operate in the frequency domain through a mere multiplication at a single frequency $\omega$:

\begin{equation}
    \bD\qty(\br,\omega) = \epsz \int \dr' \vareps\qty(\br, \br'; \omega) \bE\qty(\br',\omega)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent If we now further assume that the material is homogeneous, which is a reasonable approximation at the macroscopic scale, then the system enjoys \textit{continuous} spatial translational invariance, and the permittivity becomes a function solely of the difference $\br-\br'$.
One can show that if this is true, then we can Fourier transform the permittivity from real to reciprocal space keeping only one wavevector (or momentum, in the language of solid state physics) variable $\bk$, and through direct application of the convolution theorem once more, we can write

\begin{equation}
    \bD\qty(\bk,\omega) = \epsz \vareps\qty(\bk, \omega) \bE\qty(\bk,\omega)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which simplifies immensely the relation between the displacement field and the electric field.

The dielectric function, a central quantity in this text, in reciprocal space $\vareps\qty(\bk,\omega)$ is in general complex valued and a very complicated function of $\bk$ and $\omega$, and determining the full dependence on both momentum and frequency is a herculean task.
With the tools provided by classical electrodynamics, one can only hope to work with ad-hoc models for the dielectric function, usually using material parameters obtained experimentally.
We provide a few case examples of models commonly found in the literature in the next sections.

Before we proceed, it is worthy to remind the reader that in electromagnetism we can consider charge currents as a result from an application of an external electric field.
The relation between the electric field and the induced current is quantified by the famous (local and generalized) Ohm's law, that when assuming time and space translational invariance\footnote{Until the time when it will become relevant, we will not explicitly say that the invariance in space is continuous. In the context of solid state physics, translational invariance is discrete instead.}, and isotropy as well, reads

\begin{equation}
    \bJ_\ind\qty(\br,t) = \int_{\mathds{R}^3} \dr \int_{-\infty}^t \dd{t'} \sigma\qty(\br - \br'; t - t') \bE\qty(\br',t')\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sigma$ is the conductivity of the material (that can assume a tensorial form, just like the permittivity), and accounts for the excitation of free charges due to the applied electric field.
It is a non-null conductivity that leads to losses by Joule effect (apart from the particular case of superconductivity, where a system conducts electricity without any losses).
We introduce this quantity here, as it can be useful in electrodynamics to account simultaneously for a material's dielectric response and its conductivity in a single effective dielectric function.
Let us then rewrite Maxwell's equations~\eqref{eq:Maxwells_equations} in reciprocal space and frequency domain by Fourier transforming them (or equivalently, assuming a dependence of the form $\bF(\br,t) = \bF_0 \ei[-]{(\omega t - \bk \vdot \br)}$ for all the fields)

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \ii \bk \vdot \bD(\bk,\omega) &= \rho_\f(\bk,\omega) ,\label{subeq:Gauss_law_reciprocal} \\
        \ii \bk \times \bE(\bk,\omega) &= -\ii \omega \bB(\bk,\omega), \label{subeq:Faradays_law_reciprocal}\\
        \ii \bk \vdot \bB(\bk,\omega) &= 0 ,\label{subeq:no_magnetic_monopoles_reciprocal}\\
        \ii \bk \times \bH(\bk,\omega) &= \bJ_\f(\bk,\omega) - \ii \omega \epsz \vareps (\bk,\omega) \bE(\bk,\omega)\,, \label{subeq:Amperes_law_reciprocal}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent and focus on Ampère--Maxwell's law alongside Ohm's law written in its Fourier transformed version as $\bJ_\ind(\bk,\omega) = \sigma(\bk,\omega) \bE(\bk,\omega)$, we can rewrite the last equation as

\begin{align*}
    \label{subeq:Amperes_law_reciprocal_equivalent}
    \ii \bk \times \bH(\bk,\omega) &= \bJ_\ext(\bk,\omega) + \sigma(\bk,\omega) \bE(\bk,\omega) - \ii \omega \epsz \vareps (\bk,\omega) \bE(\bk,\omega) \\
    &=\bJ_\ext(\bk,\omega) - \ii \omega \epsz \qty[\vareps (\bk,\omega) +\ii \frac{\sigma(\bk,\omega)}{\omega \epsz}] \bE(\bk,\omega) \equiv \\
    &\equiv \bJ_\ext(\bk,\omega)  -\ii \omega \epsz \vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega) \bE(\bk,\omega)\,,
\end{align*}


\noindent where through the last equality we defined the equivalent permittivity\footnote{The function $\vareps (\bk,\omega)$ on the \gls{rhs}~has quite often in the literature a subscript writing $\vareps_\infty(\bk,\omega)$, standing for the contribution coming from the ionic background together with bound polarization charges. In this work we consider typically $\vareps_\infty(\bk,\omega) = 1$.}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:equivalent_permittivity}
    \vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega) = \vareps (\bk,\omega) + \ii \frac{\sigma(\bk,\omega)}{\omega \epsz} \,.
\end{equation}


\noindent This suggests redefining the total electric displacement field as $\bD \to \bD_\tot  = \bD +~\ii \omega^{-1} \bJ_\ind$.
In this way, equation~\eqref{subeq:Gauss_law_reciprocal} can be rewritten in terms of only the external charge density, $ \ii \bk \vdot \bD_\tot(\bk,\omega) = \rho_\ext(\bk,\omega)$.

With only the external charge and current densities appearing explicitly in Maxwell's equations and a total displacement field incorporating the induced current, we can define a total electric field as $\bE_\tot = \bD_\tot/\epsz$ and interpret the dielectric function as the ratio between the external field and the total one: $\vareps(\bk,\omega) = |\bE_\ext(\bk,\omega)|/|\bE_\tot(\bk,\omega)|$.
This interpretation is the cornerstone of the theory around the dielectric function.
It is actually introduced like this in introductory electromagnetism courses, appearing usually as a single constant relative permittivity denoted by $\vareps_r$ in the denominator in Coulomb's law for the electrostatic force.
In bulk materials, it is typically sufficient to speak of the relative permittivity as a constant of the material.
Unfortunately (or the opposite), it is in general a very complicated function of space and time, although working in reciprocal space seems to ease things.
This is a striking feature of \bd~materials, where the dependence on $\bk$ and $\omega$ cannot be ignored, even encoding rich physics such as spatial dispersion (nontrivial $\bk$ dependence), time dispersion (nontrivial $\omega$ dependence), screening in crystalline solids (main subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:Screening}) and their nontrivial absorption spectra\todo{more references no?}, plasmons in metals and \bd~materials such as graphene~\cite{GoncalvesPeres:2016,Novoselov_Geim_2004}, the effect called Landau damping in the context of kinetic theory in plasma physics~\cite{Chen_Plasma_Physics}, among others.

To familiarize a reader that has not extensive experience on the dielectric function, we provide in the next subsections two key examples of models for the dielectric function, one in the static limit $\omega \to 0$, and the other one in the local limit $\bk \to \bzero$.
Isolating these two limits and considering the dependence on $\bk$ and $\omega$ separately is enough to realize how intricate this object can be.

\subsubsection{Static limit in a 2D material}
\label{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit}

In this section we take a \bd~material as an example, as this thesis is concerned only with such materials, and we keep our focus for now on the static and long-wavelength limits of the dielectric function.
The main goal is to derive a minimal model that somehow captures the effect of electrostatic screening felt by test point-like charges inside the material.
We want then to find a function $\vareps(\bk)$ for which the total electrostatic potential between two test charges is $\phi(\bk) = v_c(\bk)/\vareps(\bk)$, where $v_c(\bk)$ is the Coulomb potential in reciprocal space that the two charges would feel remaining static in vacuum.
We say that $\phi$ is the screened Coulomb potential and $v_c$ is the bare Coulomb potential.
We will present a derivation adapted from a work by P.~Cudazzo et al.~\cite{Cudazzo_2011}.

In real space, the Coulomb potential is given by

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Coulomb_potential_real_space}
    v_c(\br) = \frac{e}{4\pi \epsz \qty|\br\,|}\,,
\end{equation}


\noindent with $e$ being the elementary charge (electron charge in absolute value)\footnote{In this text we avoid using Gaussian units, adopting the SI system instead. The Coulomb potential is to be understood in electron-Volt (eV), the reason why we have $e$ in the numerator instead of $e^2$. We call it potential even though it has units of energy. Throughout this text potential and energy are interchangeable terms when speaking of the Coulomb interaction.}.
To find the screened potential felt by a test charge $e$ in the material, we can make use of Maxwell's equations that we already introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:maxwell_equations}, in particular Gauss' law~\eqref{subeq:Gauss_law} in the form

\begin{equation}
    \div \bD(\br) = \rho_\ext(\br)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we dropped the subscript \enquote{$\tot$} for the displacement field, and the external charge density is that of a single point-like elementary charge, that without loss of generality can be placed at the origin.
Since the charge has no dimensions, we can simply write~$\rho_\ext\left(\br\right)=~e\delta\left(\br\right)$, and for the displacement field we write $\boldsymbol{D}\left(\br\right)=\epsz\boldsymbol{E}\left(\br\right)+\boldsymbol{P}^\BD\left(\br\right)$, where $\boldsymbol{P}^\BD$ is a \bd~polarization vector.
Assuming that the electric susceptibility is local (i.e., $\chi_{e}(\br,\br') \propto \delta(\br - \br')$) and the \bd~material is isotropic, the polarizability reads $\boldsymbol{P}^\BD\left(\br\right)=\epsz\chi_{e}^\BD\boldsymbol{E_{||}}\left(\br\right)|_{z=0}$,
that is, the polarization is proportional to the in-plane component of the total electric field computed along the plane of the material with Cartesian equation $z=0$, and $\chi_{e}^\BD$ is a \bd~susceptibility.
Substituting the electric field by the negative gradient of the potential in Gauss' law and the charge density by its Dirac delta like expression, we obtain the following Poisson equation

\begin{equation}
    \nabla\vdot\left[\nabla\phi\left(\br\right)+\chi_{e}^\BD\nabla_{||}\phi\left(\br_{||},z=0\right)\right]=-\frac{e}{\epsz}\delta\left(\br\right)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the in-plane electric field contributing to the polarizability is given in terms of an in-plane gradient operation, $\nabla_{||}$, acting on the electrostatic potential computed at $z=0$ and at any position vector $\br_{||}$ on the plane.
Within the approximation of zero thickness, we can work with an effective \bd~electrostatic potential in momentum space $\phi^\BD\left(\bq\right)$, defined by the total electrostatic potential computed at the plane of the material, that is, $\phi^\BD\left(\bq\right)\equiv\phi\left(\bq,z=0\right)$.
To find an expression for such a potential, one Fourier transforms the previous equation, keeping the variables for in-plane and off-plane momentum, $q$ and $\kappa$ respectively, separate as:

\begin{equation}
    \left(q^2+\kappa^2\right)\phi\left(\bq,\kappa\right)+\chi_{e}^\BD q^2\phi^\BD\left(\bq\right)=\frac{e}{\epsz}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Upon solving the equation above for $\phi\left(\bq,\kappa\right)$, doing the inverse Fourier transform in the $\kappa$ variable back to the variable $z$ and setting $z=0$, we find an equation for the quantity $\phi^\BD\left(\bq\right)$, that after being isolated reads

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:RK_potential_momentum}
    \phi^\BD\left(\bq\right) = \frac{e}{2\epsz q\left(1+r_0 q\right)},\,\qq{defining}\,r_0 \equiv \epsz\chi_{e}^\BD\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We call the parameter $r_0$ the screening length parameter, or simply screening parameter, and its value depends only on the material.
This formula contains the expression for the \bd~Fourier transform of the bare Coulomb potential given by\footnote{We remark that this expression does not have units of energy, which can be traced back to the used definition of Fourier transform in this chapter. That can be fixed by simply dividing by the area of the total system. It poses not a problem, as long as the final calculated quantity of interest has the right dimensions.}\todo{have to define somewhere the Fourier transforms}

\begin{equation}
    v_{c}(q)=\frac{e}{2\epsz q}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent but corrected with a factor of $1+r_0 q$.
We can then define an effective momentum-resolved dielectric function, $\varepsilon_\RK\left(q\right) \equiv 1+r_0 q$, called Rytova--Keldysh dielectric function, which accounts for screening in \bd~materials.
Having previously assumed that the material is isotropic, then it depends only on the magnitude of the in-plane momentum $q = |\bq|$.
It actually coincides with the static macroscopic dielectric function in \bd~materials in the high-wavelength (small $q$) limit.
It carries the usual interpretation of being the ratio between the external potential $\phi^\ext\left(\br\right)=\phi_0^\ext\left(\bq\right) \ei{\bq \vdot \br}$ and the total (or screened) potential $\phi^\tot\left(\br\right)=\phi_0^\tot\text{\ensuremath{\left(\bq\right)}}\ei{\bq \vdot \br}$, which has a contribution from the external potential and another one from the potential induced by the external perturbation.
We actually introduced this interpretation in Section~\ref{subsec:constitutive_relations_linear_response} in terms of fields instead of potentials, but the idea is the same.
The resulting effective Coulomb potential is called Rytova--Keldysh potential~\cite{Rytova_1967}.

To find the real space resolved Rytova--Keldysh potential, we do the \bd~inverse Fourier transform of~\eqref{eq:RK_potential_momentum} to get

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Rytova--Keldysh_potential_real_space}
    V_\RK (r)=\frac{e}{4\pi\epsz}\frac{\pi}{2r_0}\left[\Hz\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right)-Y_0\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right)\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where $r$ is a purely in-plane distance, $\Hz$ is the Struve special function of order zero~\cite{abramowitz_stegun}, and $Y_0$ is the Bessel special function of the second kind of order zero.
To get a feeling for how this potential behaves in real space compared to the bare Coulomb potential, we can make a plot of both potentials as a function of $r$.

In Figure~\ref{fig:Coulomb_and_RK_potential}, we compare  the two potentials as a function of the distance $r$, the bare Coulomb potential~$v_c(r)$ and the Rytova--Keldysh potential $V_\RK(r)$ as per~\eqref{eq:Rytova--Keldysh_potential_real_space}.
For large enough distances, the Coulomb potential and the Rytova--Keldysh potential become closer and closer, meaning that for very large distances the electrostatic potential is not screened at all, and the attractive or repulsive behavior of two test charges is just the same as in vacuum.
However, for very small distances we see that the potentials have different degrees of divergence, with the divergence of the Rytova--Keldysh potential being logarithmic (to see it just take the small $r$ limit in expression~\eqref{eq:Rytova--Keldysh_potential_real_space}).

The Rytova--Keldysh potential is a model that captures well enough the electrostatic interaction in \bd~materials, in particular insulating and semiconducting ones.
Whenever it comes to the interaction between charges in these materials, this is the model interaction one should use to make accurate predictions.
How the screening parameter $r_0$ can be estimated will only be left for later, when the necessary tools for doing so are provided.
For now, this picture is just a classical model with an estimated parameter.
Nonetheless, it can already capture the nontrivial dependence of the dielectric function on the wavevector.
We consider next the opposite limit, where the dielectric function depends only on the frequency, but not on the wavevector.

\subsubsection{Local limit}
\label{subsubsec:dielectric_function_local_limit}

In this section we provide another model for the dielectric function where we only account for the dependence on the frequency and take the long-wavelength limit $\bk \to \bzero$.
This approximation is called the \gls{lra}, which consists in admitting that the response function of the material, the permittivity or conductivity, is short-ranged, so that the induced field at a certain point in space depends only on the external field at that same location.
Mathematically, we can write correspondingly for the permittivity and conductivity $\vareps (\br - \br',\omega) = \delta (\br - \br') \vareps^{\mathrm{LRA}} (\omega)$ and $\sigma (\br - \br',\omega) = \delta (\br - \br') \sigma^{\mathrm{LRA}} (\omega)$.
In this approximation, the displacement field is simply given by

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:displacement_field_LRA}
    \bD(\br,\omega) = \epsz \vareps^{\mathrm{LRA}} (\omega) \bE(\br,\omega)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent that together with Ohm's law

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Ohms_law_LRA}
    \bJ_\ind(\br,\omega) = \sigma^{\mathrm{LRA}} (\omega) \bE(\br,\omega)\,
\end{equation}

\noindent form the necessary constitutive relations.

Ignoring non-local effects is valid if the wavelength of the optical excitation is much larger than the characteristic length scales of the material.
One may speak of the typical distance between atoms in a solid, or the Fermi wavelength (the inverse of the Fermi wavevector), which are quite small, usually in the order of the Ångstrom.
In the context of nanophotonics and polaritonics, the typical length scale is of the order of the nanometer, a bit close to the frontier separating classical physics from quantum physics.
Nonetheless this approximation has revealed to be quite accurate in many contexts~\cite{GoncalvesPeres:2016,maier_plasmonics_2007,Hohenester_2020}.

Just for illustrative purposes, we give a typical example of a model for the dielectric function, commonly used to explain and study the optical properties of metals.
It is called the (AC) Drude model~\cite{Drude_1900}, often introduced in the form of a conductivity that reads

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Drude_conductivity}
    \sigma(\omega) = \frac{\wp^2 \epsz}{\gamma - \ii \omega}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\wp$ is the plasma frequency\footnote{The definition of plasma frequency is $\wp = \sqrt{e^2 n_{e}/\epsz m}$, where $n_{e}$ is the volume density of electrons in the material or plasma, and $m$ is the mass of the free electrons.}, a parameter related to the natural frequency of free electron oscillations in a plasma, and $\gamma$ is a relaxation or scattering rate, with $\tau = 1/\gamma$ giving the timescale of consecutive collisions between the electrons and the ions in the lattice, being of the order of $10^{-14}$\unit{\second} in typical metals.
Together with the interrelation between the conductivity and the permittivity~\eqref{eq:equivalent_permittivity}, we can write the equivalent Drude dielectric function as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Drude_dielectric_function}
    \vareps(\omega) = 1 - \frac{\wp^2}{\omega^2 + \ii \gamma \omega}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we omitted the subscript \enquote{$\text{eq}$} on the \gls{lhs}, and used $\vareps(\omega) = 1$ for the bound polarization contribution to the dielectric function.
This last statement is called the jellium approximation.
We skip here the derivation of this model and refer the curious reader to literature where one can find it.
For instance, possible texts are Refs~\cite{GoncalvesPeres:2016,Simon_Oxford_2013,Ashcroft_Mermin}.

Even though we skip its derivation, we can say that this model is constructed solely from classical considerations and introduces the parameter $\tau$ in a ad-hoc way, somehow related to fine details of the material at the microscopic scale we cannot assess (yet).
Although this model is widely used in the literature to describe simple metals (alkali ones, for instance) at low enough frequencies, it is bad in describing the optical properties of dielectric materials that have characteristic resonant frequencies, or phenomena associated with resonant frequencies that metals might display at higher frequencies.

To describe the electrodynamics of non-metallic systems, we can use models based on Lorentzian oscillators.
Once more we skip the derivation and refer the reader to the literature, for instance, Ref.~\cite{Jackson:electrodynamics}.
The Lorentz model, based on the classical harmonic oscillator with a damping parameter, for the dielectric function reads

\begin{equation}
    \vareps (\omega) = 1 + \sum_{j} \frac{f_j}{\omega_j^2 - \omega^2 - \ii \Gamma_j \omega}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the sum is over all the oscillators, $f_j$ is the oscillation strength, $\omega_j$ the resonant frequency and $\Gamma_j$ the damping parameter\footnote{The way we wrote it here, it hides parameters of the material that are common to all oscillators in the oscillator strength. For briefness, we keep the model as simple as possible.}.
Although we do not work with this model in this thesis, it is useful to motivate the dynamical dielectric function (or more precisely, conductivity) we will find later on.
Instead of having a quadratic dependence on the frequency, we will find a linear dependence on the frequency as

\begin{equation}
    \sigma(\omega) = -\ii \sigma_0 \sum_j \qty[ \frac{f_j}{\omega_j -  \omega - \ii \Gamma_j} + \frac{f^*_j}{\omega_j + \omega + \ii \Gamma_j}]\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sigma_0$ is a characteristic conductivity, and once more $f_j$ and $\Gamma_j$ are oscillation strengths and damping rates, respectively.
At this moment, we have to be satisfied with the fact that these parameters are only adjustable parameters that can be obtained from fitting to experimental data.
We can say, though, that these parameters hide very rich physics to be unveiled in the next chapters, just like in the case of Section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit}, where we introduced the adhoc parameter $r_0$.
We beg then for the reader's patience, as further clarifications will come in the next chapters.

Finding a simple enough analytical expression for the dielectric function that encompasses both non-local and dynamical effects is a daunting task, but it can still be done for a couple of cases.
One striking example is the Lindhard function, the dielectric function for the \gls{3d} homogeneous noninteracting electron gas, named after the Danish physicist Jens Lindhard, who derived the function in 1954~\cite{Lindhard_1954}.
For the reader more inclined to hydrodynamical and kinetic theories of \bd~materials and plasmas, we refer to Refs.~\cite{Chen_Plasma_Physics,Cosme_2025}, where one can find mesoscopic approaches to the dielectric and plasmonic properties of Dirac materials, for which analytical or semi-analytical expressions for the dielectric function can be found.
Another thorough text on plasmonic properties of Dirac materials that is worth mentioning is the one by P. A. D. Gon\c{c}alves and N. M. R. Peres, Ref.~\cite{GoncalvesPeres:2016}.
Another honorable mention is the hydrodynamic Drude model~\cite{Bloch_1933,Andre_Goncalves_2020,}, a non-local extension of the Drude model from the hydrodynamic equations of motion of the electron gas, seen as a charged fluid moving in the ionic positive background of a metal.

Using only concepts introduced in this chapter, in the next section we turn to a key topic within the realm of electrodynamics that has been gaining traction in the scientific community, pertaining to the interaction of light with matter, still through the lens of classical physics.
We shall present a light--matter hybrid, the quasi-particle called polariton.

\section{Fundamentals of Polaritonics}
\label{sec:polaritonics_fundamentals}

In this part of the chapter, we dwell on polaritons: a polariton is a light--matter hybrid, resulting from the coupling of a dipolar matter excitation occurring in the plane that is an interface between two media, with the surrounding \gls{em} field.
The result is a transversal \gls{em} mode that propagates in-plane but is ultra-confined along the off-plane direction.
These modes can be predicted by simply trying a specific \textit{ansatz} solution on Maxwell's equations~\eqref{eq:Maxwells_equations} describing the situation above, complemented by the boundary conditions dictated by~\eqref{eq:boundary_conditions_tangential} and~\eqref{eq:boundary_conditions_perpendicular}, and the constitutive relations~\eqref{eq:constitutive_relation_electric_field} and \eqref{eq:constitutive_relation_magnetic_field} to close the problem.
Before doing so, we will warm up by deriving the wave equation for the \gls{em} field in matter and introduce useful concepts for later on.


\subsection{Electromagnetic wave propagation in matter}
\label{subsec:waves_in_matter}

It is well known that light propagates in vacuum at a very specific speed $c=1/\sqrt{\epsz \mu_0}$, like a wave, where the electric field and the magnetic field oscillate in phase at a given frequency, $\omega$, and with a given wavevector $\bk$ giving the direction of propagation.
The electric field $\bE$, the magnetic field $\bB$ and the wavevector $\bk$ form a direct trihedron, that is, they verify orthogonality conditions akin to the three Cartesian axes.
These transverse oscillations can be predicted by Maxwell's equations~\eqref{eq:Maxwells_equations} upon taking the cross product between $\bk$ and Faraday's law~\eqref{subeq:Faradays_law_reciprocal} from the left, and doing the same to Ampère--Maxwell's law~\eqref{subeq:Amperes_law_reciprocal} to write

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:wave_equation}
    \begin{align}
        \bk \times \qty[\bk \times \bE(\bk,\omega)] &= - \omega \bk \times \bB(\bk,\omega)\,, \label{subeq:Faradays_law_crossed}\\
        \bk \times \qty[\bk \times \bB(\bk,\omega)] &= -\ii \sigma(\bk,\omega) \bk \times \bE(\bk,\omega) - \omega \epsz \mu_0 \vareps(\bk,\omega) \bk \times \bE(\bk,\omega)\,.\label{subeq:Amperes_law_crossed}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent By combining these two equations and using the vector identity of the double cross product $\mathbf{a} \times [\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{c}] = (\mathbf{a} \vdot \mathbf{c}) \mathbf{b} - (\mathbf{a} \vdot \mathbf{b}) \mathbf{c}$, we arrive at

\begin{equation}
    k^2 \bF(\bk,\omega) - \bk \big(\bk \vdot \bF(\bk,\omega)\big) = -\omega^2 \epsz \mu_0 \vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega) \bF(\bk,\omega) \label{subeq:wave_equation_F}\,,\\
\end{equation}

\noindent for both fields $\bF = \bE, \bB$, and we used the definition for the equivalent permittivity~\eqref{eq:equivalent_permittivity}, while assuming that there are no external currents influencing the dynamics of the material ($\bJ_\ext = \bzero$).
Now, if we assume that the oscillation of the \gls{em} field is transversal, then $\bk \vdot \bE = \bk \vdot \bB = 0$, and the equations above simplify to

\begin{equation}
    \qty[k^2 - \vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega) \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}] \bF (\bk,\omega) = \bzero\,,\, \bF \in \{\bE, \bB\}.
\end{equation}

\noindent The attentive reader might have recognized the previous equation as the wave equation but written in the $(\bk,\omega)$ domain.
It presents a non-trivial solution $\bF \neq \bzero$ if the quantity in brackets is zero, therefore $\bk$ and $\omega$ have to verify

\begin{equation}
    k^2 = \vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega) \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\,.
\end{equation}

Relationships of the previous kind, relating the frequency of the wave with the wavevector, are called dispersion relations.
In this case, the wave pertains to light, so it is an optical dispersion relation.
We will face another type of dispersion relation in the next chapter.
We further note that the wavenumber $k$ and wavevector $\bk$ are in general complex, as well the permittivity $\vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega)$.
Writing the wavenumber in terms of its real and imaginary parts as $k = k' + \ii \kappa$, when looking at modes oscillating in space as $\exp (\ii k x)$, then we have $\exp (\ii k x) = \exp (\ii k' x) \exp (- \kappa x)$.
Therefore, the inverse of the imaginary part of the wavenumber $1/\kappa$ has the interpretation of a penetration length scale of the wave into the medium.
The parameter $\kappa$ is then called the extinction coefficient, and $1/\kappa$ the skin depth\footnote{We discard off course the unphysical $\kappa<0$ solutions.}.

By identifying the phase velocity of the wave as $v = \omega/k$, then we have a definition for the quantity $c/v = \sqrt{\vareps_{\text{eq}} (\bk,\omega)} \equiv n$, the refractive index of the medium.
It is in this context that we speak of dispersion, both spatial or temporal, coming from the dependence of the refractive index in $\bk$ or $\omega$, respectively.
In dispersive media, if the wave is a superposition of harmonics, then it actually makes no sense of speaking in the velocity of the wave, as there are two distinct velocities.
There is the phase velocity $v_p = \omega/k$ and the group velocity $v_g = \partial \omega/\partial k$\footnote{We could have been more rigorous, since these quantities are real, but the refractive index and the wavenumber are complex in general.}.
The phase velocity is the speed with which the phase of the wave-front propagates.
The group velocity is the speed with which the wave envelope travels, and can be smaller or greater than the phase velocity, in a normal or anomalous dispersive medium, respectively.
To summarize, we can say that in a material the dispersion of propagating \gls{em} waves can be in general different from that of free light in vacuum, $\omega = k c$.

Looking back at equation~\eqref{subeq:wave_equation_F}, and looking now for longitudinal oscillations with $\bk || \bE$ for which $\bk \vdot \bE \neq 0$, then we find instead

\begin{equation}
    \vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega) \frac{\omega^2}{c^2} \bF(\bk,\omega) = \bzero\,, \bF \in \{\bE, \bB\}.
\end{equation}

\noindent This last equation admits a non-trivial solution $\bF \neq 0$ only for\footnote{We remind the reader that we have been considering materials with no magnetic susceptibility, such that the relative permeability is identically one in the entire $(\bk,\omega)$ domain.}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:plasmon_condition}
    \vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega) = 0\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We will immediately interpret the previous condition.
Omitting the subscript \enquote{eq}, let us rewrite the constitutive relation for the electric displacement field $\bD = \epsz \bE + \bP = \epsz \vareps \bE = \bzero$, the last equality by virtue of~\eqref{eq:plasmon_condition}.
This means for the electric field $\bE = -\bP/\epsz$, that is, the electric field in the material stems only from the polarization field---the only way for $\bE$ to admit oscillating modes when $\bD$ is zero, is by having $\vareps$ equal to zero too.
These modes are freestanding and derive from the collective oscillation of free electrons in the material, typically a metal.
They are called plasmonic modes, or plasmons, and were first introduced by David Pines and David Bohm in 1952~\cite{Pines_Bohm_1952} and underpin the entire field of plasmonics~\cite{Cosme_2025,GoncalvesPeres:2016,Andre_Goncalves_2020,maier_plasmonics_2007,Ellie_2024}.

The relation~\eqref{eq:plasmon_condition} can be seen as the dispersion relation of the plasmon, that in general is implicitly defined and can only be solved numerically to get $\omega = \omega(\bk)$ explicitly.
However, if the expression of the permittivity is simple enough, then we can find an analytical expression for the dispersion relation of transverse and longitudinal modes.
In the example of a bulk, local and Drude metal without the background contribution, with $\vareps_\infty = 1$, the permittivity is given by $\vareps(\omega) = 1 -\wp^2/\omega^2$, so that light and plasmon modes have by dispersion relations $\omega^2 = \wp^2 + k^2 c^2$ and $\omega = \wp$, respectively.

The plasmon, being a dipolar excitation of the material, can couple to the transverse \gls{em} mode, for instance, along the interface separating a dielectric and a metal, to generate a polariton.
Then we would be talking of the so-called \gls{spp}.
We derive the dispersion relation of the \gls{spp} in the next section, but considering instead the more general case where the interface itself has its own optical response, quantified through an optical conductivity $\sigma(\omega)$, in the \lra~limit.
Then, the dispersion relation of the \gls{spp} can be obtained \textit{a posteriori} by setting $\sigma = 0$.

\subsection{Dispersion relation of polaritonic modes}
\label{subsec:polaritonics_dispersion_relation}

In this section we derive the dispersion relation of surface polaritons, in the case where we have a responsive \bd~material, separating two different media with permittivities $\vareps_1$ and $\vareps_2$, possibly frequency dependent.
The situation is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:cladded_monolayer}, where the substrate, in gray, is material 2, with permittivity $\vareps_2$, while the material on top is material 1, with permittivity $\vareps_1$.
At the interface there is a \bd~material represented in light blue, with its own optical response that we quantify, by convenience, through its conductivity $\sigma(\omega)$, $\omega$ being the frequency of the polariton.
The polariton propagates in the $x$ direction with wavevector $\bq = q \hat{\mathbf{x}}$ that is confined on the $xy$ plane, decaying exponentially in the $z$ direction away from it.

The starting point is assuming a plane wave dependence on $x$ and $t$ for the electric field $\bE$ and the magnetic excitation field $\bH$ (or magnetic field for short).
So we write

\begin{equation}
    \bF(\br, t)  = \mathbf{f}(z) \, \ei{(q x - \omega t)}, \,\bF \in \{\bE, \bH\},
    \label{eq:oscillatory_electric_field}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the lowercase $\mathbf{f}$ is $\mathbf{e}$ in the case of the electric field or $\mathbf{h}$ in the case of the magnetic field, if we factor out the $z$ dependence from the $x$ and $t$ dependences for both fields; the system is $y$ invariant.
To obtain an equation governing the $z$ dependence, we take the curl of Faraday's law~\eqref{subeq:Faraday_law} and use Ampère--Maxwell's law~\eqref{subeq:Ampere_law}, with both laws in frequency domain.
Furthermore, we work with the total displacement field $\bD$ defined in Section~\ref{subsec:constitutive_relations_linear_response} and with no contribution coming from external currents such that $\bJ = \bzero$. We can then write for the electric field the so-called Helmholtz equation

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\lap \bE(\br,\omega) +  \frac{\omega^2 \vareps(\omega)}{c^2} \bE (\br,\omega) &= \bzero \Leftrightarrow \\
\Leftrightarrow \qty(\lap +  k_0^2) \bE (\br,\omega) &= \bzero,
\end{split}
\end{equation}


\noindent by defining $k_0^2 \equiv \omega^2 \vareps(\omega) /c^2$, and neglecting non-local effects in the material (either 1 or 2), so that the permittivity appearing is only frequency dependent.
This allows to transform the spatial convolution of the permittivity with the electric field into a mere multiplication, such that $\bD(\br,\omega) = \vareps(\omega) \bE(\br,\omega)$ as already explained previously (see Section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_local_limit}).
Otherwise, we would have to take spatial derivatives of the response function.
This is equivalent to taking in~\eqref{subeq:wave_equation_F} the \lra~limit $\bk \to \bzero$ of $\vareps_{\text{eq}}(\bk,\omega)$, and converting the square of the wavenumber $k^2$ back into the Laplacian operator in real space (up to a sign).
Replacing the electric field $\bE$ by expression~\eqref{eq:oscillatory_electric_field}, we obtain the ordinary differential equation

\begin{equation}
    \dv[2]{\mathbf{e}(z)}{z} + \qty(k_0^2 - q^2) \mathbf{e}(z) = \bzero .
\end{equation}

Now what is left is to relate the components of the electric field with the components of the magnetic field.
This is achieved by using again Faraday's law~\eqref{subeq:Faraday_law}, and Ampère--Maxwell's law~\eqref{subeq:Ampere_law} in frequency domain with no external current sources, but for the uppercase electric field $\bE$ and the magnetic excitation field $\bH$, not the undercase fields dependent only on $z$.
We rewrite those two laws in terms of Cartesian coordinates omitting the frequency dependence in the material's permittivity, using $y$ invariance so that $\partial_y = 0$ and replacing $\partial_x \to \ii q$ to write

        \label{fig:TE_and_TM_modes}
    \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\newpage

For \gls{tm} modes, the equations in~\eqref{eq:Maxwells_equations_components} read

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:TM_modes_E_and_H_field_equations}
    \begin{split}
        \partial_z E_x-\ii q E_z &=\ii \omega \mu_0 H_y\,,\\
        \partial_z H_y &=\ii \omega \varepsilon \varepsilon_0  E_x\,,\\
        \ii q H_y &=-\ii \omega \varepsilon \varepsilon_0 E_z\,.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent From this set of equations, upon elimination of the electric field components, we obtain the equation

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial^2 H_y}{\partial z^2} + \left(\frac{\omega^2 \vareps}{c^2} - q^2\right) H_y = 0.
\end{equation}

\noindent According to equation~\eqref{eq:oscillatory_electric_field}, assuming that (the spatial dependence of) the magnetic field is of the form

\begin{equation}
    H_y (x,z) = A \ei{q x} \ex{- \kappa |z|},
\end{equation}

\noindent we obtain for the extinction coefficient

\begin{equation}
    \kappa = \sqrt{q^2 - \varepsilon \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We consider separately the bulk materials in the two different domains, the one over the interface with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_1$ and the one below with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_2$, as in Figure~\ref{fig:cladded_monolayer}.
From inspection of~\eqref{eq:TM_modes_E_and_H_field_equations}, the spatially dependent part of the electric and magnetic fields write ($j=1$ for $z>0$ and $j=2$ for $z<0$)

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        H_{jy}(x,z)&=A_j \ei{q x} \ex{ (-1)^{j} \kappa_j z}, \\[1ex]
        E_{jx}(x,z)&=- \ii (-1)^j A_j \frac{\kappa_j}{\varepsilon_j \varepsilon_0 \omega} \ei{q x} \ex{(-1)^{j} \kappa_j z}, \qq{and}\\[1ex]
        E_{jz}(x,z)&=-A_j \frac{q}{\varepsilon_j \varepsilon_0 \omega} \ei{q x} \ex{(-1)^{j} \kappa_j z}.
    \end{align}
    \label{eq:TM_modes_fields_solution_again}
\end{subequations}

\noindent By continuity of the in-plane component of the electric field at the interface $z=0$, as per~\eqref{eq:boundary_condition_E} we have

\begin{equation}
    E_{1 x} (z = 0) = E_{2 x} (z = 0) \Leftrightarrow A_2 = - \frac{\varepsilon_2 \kappa_1}{\varepsilon_1 \kappa_2} A_1.
    \label{eq:Et_continuity}
\end{equation}

\noindent The parallel component of the magnetic field, however, is discontinuous.
Recalling condition~\eqref{eq:boundary_condition_H};

\begin{equation}
    \bn_{12} \times( \bH_{2} - \bH_{1} )= \bj_s \Leftrightarrow \bH_{1,||} - \bH_{2,||} = - \bj_s \times \bn_{12}\,.
    \label{eq:H_boundary_condition}
\end{equation}

\noindent The sheet current density $\bj_s$ relates to the electric field $\bE$ through Ohm's law, where the conductivity is that of the \bd~material, $\sigma$.
In the frequency domain, and within the \lra, Ohm's law is as simple as $\bj_s(\br,\omega) = \sigma(\omega) \bE(\br,\omega)$.
For \gls{tm} modes, and taking into account the tensor nature of the conductivity, the \rhs~of the previous equation reads

\begin{equation}
    \bj_s \times \bn_{12} = \sigma_{xx} E_x \bey\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Rewriting (\ref{eq:H_boundary_condition}), using (\ref{eq:Et_continuity}) and simplifying

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        H_{1t}(z = 0) - H_{2t}(z = 0) &= - \sigma_{xx} E_x(z = 0) \Leftrightarrow \\
        A_1 - A_2 &= - \sigma_{xx} \ii \frac{\kappa_1}{\varepsilon_1 \varepsilon_0 \omega} A_1 \Leftrightarrow \\
        1 + \frac{\varepsilon_2 \kappa_1}{\varepsilon_1 \kappa_2} &= - \ii \sigma_{xx} \frac{\kappa_1}{\varepsilon_1 \varepsilon_0 \omega} \Leftrightarrow \\
        \frac{\varepsilon_1}{\kappa_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{\kappa_2} + \ii  \frac{\sigma_{xx}}{\varepsilon_0 \omega} &= 0,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\kappa_j = \sqrt{q^2 - \varepsilon_j \omega^2 / c^2}$, $j=1,2$.

We have finally obtained the dispersion relation of \gls{tm} polarized polaritons (omitting all frequency dependences for clarity)

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:TM_polariton_dispersion_relation}
    \frac{\varepsilon_1}{\kappa_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{\kappa_2} + \ii  \frac{\sigma_{xx}}{\epsz \omega} = 0
\end{empheq}

We can see that \gls{tm} polarized polaritons are supported in the range of frequencies for which $\Im\{\sigma_{xx}(\omega)\} <0$ (condition that results in positive values for the extinction coefficients).
If the real part of the conductivity is finite (non-zero), that is, if there is loss, then the equation holds on the complex plane, and either the frequency or the wavenumber has to be complex.

An analogous procedure for \gls{te} modes would lead to

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:TE_polariton_dispersion_relation}
    \kappa_1 + \kappa_2 - \ii \omega \mu_0 \sigma_{yy} = 0\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent the dispersion relation for \gls{te} modes.
We can see that the system supports \gls{te} modes when $\Im \{\sigma_{yy}(\omega) \} < 0$,
and if the real part of the conductivity is finite, then the dispersion relation holds on the complex plane, just like for \gls{tm} modes.
Analytically, both dispersion relations~\eqref{eq:TM_polariton_dispersion_relation} and~\eqref{eq:TE_polariton_dispersion_relation} can only be written implicitly, so we have to solve them numerically to obtain the dispersion relations in its explicit form $\omega(q)$.

We consider the particular case of \gls{tm} polarization and no \bd~material response, $\sigma(\omega) = 0$, so that the interface is simply the boundary separating a dielectric and a metal.
In that case, we finally arrive at the dispersion relation for \gls{spp}s

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\varepsilon_1}{\kappa_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{\kappa_2} = 0\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent With material 1 being a dielectric, and material 2 being a metal, \gls{spp}s can be supported for frequency windows where $\vareps_1$ and $\vareps_2$ have opposite signs~\cite{GoncalvesPeres:2016,maier_plasmonics_2007}.
This can be achieved in common lossless Drude metals, pertaining to the $\gamma=0$ case in equation~\eqref{eq:Drude_dielectric_function} enabling $\vareps_2(\omega) < 0$, while for most insulators $\vareps_1$ can be taken as a positive constant for all frequencies.

Figure~\ref{fig:SPP_dispersion_relation} shows the dispersive behavior of \gls{spp}s at a dielectric--metal interface, where the dielectric is air, and the metal is a local Drude metal whose permittivity is given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:Drude_dielectric_function}.
In panel \pnl{a}, for the lossless case, one can notice the very distinct behavior of the \gls{spp} dispersion relation in the bottom branch in blue, and of the wave propagating inside the metal in the top branch from the linear dispersion curve $\omega(q) = qc/\sqrt{\vareps_1}$, which corresponds to light propagating in air.
We can notice a region of forbidden frequencies opening a gap between the two branches.
In the top branch in red, the low-frequency behavior of the \gls{spp} is light-like, which contrasts with the high-frequency behavior where the polariton can achieve very high wavenumbers, meaning we can realize really high \gls{em} field confinement in this arrangement.

The peculiar properties of this \gls{em} mode can in principle be exploited for \gls{em} field manipulation at very small length scales and promote strong light--matter interactions.
However, and quite unfortunately, one cannot ignore Ohmic losses, especially in metals, henceforth we would say that the situation depicted in panel \pnl{b} is more realistic.
In the lossy case, the theoretical value for the highest wavenumber we could possibly achieve is more comparable to that of free light (but still higher).
Furthermore, the fact that the permittivity is complex results in a wavenumber with non-negligible imaginary part~\cite{Wolff_2018}, that actually peaks at approximately the same frequency where the real part peaks.
It tells us that, in practice, a high-field confinement means also high losses.
Even though we now have \gls{em} modes in the frequency region that was previously forbidden, these modes are evanescent and do not propagate over long distances.
Recall that the decay length of an evanescent wave is given by the inverse of the imaginary part of the wavenumber, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:waves_in_matter}.


It is pertinent here to make the following technical remark.
In the analysis made above, for dispersion relations that may differ from that of free light and in the case the permittivity is complex, we could have assumed instead that the frequency $\omega$ is complex, while the wavenumber $q$ is kept real\footnote{The reader might wonder if we could even consider complex frequency and wavenumber at the same time. One may do so, but that increases the dimension of the space we work with without necessity, making it harder to understand the physics and visualize the results. It is thus sufficient to consider in turns a real valued frequency or a real valued wavenumber ``cuts'' of that higher dimensional and more abstract space.\label{fn:complex_frequency}}.
In theory both approaches are valid, but if we are to compare theory with experiments, then we have to be aware of what choice would be significant.
For instance, if we assume that the frequency is complex, then it means the polariton would be a propagating wave, but decaying in time.
We should not expect to observe this effect in a continuous wave experiment, for instance.
To observe it, an experiment where the system is probed by short-lived light pulses is more adequate.
For a continuous wave experiment the complex valued wavenumber picture would offer more reliable predictions.

We will see later on in this work another kind of surface polaritons, where the polar matter excitation is one characteristic of insulators and semiconductors, the so-called exciton.
The coupling of the exciton with the \gls{em} field is logically called exciton--polariton.
The exciton is a quasi-particle that results from the optical excitation of materials that present an electronic band gap.
These can be classified as semiconductors if the band gap is smaller than or of the order of the environment thermal energy dictated by the external temperature, or insulators if the band gap is large compared with the thermal energy.
Before we can understand these concepts (band gap, insulators/semiconductors) and describe this exciton quasi-particle, and the consequent coupling with light, we need to dive deeper into the field of quantum physics, with particular emphasis on solid-state matter in the context of condensed matter physics.
That is what the next chapter is devoted to, introduce the reader to the concepts of quantum mechanics that are necessary to address the excitations of materials at length scales where the tools of classical physics are deemed insufficient.
The excitations of matter using a quantum description of materials is then the subject of the subsequent chapters.

Before we conclude the chapter and leave the realm of classical physics, we show that it can still offer more insight into how light and matter interact.
In the next section we will see how the laws of classical electrodynamics allow us to estimate the amount of radiation that can be absorbed, reflected or transmitted at an interface between two domain regions when light traverses from one region to the other.
It will culminate with Fresnel's coefficients, but in the more general case where the interface has its own optical response.

\subsection{Material absorption, reflection and transmission}
\label{subsec:polaritonics_absorption}

The purpose of this section is to derive an expression for the amount of \gls{em} radiation that is absorbed by a \bd~material when impinged by a linearly polarized plane wave field.
The configuration is that of Figure~\ref{fig:normal_incidence}, with an incoming plane wave propagating from above in medium 1 with normal incidence on the interface, with the \bd~material reflecting and transmitting part of the incident radiation.
Using Maxwell's equations and the boundary conditions at the interface once more, we will derive the Fresnel coefficients in the particular case of normal incidence, but in the more general form where the surface separating the two media has its own optical response $\sigma$ (we will omit for now the frequency dependence of the conductivity).
Omitting the time dependence, we write for the incident electric field $\bE_\inc = A_i \ei[-]{k_i z} \bex$, for the reflected electric field $\bE_\refl = A_r \ei{k_r z}  \bex$, and for the transmitted electric field $\bE_\trans = A_t \ee^{-\ii k_t z}  \bex$.
Capital $A$ stands for field amplitude.
Using Faraday's law in reciprocal space~\eqref{subeq:Faradays_law_reciprocal}, the incident, reflected and transmitted magnetic fields read $\bH_\inc = - k_i/\omega \mu_0 A_i \ei[-]{k_i z} \bey$, $\bH_\refl = k_i/\omega \mu_0 A_r \ei[-]{k_i z} \bey$ and $\bH_\trans = - k_t/\omega \mu_0 A_t \ee^{-\ii k_t z} \bey$, respectively.
Boundary conditions at the interface $z=0$ given by~\eqref{eq:boundary_condition_E} and~\eqref{eq:boundary_condition_H}, where the subscript ``2'' denotes the transmitted field, while the subscript ``1'' denotes the total field in region 1, incident plus reflected, demand that


\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        A_i + A_r &= A_t\,, \label{subeq:boundary_condition_E_incidence}\\
        -\frac{k_i}{\omega \mu_0} A_i + \frac{k_r}{\omega \mu_0} A_r + \frac{k_t}{\omega \mu_0} A_t &= - \sigma A_t \label{subeq:boundary_condition_H_incidence}\,.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent Note that in the \gls{rhs} of the second equation we could equally write $A_i + A_r$ instead of $A_t$, due to the continuity of the tangential component of the electric field.
Using the identity $k = n \omega / c $ and solving the previous system of equations, we can define the Fresnel coefficients for reflection, $r$, and transmission, $t$, as

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        r &\equiv \frac{A_r}{A_i} = \frac{n_1 - n_2 - \sigma/c \epsz}{n_1 + n_2 + \sigma/c \epsz} \qq{and} \label{subeq:Fresnel_reflection_coefficient} \\
        t &\equiv \frac{A_t}{A_i} = \frac{2 n_1}{n_1 + n_2 + \sigma/c \epsz}\,. \label{subeq:Fresnel_transmission_coefficient}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{Chapters/Figures/Chapter_2/normal_incidence}
    \mycaption[Normal incidence of a plane wave on an interface between two dielectrics.]{A plane wave with wavevector $\bk_\inc$ and frequency $\omega$, travelling through medium 1 with permittivity $\vareps_1$, impinges on the surface of a \bd~material, in blue, deposited on top of a dielectric substrate with permittivity $\vareps_2$.}
    \label{fig:normal_incidence}
\end{figure}


The attentive reader may notice that the Fresnel coefficients are not yet a measure of the amount of radiation that the \bd~material reflects or transmits, they are only relative field amplitudes.
In order to calculate the amount of radiation reflected and transmitted, and consequently absorbed, we actually still need to calculate the relative field intensities.
We can define the intensity of the \gls{em} field as the magnitude of the Poynting vector $\bS$, defined by $\bS = \bE \times \bH$~\cite{Jackson:electrodynamics,Hohenester_2020}, averaged in time over one complete oscillation.
As we are considering only plane waves composed of a single harmonic frequency $\omega = c k /n$, we can write for the time-averaged Poynting vector~\cite{Hohenester_2020}

\begin{equation}
    \langle \bS \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \Re\{\bE \times \bH^*\} = \frac{\qty|\bE|^2}{2 \omega \mu_0} \Re{\bk}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where, to get the second equality, we made use once more of Faraday's law, and also the vector identity for the double cross product already mentioned in Section~\ref{subsec:waves_in_matter}.
For the incident and reflected radiation fields, respectively, we have $\bS_\inc = -\frac{1}{2} c \epsz \qty|\bE_\inc|^2  \Re{n_1} \bez$ and $\bS_\refl =\frac{1}{2} c \epsz \qty|\bE_\refl|^2 \Re{n_1}\bez$, while for the transmitted field we have $\bS_\trans = - \frac{1}{2} c \epsz \qty|\bE_\trans|^2 \Re{n_2} \bez$.
We can finally define the relative amount of radiation that is reflected and transmitted at the interface, respectively, as

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \mathcal{R} &= \frac{\langle \qty|\bS_\refl| \rangle}{\langle \qty|\bS_\inc| \rangle} = \frac{\qty|\bE_\refl|^2}{\qty|\bE_\inc|^2} = \qty|r|^2 \qq{and} \label{subeq:reflected_radiation}\\
        \mathcal{T} &= \frac{\langle \qty|\bS_\trans| \rangle}{\langle \qty|\bS_\inc| \rangle} = \frac{\Re{n_2}}{\Re{n_1}} \frac{\qty|\bE_\trans|^2}{\qty|\bE_\inc|^2}  = \frac{\Re{n_2}}{\Re{n_1}} \qty|t|^2 \,,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent where the expressions for $r$ and $t$ can be found in equations~\eqref{subeq:Fresnel_reflection_coefficient} and~\eqref{subeq:Fresnel_transmission_coefficient}, respectively.
We are in place now to compute the amount of radiation that is absorbed by the \bd~material, or simply absorption, through the identity

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{equation}
    \mathcal{A} = 1 - \mathcal{R} - \mathcal{T} = 1 - |r|^2 - \frac{\Re{n_2}}{\Re{n_1}} |t|^2
    \label{eq:absorption}
\end{empheq}

Maxwell's equations valid in continuous domains, together with the boundary conditions for boundaries between domains and the constitutive relations, form a closed system of equations, with which we could tackle any problem in electrodynamics involving \bd~materials; at least in principle, and if not analytically, then numerically.
It happens that to make realistic predictions and expect agreement with experiments, we would need to know how the dielectric function, or equivalently the conductivity, depends on the frequency and wavevector\footnote{Apart from non-linear phenomena, where higher-order susceptibilities have to be defined, and it no longer makes sense speaking of a single permittivity or conductivity.}.
We have considered particular cases in the previous Section~\ref{subsec:constitutive_relations_linear_response}, for which certain approximations allow for writing an analytical expression for the dielectric function, as the example of the local Drude metal, its extension to the Drude--Lorentz model for dielectrics and the \bd~dielectric function in the static limit.
Up until now, we have not discussed how in fact such models can be obtained for different materials.
If we could do so, then we could know what values to use for the adjustable parameters in each model, and maybe even find the complete dependence of the material properties in the whole $(\bk,\omega)$ domain, even if numerically.
For us to have hope in doing so, we need the appropriate tools necessary to describe nanometric materials, or at even smaller ones down to the atomic level, where a plethora of different effects and phenomena underlie the emerging macroscopic physics that can be observed in real experiments.
In the next chapter we introduce the reader to the principles of quantum mechanics that are absolutely necessary to attack the problem of electrodynamics in materials at the quantum level, with focus on crystalline solids.
This will allow us to have a proper, microscopic description of screening in periodic systems at the single-particle or noninteracting level.
It is only after having a proper, microscopic, description of electronic properties and screening in solids that we can describe phenomena at the interacting level, namely excitons and related optical properties, the topics of the chapters coming after the next one.
