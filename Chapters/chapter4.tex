\chapter{Excitonic effects in Two-dimensional Semiconductors}
\label{ch:Excitons}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In previous chapters, we have discussed how a classical description of electrodynamics in matter together with a quantum description of its properties, we can describe different kinds of phenomena in solids.
The tools of quantum mechanics provide means of calculating the dielectric function, and as we will see, also the conductivity.
Given their equivalence established in the first chapter, knowing the dielectric function is sufficient to describe electrodynamic effects, such as dispersion, bulk plasmons in Drude metals, Ohmic losses and material absorption, the last two more directly through the conductivity.
The fact that the classical description of plasmons, absorption and reflection in metals via Maxwell's equations and empiric dielectric functions is so successful, although it might seem surprising, can now be understood.
Per our quantum description of matter, we have seen that gapped materials can be identified with insulators and semiconductors, poor conducting materials.
This is so because the valence bands in such materials are filled, requiring large amounts of energy to excite electrons to the conduction band such that there is a nonvanishing current~\cite{Kittel_2004,Simon_Oxford_2013}.
In contrast, metals are good conductors because the highest valence band is partially filled.
This means that it requires small amounts of energy to excite the electrons so that they can more easily participate in the charge flow.
In other words, in gapped undoped materials and at low temperatures, the system can only be excited from the ground state via energetically costing interband transitions, whereas metals can be excited via energetically efficient intraband transitions.
However, if the energies involved are high enough, a metal can experience interband transitions, which bring a contribution to the optical response that the Drude approach cannot capture.
Such an example is gold~\cite{Christy_1972}, which displays a Drude-like optical conductivity until a certain frequency threshold, above which the optical measurements evidence the shortcomings of the classical model.
Ad hoc corrections to the Drude model can be made~\cite{Meyer_2006}, but a complete picture is still missing.
One rapidly sees that classical models cannot explain all phenomena in solids, in particular phenomena observed in optical spectroscopy that induce quantum effects with no classical analogue.

Within quantum mechanics, we can quantify material responses that stem from interband transitions with no classical counterpart, which are naturally incorporated in the dielectric function through the expression for the polarizability as per~\eqref{eq:polarizability_momentum_frequency}, which in fact includes interband and intraband transitions.
Only in this way can we expect to describe the dielectric properties of insulators and semiconductors.
However, the expression for the dielectric function obtained in the previous chapter was written in terms of a noninteracting polarizability $\chi^0$, that accounts solely for single-particle transitions.
An optical experiment, for instance, a photoluminescence or optical absorption one, would measure an optical response for frequencies above the bandgap, where conduction states can be populated by electrons exited from the valence bands by photon absorption.
The same experiment, when swiping the frequency of the incident light along the bandgap, would detect any response.
However, that is not what happens.
One photoluminescence experiment has observed an absorption resonance for monolayer \gls{hbn} at an energy lying inside the electronic bandgap~\cite{Cassabois_2019}.
This observation can only be explained by taking into account the electron--electron interactions beyond the mean-field level and going beyond the single-particle picture.
The two-body interaction allows a quantum system state that we could not predict from the machinery introduced so far---a many-body state.

Up until now, we have been stressing that the full interacting response of condensed matter systems cannot be computed in practice, which is true.
Nonetheless, we do not need the full interacting picture to explain the experimental observations.
We just need to account for the Coulomb interaction between the electron, that was excited to the conduction band, and the absence of the negative charge that it left behind in the valence band---the positively charged hole.
This interaction between the electron and the hole, just like the atomic nucleus in the hydrogen atom attracts the orbiting electron, can be so strong that the electron--hole pair form a bound state---the exciton~\cite{Datta_Marie_2024}.
The situation is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation}, where the electron, the red circle in the figure, is in the conduction band, and the hole, the red circumference, is in the valence band.
As we have just explained, a photon with an energy lying inside the bandgap can excite the material from its ground state to an excitonic state, or one of several excitonic states.
The minimum amount of energy required for that to happen is what defines the \textit{optical} bandgap, which coincides with the energy of the \textit{excitonic} ground state.
We can define the binding energy $E_{b, \nu}$ of a given exciton in a state $\nu$, depicted in the schematic at the right in Fig~\ref{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation}, as the difference between the energy of the electronic bandgap $\Delta$ and the optical one $E_X$, the latter coinciding with the energy of the excitonic ground state.
Just like the ionization energy in the hydrogen atom, it can be interpreted as the energy that we have to provide to separate the electron from the hole, resulting in their recombination.

These realizations do not mean that all the techniques introduced until now in the last two chapters are not useful; actually the opposite.
As we will see, getting the Coulomb attraction, which is essentially electrostatic, right in a solid is essential in order to compute the excitons correctly.
We mean that we need to take screening into account by using an appropriate dielectric function.
As we shall explain in a moment, this is crucial in \bd~materials.
But first, let us try top draw some conclusions and gain intuition on the problem before we appropriately tackle it.

As we shall see in this chapter, the excitonic problem that consists of finding the excitonic eigenenergies and eigenstates (or wavefunctions) can be quite intricate.
Nonetheless, we have argued that determining the excitonic properties of a material is key to describe their optoelectronic properties, in particular those related to the sub-bandgap physics of gapped materials.
At the range of frequencies considered in this work, no other properties come into play, such as phonon or magnetic excitations.
Herein, we only model the electronic and optical properties of \bd~materials, and how these relate with each other.
There are several approaches to the exciton problem, and in this chapter we provide a couple of them, going up in difficulty and complexity.

Within a very crude approximation, the problem of bound electron--hole complexes can be identified with the hydrogen atom problem if the energy surfaces for the electron and the hole are spherical and nondegenerate~\cite{Kittel_2004}.
From a heuristic point of view, this implies that the energy levels are given by a modified Rydberg formula

\begin{equation}
    E_{X,n} = E_g - \frac{R^*}{n^2}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Rydberg_modified}
    R^* = \frac{2 \mu^2 e^4}{\hbar^2 (8 \pi \varepsilon_r)^2}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the modified Rydberg constant, which instead of using the electron mass like usual, it uses the reduced mass between the electron and the hole effective masses

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{m_e} + \frac{1}{m_h}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $\varepsilon_r$ is just the dielectric constant of the medium.
This description is suited for weakly bound excitons, also-called Wannier excitons~\cite{Koch_2009}, originally proposed by Wannier~\cite{Wannier_1937} and Mott~\cite{Mott_Littleton_1938,Mott_1938_II,Mott_1938_III}.
The characteristic size of the exciton is given by a modified expression for the Bohr radius:

\begin{equation}
    a_B^* = \frac{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 \hbar^2}{\mu^2 e^2}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which can extend over a couple of atomic sites.
If the exciton is tightly bound, then it is called a Frenkel exciton~\cite{Frenkel_1931_I,Frenkel_1931_II,Davydov_1971}.
In this case, the excitation is localized at a single atom.
Wannier excitons can form in inorganic semiconductors, like cuprous oxide, \ch{Cu2O}, or gallium arsenide, \ch{GaAs}.
For \ch{GaAs}, the binding energy of the ground state exciton is $4.2$ \unit{\milli\electronvolt}~\cite{GaAs_exciton_1976}.
Frenkel excitons are more common in ionic crystals~\cite{Knox_Inchauspe_1959}, such as \ch{CuCl} or \ch{NaCl} or organic molecular crystals~\cite{Nematiaram_Padula_Troisi_2021,Silinsh_1980}.
In solid krypton, the ground state excitonic binding energy is around $1.75$ \unit{\electronvolt}~\cite{Laporte_1985}.

If we wish to apply the description above of Wannier excitons to \bd~materials, the excitonic binding energies are given now by the modified Rydberg formula for the \bd~hydrogen atom~\cite{2D_Hydrogen_atom_1991}

\begin{equation}
    E^{\BD}_{X,n} = - \frac{R^*}{\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^2}\,.
\end{equation}

Focusing on the ground state $n=1$, we can see that $E^{\BD}_{X,n=1} = 4 E_{X,n=1}$.
Furthermore, the Bohr radius for \bd~materials is half of that of the \gls{3d} case~\cite{2D_Hydrogen_atom_1991}.
We can then predict that the exciton contribution to the optical spectrum is more prominent in \bd~materials when compared to \gls{3d} ones.
This is a consequence of the reduced dimensionality, deeming the screening of the electrostatic Coulomb interaction in \bd~materials less effective than in \gls{3d} ones~\cite{Cudazzo_2011}.
In \bd~materials excitons can form even at room temperatures~\cite{Morozov_Wolff_Mortensen_2021}.
Therefore, the hydrogen atom picture for the excitons does not apply to \bd~materials, as the binding energies do not follow the hydrogenic Rydberg series, and a modified Rydberg constant as per~Eq.\eqref{eq:Rydberg_modified} with a dielectric constant is not valid.
As we shall see later on, mainly in Chapter~\ref{ch:screening}, the dielectric function in \bd~gapped materials is strongly non-local.
This implies that we cannot speak of a single dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r$ as a relative permittivity, and writing for the screened potential $W(r) = v_c(r)/\varepsilon_r$ is not sufficient.
Actually, it has even been seen experimentally, at least for \ch{WS2}, that the series of excitonic energies is nonhydrogenic~\cite{Chernikov_2014}, and this behavior is expected to be universal among \bd~insulators.
The \bd~material \ch{WS2} belongs to a class of semiconducting materials called \glspl{tmd}~\cite{Manzeli_Ovchinnikov_Pasquier_Yazyev_Kis_2017}, which have by chemical formula \ch{MX2}, where \ch{M} is a transition metal, such as molybdenum (\ch{Mo}) or wolfram (\ch{W}), and \ch{X} is a chalcogen, such as sulfur (\ch{S}) or selenium (\ch{Se}).
In bulk, they are layered materials and present an indirect bandgap, while in their monolayer limit the bandgap is direct.
Among them, there is molybdenum disulfide~\cite{Singh_Kumar_Late_Kumar_Patel_Singh_2018}, with chemical formula \ch{MoS2}, a prototypical \bd~material for studying excitons that we shall encounter later on in this work.

In order to explain the peculiarities of the excitons in two dimensions, we can in a first attempt adopt the hydrogen atom model, but for the electron--hole complex, and a modified potential.
The excitonic states and respective binding energies can be estimated by solving the Wannier--Mott equation~\cite{Wannier_1937}, or simply Wannier equation, that reads

\begin{equation}
    E_{b, \nu} \psi_\nu (\br) =  \frac{p ^2}{2 \mu} \psi_\nu (\br) - V(\br) \psi_\nu (\br),
\label{eq:Wannier_equation}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu$ is the electron--hole reduced effective mass as before, and $V(\br)$ is the attractive potential.
One can notice similarities with the \gls{tise} for the hydrogen atom, but with an effective reduced mass (between two particles that have a comparable mass), and an attractive potential, but including now screening effects from the material itself.
We have seen before, in the section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit}, that for \bd~materials the dielectric function in the static limit has nontrivial momentum dependence.
It is linear in $q$ as $\varepsilon(q) = 1 + r_0 q$, and we called this permittivity with the model parameter $r_0$ the Rytova--Keldysh dielectric function, giving origin to the Rytova--Keldysh potential~\cite{keldysh2024coulomb}.

The Wannier equation does not capture the full structure of the excitonic states (like nondegenerate $p$ states or the symmetries of the crystal), hence we need to resort to more accurate descriptions of the excitonic problem.
A breakdown of the Wannier model occurs when the exciton is delocalized in reciprocal space, so that we have to account for the band structure beyond the effective mass approximation.
Nonetheless, the Wannier--Mott formalism offers good theoretical insights, even in the \bd~case where an analytical solution cannot be found.
One can go beyond by using a reciprocal space description of the excitons through the so-called \gls{bse}---an integral equation where finer details of the band structure, the Coulomb interaction and the system Hamiltonian are included.

In this work, we work with two versions of the \gls{bse}\@.
One that we will apply to effective low-energy models, and another one using exact diagonalization methods.
The \abinit~version of the \gls{bse}\footnote{For a reader fond of high-energy physics, we leave here a historical. The \gls{bse} was introduced in the literature by Y.~Nambu in 1950~\cite{Nambu_1950} and is named after the physicists Hans Bethe and Edwin Salpeter, who derived it in 1951~\cite{Bethe_Salpeter_1951}. Its original formulation is in the context of quantum field theories, where bound fermion-pairs can arise as well. One example is the electron--positron pair, positronium. Either in the context of particle physics or condensed matter, the starting point of the derivation is defining a four-point, or two-particle, interacting Green function. Just like in section~\ref{sec:many_particle_description} for the many-particle description of solids, where we introduced the diagram notation, one can also write the \acrlong{bse} in a diagrammatic form (which of course has its own version in particle physics).}. is not addressed here.
Besides being technically more involved, it is extremely demanding computationally, so it is necessary to come up with more efficient approaches.
Nonetheless, it can be frequently encountered in the literature, in particular works using \abinit~methods for the optical response of materials~\cite{BerkeleyGW,Yambo_2009,}.
If the reader is curious about it, we refer to the original work~\cite{Louie_2000} by M.~Rohlfing and S.~G.~Louie, or the PhD thesis~\cite{Alex_PhD} for a more recent text with a derivation adapted from~\cite{Louie_2000}.

In the following section we derive the Bethe--Salpeter equation applied to effective models, followed by a derivation of the Wannier equation.
The section after dwells once more on the \gls{bse}, but with using exact diagonalization methods as they are implemented in the code we contributed to in this thesis.
To finalize the chapter, we derive expressions to study the optical response of materials, with and without excitonic effects.


\section{The Bethe--Salpeter Equation: application to effective models}
\label{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_effective_models}

In this section we derive the \gls{bse}, an integral equation written in momentum space allowing a more accurate description of a material's excitonic properties.
A $\bk$-space description of the problem feels natural, given the concepts introduced in the previous chapter.
We will use the tools therein acquired to our advantage.
This derivation is adapted from a Master's thesis in Ref.~\cite{Henriques_thesis} and the tutorial in Ref.~\cite{}, where one can look into it with more detail.
Here we explain as well how to arrive at the interaction Hamiltonian in momentum space, which is not as easy as in high-energy physics, because one has to account for the crystalline structure of the material.
Instead of working within a plane-wave representation, we work with a basis of Bloch states, but we shall exemplify how in a moment.

We have seen that the ground state of insulators, or semiconductors at low enough temperatures, comprises all the valence bands filled and the conduction bands empty.
Many times, in the context of many-particle physics, the ground state is called Fermi sea.
The Fermi sea comes from populating the electronic states of the material starting from the lowest energy states, and filling up the states with increasing energy, until charge neutrality has been reached.
If there is no electronic bandgap, the energy of the highest occupied state, at zero temperature, is called the Fermi energy or Fermi level, $\EF$ in symbols.
In metals, the Fermi energy lies within the band width of the highest valence band, which is partially filled.
In semiconductors and insulators, the Fermi energy lies in the midway between the energy of the highest occupied state and the energy of the lowest unoccupied state.
In general, it can be defined as the chemical potential\footnote{Usualy introduced in the context of thermodynamics and statistical physics, the chemical potential can be defined as .....} at zero temperature~\cite{Simon_Oxford_2013}.
Since we introduced the exciton as a bound electron--hole complex, we will assume that the unbound electron--hole states serve as a good basis in which we can expand the excitonic states.
To simplify the reasoning, we assume that only the top valence band and the lowest conduction band contribute to the exciton.
In this way, in the second quantization formalism we can write the generic exciton state $\nu$ as the coherent superposition of electron--hole pairs in the following way


\begin{equation}
    b_\nu^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) c_{\bk, c}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, v}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $A$ is the extension area of the \bd~system, $\phi_{\nu}(\bk)$ is the momentum-resolved excitonic wavefunction (that we wish to find), $\nu$ is a set of (good) exciton quantum numbers, and $c^\dagger_{\bk, \lambda}$ ($c_{\bk, \lambda}$) creates (annihilates) an electron with momentum $\bk$ in the band $\lambda$.
For our two-band model $\lambda = v,c$.
Often in the literature, one works with a fermionic operator for the hole as a quasiparticle, but that is not strictly necessary.
For us, the hole is perceived simply as the absence of an electron, and we stick to electron operators.
Sometimes, we can adopt the nomenclature from atomic physics to label the exciton states, say $1s$, $2s$, $2p$, etc.
Here, we assume only vertical transitions due to the small wavevector carried by the photon, meaning that the exciton center of mass carries no momentum.
We denote by $\ket{\GS}$ the ground (or vacuum) state of the material, with a completely filled valence band and an empty conduction band.
This should be sufficient for a two-band model.

The operator $b_{\nu}^{\dagger}$ acting on the ground state creates one exciton in the state $| X_\nu \rangle$; in the occupation number representation, we can write

\begin{equation}
    b_{\nu}^{\dagger}|\GS\rangle=|1_{v \bk_1},1_{v \bk_2},1_{v \bk_f},0,0,\dots,\underset{\underset{X_\nu}{\downarrow}}{1},0,0,\dots,0_{c \bk_1},0_{c \bk_2},\dots,0_{c\bk_f}\rangle\,.
\end{equation}

Since the operators $b$ and $b^{\dagger}$ are quadratic in fermionic operators, they obey commutation relations, hence they are bosonic.

The total Hamiltonian of the system

\begin{equation}
    \Ha = \Ho + \hat{V}
\end{equation}

\noindent has two parts, the kinetic or noninteracting one, that after diagonalized and ignoring spin, reads

\begin{equation}
    \Ho = \sum_{\bk, \lambda} \epsilon_{\bk, \lambda} c_{\bk, \lambda}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, \lambda},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\lambda$ is the band index, $\epsilon_{\bk, \lambda}$ is the energy of an electron with momentum $\bk$ in the band $\lambda$, and the interacting one, that in a real space basis reads

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_field_operators}
    \hat{V} = \frac{1}{2} \int \dr \int \dr '\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\br)\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\br') V (\br - \br')\hat{\psi}(\br')\hat{\psi}(\br).
\end{equation}

\noindent if we ignore the spin degree of freedom once more, and $\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\br)/\hat{\psi}(\br)$ creates/destroys an electron at position $\br$.
These operators are called field operators, and we keep the hat on them to stress that they are operators and to distinguish from $\psi(\br)$ written to denote wavefunction.
In this work we will not worry with spinful excitons, so there is no need to include spin indices in the field and fermionic operators.
They are also unnecessary if we ignore \gls{soc} in the noninteracting Hamiltonian and assume that the electron--electron interaction includes only the Coulomb potential, so there are no interactions that can induce spin flipping.
The Coulomb potential herein used is already the Rytova--Keldysh one, and that will be implicit until the end of this section.
We use the notation $V$ instead of $W$ or $V_{\RK}$ for ease of notation.
To write both terms in the Hamiltonian in momentum space, we have to convert the interaction term to the $\bk$-space.
For that, we use the expansion of the field operators in terms of Bloch states

\begin{equation}
    \hat{\psi}(\br)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bk,\lambda}\braket{\br}{\bk,\lambda} c_{\bk,\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bk,\lambda}\psi_{\bk,\lambda}(\br)c_{\bk,\lambda}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $N$ is the number of unit cells (or $\bk$ points), and writing the potential in terms of its Fourier transform (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:finite_volume_two_point_inverse_Fourier} in Appendix~\ref{app:fourier_transform})

\begin{equation}
    V (\br - \br') = \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bq} \ee^{\ii \bq \vdot(\br-\br')} V(\bq)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent Replacing everything in~\eqref{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_field_operators}, we get

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_momentum_space_substitution}
    \begin{aligned}
        \hat{V} = & \frac{1}{2 A N^{2}} \sum_{\bq} \sum_{\{\bk_{i},\lambda_i\}} V(\bq) c_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} \times \\
                  & \times \left(\int_A \psi_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br) \ee^{\ii \bq \vdot \br}\psi_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br) \dr \right) \left(\int_A \psi_{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}^{*}(\br') \ee^{-\ii \bq \vdot \br'} \psi_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}(\br') \dr' \right)\,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sum_{\{\bk_{i},\lambda_i\}} \equiv \sum_{\bk_1 \bk_2 \bk_3 \bk_4} \sum_{\lambda_1 \lambda_2 \lambda_3 \lambda_4}$, the integrals are over the plane of the material or using braket notation

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_momentum_space_substitution_option_2}
    \begin{aligned}
        \hat{V} = & \frac{1}{2 A N^{2}}  \sum_{\bq} \sum_{\{\bk_{i},\lambda_i\}} V(\bq) c_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} \times \\
                  &  \times \mel{\bk_1,\lambda_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\bk_4,\lambda_4} \mel{\bk_2,\lambda_2}{\ei[-]{\bq \vdot \br'}}{\bk_3,\lambda_3} 
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent An auxiliary calculation for the matrix elements follows.
The Bloch wavefunctions, in the atomic gauge that we used to write the TB Bloch Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn}, have the form

\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\bk,\lambda}(\br)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bR,i\alpha}\ee^{\ii\bk\vdot(\bR+\bt_{i})}C_{i\alpha}^{\bk,\lambda}\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)
\end{equation}

\noindent Now we can go back and compute the matrix elements

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mel{\bk_1,\lambda_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\bk_4,\lambda_4} &= \int_A \psi_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)\,\ee^{\ii\bq\vdot\br}\psi_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br)\,\dr = \\
        & = \int_A u_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)\,\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bk_{1}+\bk_{4})\vdot\br}u_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br)\,\dr \approx \\
        & \approx \left \langle u_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)u_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br) \right\rangle_{\mathrm{UC}} \underbrace{\int_A \ee^{\ii(\bq-\bk_{1}+\bk_{4})\vdot\br} \dr }_{A\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}}} \,.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we used the envelope function approximation.
It consists in replacing the multiplying periodic parts of the Bloch wavefunctions (see Bloch Theorem~\ref{th:Bloch_theorem}) by their average over the unit cell.
This approximation assumes that the periodic part of the Bloch function varies fast over the length scale of the unit cell, while the exponential part varies slowly.
The average of the periodic part of the Bloch wavefunctions over a unit cell can be computed, also approximately using the point-like orbital approximation, by


\begin{align*}
    \left\langle u_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)u_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br)\right\rangle _{\mathrm{UC}} =& \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \int_{\mathrm{UC}} u_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br) u_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br)\, \dr =\\
    =& \frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \int_{\mathrm{UC}} \Bigg(\sum_{\bR,i\alpha}\ee^{-\ii\bk_{1}\vdot(\bR + \bt_{i} - \br)} \left(C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*}\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)\Bigg) \times \\
    & \times \Bigg(\sum_{\bR',j\beta} \ee^{\ii\bk_{4}\vdot(\bR'+\bt_{j}-\br)} C_{j\beta}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\phi_{\beta}\left(\br-\bR'-\bt_{j}\right)\Bigg)\dr =\\
    =&\frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \sum_{\bR,\bR'}\ee^{-\ii\bk_{1}\vdot(\bR + \bt_{i} - \br)} \ee^{\ii\bk_{4}\vdot(\bR'+\bt_{j}-\br)} \sum_{i\alpha,j\beta}\left(C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*}C_{j\beta}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} \times \\
    & \times \int_{\mathrm{UC}} \ee^{\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot\br}\underbrace{\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)\phi_{\beta} \left(\br-\bR'-\bt_{j}\right)}_{\approx \delta_{ij} \delta_{\alpha \beta} \delta_{\bR \bR'} \delta(\br-\bR-\bt_{i})}\dr\approx\\
    \approx & \frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \sum_{\bR} \cancel{\ee^{-\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot(\bR+\bt_{i})}} \sum_{i\alpha,j\beta} \left(C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*} C_{j\beta}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} \cancel{\ee^{\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot(\bR+\bt_{i})}} =\\
    =&\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \sum_{i\alpha}\left(C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*}C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} = \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\left(\bC^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}},\text{ since }\sum_{\bR}1=N.
\end{align*}

\noindent Finally, the plane-wave matrix element reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mel{\bk_1,\lambda_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\bk_4,\lambda_4} & = \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\left(\bC^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger} \bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}A\,\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}} = \\
                                                                   & = N \left(\bC^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent while for the other matrix element, the same reasoning applies.
In this way, the interaction term of the Hamiltonian, in momentum space, reads


\begin{align}
    \hat{V} = & \frac{1}{2 A N^{2}}\sum_{\bq}\sum_{\{\bk_{i},\lambda_{i}\}} V N^{2}\left(\bC^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}}\left(\bC^{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}\delta_{\bq,\bk_{3}-\bk_{2}} \times \\
              & \times  c_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} = \\
            = & \frac{1}{2 A N^{2}}\sum_{\bq,\bk_{3},\bk_{4}}\sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}V(\bq) N^{2}\left(\bC^{\bk_{4}+\bq,\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\left(\bC^{\bk_{3}-\bq,\lambda_{2}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}} \\
              & \times  c_{\bk_{4}+\bq,\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3}-\bq,\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} = \\
            = & \frac{1}{2 A}\sum_{\bq,\bk_{3},\bk_{4}}\sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}} V(\bq) F_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}}\left(\bk_{3},\bk_{4},\bq\right)c_{\bk_{4}+\bq,\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3}-\bq,\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\,,
\end{align}

\noindent where we defined the form factor $F_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}} \left(\bk,\bp,\bq\right) = \left(\bC^{\bp+\bq,\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bp,\lambda_{4}}\left(\bC^{\bk-\bq,\lambda_{2}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk,\lambda_{3}}$.

For simplicity, we can take spinors of the low-energy Bloch Hamiltonian for the vectors $\bC^{\bk,\lambda}$, if we assume that the exciton is localized around a specific point in the \bz.
There may be in general more than one of these points.
For instance, in the example of \gls{hbn} to which we will apply this formalism, there are the nonequivalent $K$ and $K'$ points of the \bz~where the bandgap is located.
As both valleys share the same value for the bandgap, the exciton states will have valley degeneracy.
That means we can solve the \gls{bse} focusing only on one of the valleys.
If that is not the case, then the valley degree of freedom should be accounted for as well.
We assume that the system can be well described by the bosonic $b$ operators, and that the numbers $\nu$ are good quantum numbers, in the sense that the Hamiltonian is diagonal in this basis, which allows us to write

\begin{equation}
    \Ha_X = \sum_{\nu} E_{\nu} b_{\nu}^{\dagger} b_{\nu}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we introduced the notation $\Ha_X$ for the Hamiltonian written in the basis of exciton states $\nu$.
Now the strategy is the following: we take the commutator $[\Ha_X, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]$ between $\Ha_X$ and the creation operator $b_{\nu}^{\dagger}$; next, we take the same commutator, but with the initial total Hamiltonian $\Ha$ instead (this step is the most involved one); finally, we equate both results and the Bethe--Salpeter equation is then automatically extracted.
The first commutator is straightforward:

\begin{equation}
    [\Ha_X, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = E_{\nu} b_\nu^{\dagger} = E_\nu \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_\nu(\bk) c_{\bk, c}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, v}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we, to write the second equality, explicitly wrote the bosonic creation operator in terms of electron--hole pairs.
Now with the total Hamiltonian $\Ha$ in terms of fermionic operators, we need to compute

\begin{equation}
[\Ha, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = [\Ho, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] + [\hat{V}, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent but we better work out both contributions separately.
First, the commutator with the kinetic component that after simplifying, evaluates to

\begin{equation}
    [\Ho, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = \sum_{\bk, \lambda} \epsilon_{\bk \lambda} \sum_{\bq} \frac{\phi_{\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{A}} \left [c_{\bk, \lambda}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, \lambda}, c_{\bq,c}^{\dagger} c_{\bq,v} \right ] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) (\epsilon_{\bk,c}-\epsilon_{\bk,v}) c_{\bk, c}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, v}\,.
\end{equation}

Now we have to do the commutator with the interacting term.
This task is not hard, just very tedious.
So we describe briefly by words key steps and present the final result.
For the commutator $\left[\hat{V}, b_\nu^{\dagger}\right]$, we have to compute the commutator between an operator quartic in $c$ with one quadratic in $c$; four contributions arise.
Each contribution is an operator quartic in $c$.
To make it comparable with the very first commutator, we need to introduce an approximation that turns the quartic operators into quadratic ones, which is the mean-field approximation.
It can also be called Hartree--Fock decoupling~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004}.
Applying this approximation to one of the initial contributions results in four terms, and each of these terms is the product between an operator quadratic in $c$ and an equilibrium ground state average of another quadratic operator in $c$.
To better clarify, we write this decoupling using mathematical symbols.
Assuming that the fermionic operators are written in a specific state basis $| i \rangle$, we write

\begin{equation}
    c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} c_{i_4} \approx c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} \langle c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_4} \rangle  + c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_4} \langle c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} \rangle - c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_4} \langle c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} \rangle - c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} \langle c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_4} \rangle\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent This approximation comes from admitting that an operator of the form $c^\dagger c$ exhibits small fluctuations around its average value $\langle c^\dagger c \rangle$, which is a thermal average computed at equilibrium (and accounting for fermion statistics when considering an operator quartic in $c$).
This is explained in Ref.~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004}.

We had four contributions, so in total we have sixteen terms.
In each of these sixteen terms, we have to evaluate an expectation value of the form $\langle c^\dagger c \rangle$.
These expectation values are simply equilibrium thermal averages, and at evaluated at zero temperature for simplicity\footnote{In the eventuality of the gapped material has a gap so low that it is considered a semiconductor, then considering $T = 0 $ K simplifies the analysis a lot, s it keeps the ground state considered at the beginning of this section.}.
Therefore, we can apply the rule

\begin{equation}
    \langle c_{\bk, \lambda_1}^{\dagger} c_{\bk', \lambda_2}  \rangle =
    \begin{cases}
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = c, \\
        \delta_{\bk,\bk'} \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = v, \\
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = c \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_2 = v, \\
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = v \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_2 = c.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent After that, we need to gather all the contributions, while recovering the sums in momentum to simplify the expression---equal terms simply add up or cancel.
One can also smartly use the properties of the form factor, changing the order in which the $\bC$ arrays appear.
To arrive at the final result, a couple of shifts in momentum might also be in order.
At the end, we can set $[\Ha_X, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = [\Ho, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] + [\hat{V}, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]$, from which we can extract the Bethe--Salpeter equation for the exciton wavefunction $\phi_\nu(\bk)$ and its respective energy $E_\nu$

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        E_\nu \phi_\nu(\bk)  =&   \phi_{\nu}(\bk) (\epsilon_{\bk,c}-\epsilon_{\bk,v}) \\
        & - \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V(\bk-\bp) F_{cvvc}(\bk,\bp,\bk-\bp)   \\
        & + \frac{1}{A} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) \sum_{\bp} V(\bp) \left[F_{vvvv}(\bp + \bk ,\bk,\bp) - F_{vcvc}(\bp + \bk,\bk,\bp) \right].
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

The first term on the \gls{rhs} is the gain in energy due to the transition of the system from the ground state to the excited state of an unbound electron--hole pair.
The second term accounts for the attractive interaction between the electron and the hole, making it possible for the complex to form a bound state.
We can interpret the last term as a renormalization of the single-particle bands when electron--electron interactions are accounted for, shifting the bands in energy, so it can be simply absorbed into the first term.
It does not mean that it can be ignored, but we sweep it under the rug this time.
Doing so, we arrive at the simplified form of the Bethe--Salpeter equation:

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{equation}
    E_\nu \phi_\nu(\bk) = (\epsilon_{\bk,c}-\epsilon_{\bk,v}) \phi_{\nu}(\bk) - \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \phi_\nu (\bp) V(\bk-\bp) F_{cvvc}(\bk,\bp,\bk-\bp)
    \label{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation}
\end{empheq}

\noindent The extension to multi-band systems is straightforward, but we do not need it.
The multi-band case is to be considered only in the next section and with a different formalism altogether.
We just make the remark that the negative sign going with the term in the potential is correct, and the electron--hole interaction is for sure attractive, since we use the convention of a positive interaction potential for $V(\bk)$.

In this section, we take the opportunity to derive the Wannier equation as in~\eqref{eq:Wannier_equation} from the \gls{bse}\@.
For that we need to introduce two approximations.
First, we make the parabolic dispersion approximation, that is

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\bk,\lambda} \simeq \epsilon_{\bzero,\lambda} + \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 m_{\lambda}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\lambda$ is a band index, $m_\lambda$ is the effective mass of an electron/hole in the band $\lambda$ and $\bk$ is measured from the point in the \bz~where the exciton is localized at.
It follows that

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\bk,c}-\epsilon_{\bk,v} \simeq E_g + \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 \mu},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $E_g =  \epsilon_{\bzero,c}-\epsilon_{\bzero,v}$ is the energy difference between the top and bottom of the valence and conduction bands, respectively, and $\mu$ is the electron--hole effective reduced mass as usual.
In the \gls{rhs} of~\eqref{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation}, we approximate $F \approx 1$, which is valid for large enough bandgaps~\cite{Ventura_2019}.
In this way, the equation~\eqref{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation} reads

\begin{equation}
    E_{b,\nu} \phi_{\nu} (\bk) = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 \mu} \phi_{\nu} (\bk) - \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V(\bk - \bp),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $ E_{b,\nu} = E_{\nu} - E_g$ is the binding energy of the excitonic state $\nu$.
Next, we apply a Fourier transform to the previous equation.
The term on the \gls{lhs} is direct, it becomes $ E_{b,\nu} \phi_{\nu} (\br)$.
The numerator in the first term on the \gls{rhs} in real space representation reads simply $p^2$ acting on the wavefunction $\phi_{\nu} (\br)$.
The second term on the \gls{rhs} is a convolution in momentum space, so it becomes a product in real space.
To see that, let us perform the inverse Fourier transform of just that term and write\footnote{Any details about Fourier trnasforming that are eventually skipped can be found in App.~\ref{app:Ventura_2019}.}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:potential_inverse_Fourier_transform}
\begin{aligned}
     &\frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bk} \ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br} \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V(\bk - \bp) = \frac{1}{A^2} \sum_{\bk} \sum_{\bp} \ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br} \ee^{-\ii \bp \vdot \br} \ee^{\ii \bp \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V(\bk - \bp) = \\
    =& \frac{1}{A^2} \sum_{\bp} \ee^{\ii \bp \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) \sum_{\bk} \ee^{\ii (\bk - \bp) \vdot \br} V(\bk - \bp) \overset{\bk \to \bk + \bp}{=} \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \ee^{\ii \bp \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) \frac{1}{A}  \sum_{\bk} \ee^{\ii (\bk) \vdot \br} V(\bk) = \\
    =& \phi_{\nu} (\br) V(\br)\,.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent In this way, we recover the Wannier equation

% \begin{equation}
%     \phi_{\nu} (\bk) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\br} \ei{ \bk \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\br)
% \end{equation}

% \noindent and to the potential

% \begin{equation}
%     V(\bk - \bp) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\br'} \ei{ (\bk - \bp) \vdot \br'} V (\br')
% \end{equation}

% \noindent and use the identity

% \begin{equation}
%     \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \ei{ \bp \vdot (\br - \br')} = \delta_{\br,\br'},
% \end{equation}

% \noindent to recover the Wannier equation:

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{equation}
    E_{b, \nu} \psi_\nu (\br) = \frac{p^2}{2 \mu} \psi_\nu (\br) - V(\br) \psi_\nu (\br)
    \label{eq:Wannier_equation_derived}
\end{empheq}

The excitonic problem is a differential equation in real space, and an integral equation in momentum space.
The real space approach is more useful for analytic treatments (or semi-analytical if we use the Rytova--Keldysh potential).
Unfortunately, it overlooks some features due to the approximations, like the lack of degeneracy between excitonic states with symmetric angular quantum numbers, a consequence of approximating $F$ to 1.
The momentum space approach is heavier numerically but produces more accurate results.
To solve the \gls{bse} as presented in this section, we have to do so numerically.
This formalism can be readily applied to isotropic systems, like \gls{hbn}.

Basically, one can separate the angular dependence of the wavefunction from the radial dependence, and in that way the problem can be cast into a \gls{1d} integral equation, that after discretizing the momentum is converted into an eigenvalue problem with only one momentum variable.
How to do so in practice is explained in detail in Refs.~\cite{Mauricio_2022} and~\cite{Henriques_Epstein_Peres_2022}, namely in their respective appendices.
In contrast with isotropic systems, for anisotropic systems one cannot separate the angular and radial variables, and one has then to construct a big matrix with two momentum variables.
But for such systems, the \gls{bse} formalism of the next section is more useful.
However, in Chapter~\ref{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice} we approach the anisotropic excitonic problem for nanostructured \gls{hbn} using the Wannier formalism---with this method we obtain interesting results with very low computational effort.

\section{The Bethe--Salpeter Equation: exact diagonalization}
\label{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_exact}

In this section, we dwell on a second-principles-based approach to the calculation of the excitons, within an atomistic description where the tight-binding approximation discussed previously is key.
The discussion is adapted from~\cite{Alex_PhD} and~\cite{Xatu}.
This approach presents technical and computational advantages to first-principles-based methodologies for excitons in extended systems, something pretty much established in the literature, such as the \GW-\bse~ method~\cite{Louie_2000,BerkeleyGW,Diana_PhD,Jornada_PhD}.
Even though more numerically inefficient as solving the Wannier equation or the\bse~applied to effective models, this method is still much more efficient as \abinit~ones, but nonetheless we can expect with it more detailed insights into the excitonic problem.
It can be useful if, for instance, when one is interested in bigger systems, or when the symmetries of the excitonic states are of relevance, which in principle are inherited from the symmetries of the crystal, something we cannot capture with effective models, since the symmetries of the initial Hamiltonian may not be preserved when taking the low-energy limit.

The starting point is to write down the electronic Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Hamiltonian_start}
    \Ha = \Ho + \V = \sum_{i,j} t_{i,j} \cdagger_i c_j + \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{i,j,k,l} \cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the indices include the relevant degrees of freedom (orbital site and orbital type), the first term corresponds to the interaction between the valence electrons and the ions (with frozen degrees of freedom), and the second term corresponds to the electrostatic Coulomb interaction between the electrons.
The single-particle physics is encoded in $\Ho$, to which all the techniques and results discussed in Section~\ref{sec:single_particle_properties} apply (and the ones within \rpa~from Section~\ref{sec:dielectric_function_quantum} as well).
In the Section~\ref{sec:single_particle_properties}, where we discussed single-particle properties of electrons in crystals,  particle--particle interactions were not completely discarded, in the sense that the electronic eigenstates--the Bloch states---that describe the ground state of the material, were previously obtained through self-consistent calculations (\gls{hf} or \gls{dft}), where interactions and correlations are treated at the mean-field
The many-particle physics, including excitons, is encoded in $\V$ without which excitons cannot be described, in concordance with the previous section.
Instead of departing from the many-body Hamiltonian in real space in terms of field operators, we depart from a Hamiltonian in real space already in an atomistic basis, i.e., we adopt the tight-binding description from the Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method} of the previous chapter.

Therefore, the sums in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hamiltonian_start} run over indices including orbitals and position degrees of freedom (and eventually spin).
The sums in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hamiltonian_start} run over indices including orbitals and position degrees of freedom (and eventually spin).
The parameters $t_{i,j}$ are the hopping or tight-binding parameters, while $V_{i,j,k,l}$ are the interaction matrix elements\footnote{Note that by matrix elements, we mean the symbols $V_{i,j,k,l}$, which are matrix elements of the first quantized interaction term $V$ between single-particle states belonging to the one-particle Hilbert space, and not matrix elements of the second quantized operator $\V$ between states living in the Fock space.}

\begin{equation}
    V_{i,j,k,l} = \mel{kl}{V}{ij} = \int \dr \int \dr' \phi^*_i(\br) \phi^*_j(\br') V(\br,\br') \phi_k(\br) \phi_l(\br'),
\end{equation}

\noindent At this point, we do not particularize how we model the screening, postponing that for later on.

\subsection{The Bethe--Salpeter equation rederived}

\todo{say somewhere that this approach is variational}

Now, with the purpose of describing excitons, we need to start from a good enough ground state $\ket{\GS}$.
For undoped \bd~semiconductors or insulators, whose ground state consists of all the valence bands filled and empty conduction bands, we can write mathematically for the ground state

\begin{equation}
    \ket{\GS} = \prod_{n,\bk}^{\epsilon_{n \bk} \leq \EF} c_{n \bk}^\dagger \ket{0} \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\ket{0}$ denotes the \enquote{vacuum} state (the crystal in the absence of electrons) and $\EF$ is the Fermi energy.\todo{define Fermi energy}
Exciting an electron from the valence band $v$ with momentum $\bk$ to the conduction band $c$ with momentum $\bk+\bQ$, we are before an electron--hole pair, whose state can be formally written as

\begin{equation}
    \ket{v,c,\bk,\bQ} \equiv \cdagger_{c, \bk+\bQ} c_{v, \bk} \ket{\GS}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent As a passing note, there is no need in our description for adopting a quasiparticle hole description for the holes.
We see it simply as the absence of a negative elementary charge in the Fermi sea.

If the exciton has a center of mass momentum $\bQ$, and assuming that a basis of electron--hole pairs is appropriate for the excitonic states, then we write for a generic exciton state $\ket{X_{\bQ}}$

\begin{equation}
    \ket{X_{\bQ}} = \sum_{v,c,\bk} A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \ket{v,c,\bk,\bQ} = \sum_{v,c,\bk} A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \cdagger_{c, \bk+\bQ} c_{v, \bk} \ket{\GS} \,.
\end{equation}

In this way, the exciton state is written as a linear combination of electron--hole pair states summing over valence bands, conduction bands and momentum.
We can consider the quantum number $\bQ$ a good one since the interaction conserves momentum, as all electron--hole pairs share the same center of mass momentum $\bQ$.
The expansion coefficients $A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk)$ are determined by minimization of the expectation value (omitting the $\bQ$ subscript momentarily) $\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}$ with respect to $\ket{X}$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expectation_value_exciton}
    \fdv{E[X]}{X} = \fdv{X}  \left[\frac{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{\braket{X}{X}}\right] = \fdv{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{X} \braket{X}{X} - \underbrace{ \frac{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{\braket{X}{X}}}_{E_X}  \fdv{\braket{X}{X}}{X} = 0 \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Upon performing the variational derivatives and defining $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) \equiv \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ha}{v',c',\bk',\bQ}$ for the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian between two electron--hole pair states, we arrive at the equation

\begin{equation}
   \sum_{v',c',\bk'} H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) A^{\bQ}_{v'c'}(\bk') = E_X A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which is a different way of writing the \bse, and the symbols $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)$ are matrix elements of what we call the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian.

The expansion of the excitonic state in a basis of electron--hole pairs is in fact an ansatz, or an assumption that the basis is adequate (or complete, technically speaking) to be able to write the true excitonic states.
Instead of considering all the possible combinations of electron--hole excitations, where several electrons would populate the conduction bands leaving as many holes behind in the valence bands, do the calculations with a reduced Fock space.
We admit only one electron at a time be excited from the valence band with momentum $\bk$ to a conduction band with momentum $\bk + \bQ$.
We explained this in layperson terms, but we could have made the discussion more rigorous.
However, that can be already found in the literature~\cite{Xatu,Alex_PhD}.

We need to compute the matrix elements $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)$.
Naturally, it has to be done numerically in practice, which also means that we have to truncate the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian and work with an approximate finite matrix representation.
Physically speaking, this approximation means that we are considering only a finite number of valence and conduction bands, and a finite number of momentum points in the \bz~(or unit cells, equivalently).
Mathematically, it means that we are working with a truncated basis for $\Ho$, which in practice is not complete.
In other words, we have to compute the matrix elements for each pair of momentum points $(\bk,\bk')$ in the \bz~mesh, for each pair $(vc,v'c')$ of $(vc)$ pairs of valence and conduction bands.
The results later on will have to be converged against the number of points in the \bz~and the number of valence and conduction bands included to get the expected degeneracies and symmetries.
Naturally, we select only the valence and conduction bands more relevant for the excitons, those closer to the Fermi level $\EF$.

Even if they are to be evaluated numerically at the end, let us see how far we can go with only analytical expressions.
The Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian matrix elements are given by $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ha}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ho}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} + \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ}$, matrix elements between states of uncoupled electron--hole pairs.
The first term corresponds to the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian at the mean-field level between two electron--hole pair states, while the second term corresponds to the interaction matrix elements.
We work out first the matrix elements of $\Ho$, as they are easier to deal with.
For that, let us assume that the operator $\Ho$ can be written in a spectral representation of Bloch states labeled by $\ket{n,\bk}$.
Then, in its second-quantized form, the single-particle Hamiltonian reads

\begin{equation}
   \Ho = \sum_{n,\bk} \epsilon_{n \bk} \cdagger_{n \bk} c_{n \bk}\,,
\label{eq:Hamiltonian_single_particle}
\end{equation}

\noindent assuming that the process of diagonalizing it has been effectuated.
Replacing the expression above in the matrix element of $\Ho$, it is not hard to show that

\begin{equation}
    \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ho}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = \qty(\epsilon_{c, \bk + \bQ} - \epsilon_{v, \bk}) \delta_{v,v'} \delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{c,c'} \qq{up to a global shift of}\EF \,.
\end{equation}

The interaction matrix elements are tricky to compute, as the interaction operator $\V$ is a two-body operator, quartic in the creation and annihilation operators.
Nonetheless, in this section we refrain from performing any kind of mean-field approximation and compute the matrix elements exactly.
Doing so is not hard, but it is, once again, very tedious.
We do not perform all the steps and just explain briefly how to proceed.
For convenience, we make a brief change and abuse of notation, and relabel as $(v',\bk') \to v'$, $(c',\bk' + \bQ) \to c'$, $(v,\bk) \to v$ and $(c,\bk + \bQ) \to c$, and keep the notation for $\V$ used above.
Then we have

\begin{align*}
    \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} &= \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{i,j,k,l} \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = \\
    &= \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{i,j,k,l} \mel{\GS}{\cdagger_{v} c_c \cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k \cdagger_{v'} c_{c'}}{\GS}\,.
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element}
\end{align*}

\noindent We pick, for instance, the pair $c_c \cdagger_i$ and use the anti-commutation relations to move the creation operator to the left, and the annihilation operator to the right, giving rise to two new terms.
Then, in each term, we select a new pair of creation and annihilation operators $\cdagger c$ and repeat the process, making new terms appear.
Following this procedure recursively, until we exhaust all our options for pairs of creation and annihilation operators, and end up only with a matrix element of a single $\cdagger c$ pair to evaluate, we arrive at

\begin{align*}
    \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = &V_{c'vv'c} - V_{c'vcv'} + \Kd{v v'} \sum_j \qty(V_{c'jcj} - V_{c'jjc}) + \Kd{c c'} \sum_j \qty(V_{vjjv'} - V_{vjv'j}) + \\
    &+ \Kd{v v'} \Kd{c c'} \frac{1}{2} \sum_{ij} \qty(V_{ijij} - V_{ijji})\,.
\end{align*}

\noindent Summing the single-particle and two-particle contributions, we have for the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian matrix elements

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = &\delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{v,v'} \qty[\epsilon_{c, \bk + \bQ}  \delta_{c,c'} + \Sigma_{c c'}(\bk + \bQ, \bk' + \bQ)] - \\
        &-\delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{c,c'} \qty[\epsilon_{v, \bk}  \delta_{v,v'} + \Sigma_{v' v}(\bk + \bQ, \bk' + \bQ)] - \\
        &-(D - X)_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)\,,
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:BSE_Hamiltonian_matrix_elements}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have defined the direct and exchange interaction matrix elements, as

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:direct_and_exchange_terms}
    \begin{align}
        D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= V_{c\bk + \bQ,v'\bk', c'\bk' + \bQ, v\bk} \label{subeq:direct_term} \qq{and}\\
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= V_{c\bk + \bQ,v'\bk', v\bk, c'\bk' + \bQ}\,, \label{sub eq:exchange_term}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent respectively and the self-energy, accounting for the interaction between the electron (or hole) with the Fermi sea, as

\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{nm} (\bk,\bk') = \sum_{j,\bk''}^{\text{occ}}\qty(V_{n\bk,j\bk'',m\bk',j\bk''} - V_{n\bk,j\bk'',j\bk'',m\bk'})\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the sum is only over occupied bands.
About the self-energy, we have to make a technical remark.
As a sanity check, it is instructive to look closely at~\eqref{eq:BSE_Hamiltonian_matrix_elements}, and check for hermiticity by replacing $v \leftrightarrow v'$, $c \leftrightarrow c'$ and $\bk \leftrightarrow \bk'$.
If we do so, we arrive at the conclusion that the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian is not hermitian, with the self-energy terms being the culprits.
We can avoid this problem if we work at the mean-field level, using a single Slater determinant for the \gs~ of~\eqref{eq:Hamiltonian_start}, or equivalently, making a \hf~approximation.
We can then see the self-energy corrections as incorporated in the \qp~energies $\epsilon_{n \bk}$, and without any worries assume that any quasiparticle energy corrections have already been performed \textit{a priori} through in the diagonalization of the single-particle Hamiltonian by \abinit~methods.
In this way, the Bethe--Salpeter equation reads


\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{equation}
   \qty(\epsilon_{c, \bk + \bQ} - \epsilon_{v, \bk}) A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) + \sum_{v',c',\bk'} K_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) A^{\bQ}_{v'c'}(\bk') = E_X A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk)
    \label{eq:exact_Bethe_Salpeter_equation}
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the energies $\{\epsilon_{n, \bk}\}$ are now \hf~\qp~energies, and where we have defined the interaction kernel as $K = -\qty(D - X)$.
This approximation in the \bse~is commonly called in the literature the Tamm--Dancoff approximation~\cite{Dreuw_2005,Hirata_1999}, avoiding any complex diagonalization procedures.
We would also like to note that the Tamm--Dancoff approximation was for the first time introduced in the context of \mbpt, where a similar issue for the \bse~arises.
Nonetheless, there are two striking differences between the \bse~ we solve and the one obtained with \mbpt~\cite{Jornada_PhD,Diana_PhD}.
In the latter, the problem has a frequency dependence that we do not find here.
The screening is dynamic, and that entails performing an integral in frequencies to compute the interaction matrix elements.
Here we model the screening using a static dielectric function, so we work always in the static limit $\omega \to 0$.
On one hand, this approximation is also common in \abinit~calculations in practice, although in that context it has to be justified.
On the other hand, the general case $\omega \neq 0$ hinders heavily the numerical calculations.
The other difference we can point out is that within \mbpt~, the exchange term is a matrix element of the \textbf{bare} Coulomb potential, while only the direct term is a matrix element of the \textbf{screened} Coulomb potential.
In our case, both direct and exchange terms are matrix elements of the same Coulomb potential, and there is no reason for the exchange term to involve the bare Coulomb potential (it is usually discarded either way).

We have not hitherto specified the form of the Coulomb potential, even though screening was a key concept in previous chapters.
We shall discuss that right in the next section, where we explain how we can compute the interaction matrix elements in the Bethe--Salpeter equation.


\subsection{The interaction matrix elements and screening}
\label{subsec:interaction_matrix_elements_and_screening}

Let us first remind the reader that we use Bloch states of the form

\begin{equation}
    \psi_{n \bk} (\br) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} \sum_{i \alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \phi_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_i)\,,
\label{eq:Bloch_states_again}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the tight-binding coefficients are determined by diagonalizing the Bloch Hamiltonian (as explained in Sec.~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}), and the atomic orbitals denoted by $\phi_\alpha$ are orthogonal, as we work under the tight-binding formalism\footnote{They in general may not be orthogonal. If the model Hamiltonian has been obtained by a Wannier interpolation, that they are orthogonal by construction. However, if the Hamiltonian comes from calculations on a basis of localized functions other than Wannier functions, then they are not orthogonal in general. This is not a problem at the end, because one can always follow an orthogonalization procedure. The code we work with does just that. We shall remind the reader again once it comes the time of discussing the code developed.}.
Focusing first on the direct interaction term, per~\eqref{subeq:direct_term}, in terms of Bloch states is

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:direct_term_real_space_formula}
    D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') W(\br,\br') \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v,\bk}(\br')\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have denoted now the screened potential by $W$ instead of $V$.
Upon replacing the Bloch states by their expression~\eqref{eq:Bloch_states_again}, at some point we need to evaluate four-body integrals of the form

\begin{equation}
    \int \dr \int \dr' \phi^*_{\alpha} (\br) \phi^*_{\beta} (\br') W(\br,\br') \phi_{\alpha'} (\br) \phi_{\beta'} (\br')\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent with the position vectors as arguments of the orbitals may have a shift in position of the form $\br \to \br - \bR - \bt_i$.
In \abinit~calculations......
Considering point-like orbitals where their spatial structure is overlooked and assuming orthogonality, whenever a product between orbitals appears, we can make the substitution

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_{i}) \phi_{\beta} (\br - \bR' - \bt_j) \approx \delta_{\alpha \beta} \delta_{\bR \bR'} \delta_{ij} \delta(\br - \bR - \bt_i)\,
\end{equation}

\noindent This allows for performing the space integrals directly and analytically, so that we obtain a relatively simple expression for the direct term:

\begin{equation}
    D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \alpha}  \sum_{j \beta} \qty(C_{i \alpha}^{c \bk+ \bQ})^* C_{i \alpha}^{c' \bk' + \bQ} \qty(C_{j \beta}^{v' \bk'})^* C_{j \beta}^{v \bk} V_{ij}(\bk' - \bk)\,,
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_real_space}
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    V_{ij}(\bk' - \bk) = \sum_{\bR} \ei{\qty(\bk' - \bk) \vdot \bR} W\qty(\bR + \bt_i; \bt_j)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which \textbf{is not} a Fourier transform.
If we model the screening using the Rytova--Keldysh potential, then we can further write $W\qty(\bR + \bt_i; \bt_j) = V_\RK (\bR + \bt_i - \bt_j)$: the Rytova--Keldysh model is classical, the dielectric function is assumed to be a single momentum function, implying one has assumed continuous translation invariance, which is clearly an approximation.
To go beyond classical models for screening and take into account the inhomogeneity of the crystal, we can develop the expression~\eqref{eq:direct_term_real_space_formula} by Fourier transforming the screened Coulomb potential, and write

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal}
        &D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \\
        &\int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') \left(\frac{1}{A^2} \sum_{\bq,\bq'} \ei{\bq \vdot \br} W(\bq,\bq')\ee^{-\ii \bq' \vdot \br'}\right) \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v,\bk}(\br') = \\
        &=\int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') \left(\frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bq \in \mathrm{BZ}} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \ei{(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br} W(\bq + \bG,\bq + \bG')\ee^{-\ii (\bq + \bG') \vdot \br'}\right) \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v,\bk}(\br') = \\
        &= \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bq \in \mathrm{BZ}} \sum_{\bG,\bG'}
        \qty(\int \dr  \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \ei{(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br} \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br))  W_{\bG \bG'}(\bq) \qty(\int \dr' \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') \ee^{-\ii (\bq + \bG') \vdot \br'}  \psi^*_{v,\bk}(\br')) = \\
        &= \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bq \in \mathrm{BZ}} \delta_{\bq,\bk - \bk'} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \bra{c,\bk+\bQ} \ei{(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br} \ket{c',\bk'+\bQ} W_{\bG \bG'}(\bq) \bra{v',\bk'} \ee^{-\ii (\bq + \bG') \vdot \br} \ket{v,\bk} = \\
        &=  \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \qty(I^{\bG}_{c \bk+\bQ, c' \bk'+\bQ})^* W_{\bG \bG'}(\bk - \bk') I^{\bG'}_{v \bk, v' \bk'}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we introduced the inverse Fourier transform for the screened potential (consult App.~\ref{app:fourier_transform}), and introduced the definition

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:plane_wave_matrix_element_definition}
    I^{\bG}_{n \bk, n' \bk'} = \int \dr\, \psi^*_{n,\bk} (\br) \ee^{-\ii(\bk  - \bk' + \bG) \vdot \br} \psi_{n' \bk'} (\br)\,.
\end{equation}

The final expression in equation~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal} is the one usually encountered in the literature pertaining to first-principle methods~\cite{BerkeleyGW,Yambo_2009}.
These methods allow also computing the dielectric matrix and its inverse, so that one can calculate the matrix elements of the screened Coulomb potential through $W_{\bG\bG'}(\bq) = \vareps^{-1}_{\bG\bG'}(\bq) v_c(\bq + \bG')$, the static limit of Eq.~\eqref{eq:screened_dynamic_Coulomb_potential}.

Regarding the exchange term, even though we typically will discard, for completion we present its expression, that is

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_exchange_reciprocal}
    \begin{split}
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \alpha}  \sum_{j \beta} \qty(C_{i \alpha}^{c \bk+ \bQ})^*   C_{i \alpha}^{v \bk} C_{j \beta}^{c' \bk' + \bQ} \qty(C_{j \beta}^{v' \bk'})^* V_{ij}(\bQ)  \\
                                  &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \qty(I^{\bG}_{c \bk+\bQ, v \bk})^* W_{\bG \bG'}(\bQ) I^{\bG'}_{c' \bk' + \bQ, v' \bk'}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

If instead we use for the screened Coulomb potential the Rytova--Keldysh model, but work in reciprocal space, then the expressions for the direct and exchange terms write

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_elements_reciprocal_RK}
    \begin{align}
        D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bG} I^{\bG}_{c \bk+\bQ, c' \bk'+\bQ} V_{\text{RK}}(\bk - \bk' + \bG) (I^{\bG}_{v \bk, v' \bk'})^* \label{eq:direct_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK} \\
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bG} I^{\bG}_{c \bk+\bQ, v \bk} V_{\text{RK}}(\bQ + \bG) (I^{\bG}_{c' \bk' + \bQ, v' \bk'})^* \,, \label{eq:exchange_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent where $V_{\text{RK}}(\bq) = \frac{e}{2\epsz q\left(1+r_0 q\right)}$ is the momentum-resolved Rytova--Keldysh potential, encountered in this work for the first time in the Section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit} of the very first chapter of this thesis.
A striking difference between the expressions~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_elements_reciprocal_RK} and~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal} and~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_exchange_reciprocal}, is that in the latter there is a double sum over reciprocal lattice vectors $\bG$ and $\bG'$, while in the former there is only a single sum over reciprocal lattice vectors.
The double sum allows for including non-local field effects in the interaction matrix elements.
In the single sum, the inclusion of the reciprocal lattice vectors has the purpose of preserving the symmetry in the interaction between electrons. % with momenta $\bk$ and $\bk'$~\cite{Ridolfi_2015}---the \bd~Fourier transform of the Rytova--Keldysh potential from the reciprocal space to the real space must involve a sum over all momenta in the reciprocal lattice to recover the correct $\br$ dependence, and the sum over all momenta can be decomposed into a sum over momenta in the \bz~with a sum over all reciprocal lattice vectors, so that the entire reciprocal lattice is covered.

\subsection{Noninteracting optical conductivity}
\label{subsec:non_interacting_conductivity}


We could follow a more technical strategy to derive the expression for the optical conductivity as in Refs~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004,Mahan}, but since we have at our disposal the dielectric function, we will simply use it instead.
We start from the dynamical longitudinal dielectric function as per~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency}, that we recall here:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \varepsilon_{\bG\bG'}(\bq,\omega) &= \delta_{\bG\bG'}-v_{c}(\bq+\bG)\chi_{\bG\bG'}^{0}(\bq,\omega)=\\
        &=\delta_{\bG\bG'}-\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}|\bq+\bG|^{2}V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk+\bq})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk+\bq}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii \eta} \times \\
        &\times \langle n,\bk| \ei[-]{(\bq+\bG)\vdot\br} |n',\bk+\bq\rangle\langle n',\bk+\bq|\ei{(\bq+\bG')\vdot\br}|n,\bk\rangle
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we used the \gls{3d} Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential, and recovered the square in the electron charge for convenience, while $V$ is the volume of the system, assumed to be \gls{3d}\@.
Taking the local limit $\bq\to\bzero$ in the $\bG=\bG'=\bzero$ matrix element, we get

    &=1-\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0} V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii \eta}\left|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\br\,|n',\bk\rangle\right|^2
\end{align}


\noindent To arrive at the previous expression, we expanded the exponential to first order in $\bq$.
Assuming that the wavelength is so large that we can expand the exponential and keep only the first contributing term is called the dipole approximation\footnote{The optical transition is induced by the dipole operator $-e \br$, hence the name of the approximation.}.
Using relation $\varepsilon=1+\ii\frac{\sigma}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}}$, mentioned before for the first time in Chapter one as Eq.~\eqref{eq:equivalent_permittivity}, we find

\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \sigma(\omega)=-\ii\omega\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon-1)=\\
        =\ii\frac{\omega e^{2}}{V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii \eta}\left|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\br\,|n',\bk\rangle\right|^2=\\
        =\ii\frac{\omega e^{2}}{V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii \eta}\frac{\left|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\left[H,\br\right]|n',\bk\rangle\right|{{}^2}}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}}=\\
        =\frac{\ii e^{2}\hbar}{V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\hbar\omega\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii \eta}\frac{\left|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\bp/m|n',\bk\rangle\right|{{}^2}}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}}=\\
        =\frac{\ii\hbar}{V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\hbar\omega\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii \eta}\frac{\left|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\mathbf{j}\,|n',\bk\rangle\right|{{}^2}}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}}
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\noindent  where we have used the identities $\bp=\ii m/\hbar\left[H,\br\right]$ and $\mathbf{j}=-e\mathbf{v}=-e\bp/m$\footnote{We advert here the reader for an important detail. In general, the position, velocity and momentum operators are not in general interchangeable when doing calculations for extended systems. In the context of atomic physics, there is no issue at all. The same cannot be said about infinite crystalline solids. With the intention of not interrupting the flow of the text, we postpone a more detailed discussion.}.
We also introduce the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\bq}=\bq/q$ to denote the orientation of the wave vector $\bq$.
We can define a tensor like conductivity $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$ as

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\frac{\ii\hbar}{V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\hbar\omega\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii \eta}\frac{\langle n,\bk|j_{\alpha}|n',\bk\rangle\langle n',\bk|j_{\beta}|n,\bk\rangle}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}}
\end{equation}

\noindent Using the Sokhotski--Plemelj identity

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sokhotski_plemelj}
    \frac{1}{x\pm \ii \eta}=\mathrm{P.V.}\!\left(\frac{1}{x-a}\right) \mp \ii\pi\delta(x-a),\quad \eta=0^+,
\end{equation}

\noindent we obtain the so-called Kubo--Greenwood formula for the real part of the conductivity reading

\begin{equation}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi e^{2}\hbar}{V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}}  v^{\alpha}_{n n'}(\bk)  v^{\beta}_{n' n}(\bk) \delta(\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk}))
\end{equation}

\noindent if written in terms of the velocity operator matrix elements, where we used the trick of replacing $\omega \to \epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}$ to cancel the frequency in the numerator, which is allowed due to the presence of the Dirac delta function, and defined $v^{\alpha}_{n n'}(\bk) = \langle n,\bk|v_{\alpha}|n',\bk\rangle$.
The Kubo--Greenwood formula for the conductivity is actually a particular case of the more general Kubo formula, which accounts for all possible state transitions among all many-body states of the system~\cite{Mahan,Coleman_2015}.
Being in practice impossible to calculate at the full interacting level, normally some sort of approximation is needed.
THe Kubo--Greenwood formula is the Kubo formula without interactions, accounting only for transitions between single-particle states---this is called the \gls{ipa}~\cite{Aversa_Sipe_1995}.
Particularizing for a semiconductor at zero temperature and simplifying, we are allowed to write

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:non_interacting_Re_optical_conductivity}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi e^{2}\hbar}{V}\sum_{vc,\bk} \frac{v^{\alpha}_{c v}(\bk)  v^{\beta}_{v c}(\bk)}{\epsilon_{c,\bk}-\epsilon_{v,\bk}}   \delta[\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{c,\bk}-\epsilon_{v,\bk})]
\end{empheq}

\noindent At this point we will not explain how to compute the position or velocity matrix elements in practice.
They will be addressed in two different ways in chapters~\ref{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice} and~\ref{ch:screening}, so we postpone the explanation to those two chapters.


\subsection{Excitonic optical conductivity}
\label{subsec:excitonic_conductivity}

In this section we are concerned with the contribution of excitons to the optical conductivity.
If the material is subject to a drive from a uniform time-dependent electric field composed of a single harmonic $\omega$, the expression for the optical conductivity up to leading order is given by~\cite{Pedersen_2015}


\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{align}
    \sigma (\omega) = \frac{e^2}{8 \pi^3 \ii \hbar} \sum_{\nu} \qty[E_{\nu} \frac{\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} \vdot \vectorbold{\Omega}^*_{\nu} }{E_{\nu} - \hbar \omega + \ii \hbar \Gamma} + E_{\nu} \frac{\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} \vdot \vectorbold{\Omega}^*_{\nu} }{E_{\nu} + \hbar \omega - \ii \hbar \Gamma}]
    \label{eq:optical_conductivity_def}
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the sum is over the excitonic states, $E_{\nu}$ being the energy of the $\nu$-th state, and $\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu}$ is the Berry curvature given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Berry_curvature_definition}
    \vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} = \sum_{cv,\bk} \psi^{\nu}_{c,v} (\bk) \langle v, \bk | \br \vdot \hat{\vectorbold{e}} | c, \bk \rangle,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\br$ is the dipole operator, $\hat{\vectorbold{e}}$ is the polarization unit vector and $\psi^{\nu}_{c,v} (\bk)$ is the excitonic wavefunction for the pair of bands $(c,v)$.
If once more we wish to define the optical conductivity as a tensor, then we can deduct an expression similar to~\eqref{eq:non_interacting_Re_optical_conductivity}, we just have to replace the transition energy $\epsilon_{c,\bk}-\epsilon_{v,\bk}$ by the exciton energy $E_{\nu}$, and the velocity matrix elements by their interacting version given by a weighted sum over momentum of the noninteracting matrix elements, with the weight coinciding with the excitons wavefunction, similarly to what is written in~\eqref{eq:Berry_curvature_definition}.
