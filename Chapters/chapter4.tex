\chapter{Excitonic effects in Two-dimensional Semiconductors}
\label{ch:Excitons}
\glsresetall
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In previous chapters, we have discussed how a classical description of electrodynamics in matter, together with a quantum description of its properties, we can describe different kinds of phenomena in solids.
The tools of quantum mechanics provide means of calculating the dielectric function, and as we will see, also the conductivity.
Given their equivalence established in the first chapter, knowing the dielectric function is sufficient to describe electrodynamic effects, such as dispersion, bulk plasmons in Drude metals, Ohmic losses and material absorption---the last two more directly through the conductivity.
The fact that the classical description of plasmons, absorption, and reflection in metals via Maxwell's equations and empiric dielectric functions is so successful, although it might seem surprising, can now be understood.

At the light of our quantum description of matter, we have seen that gapped materials can be identified with insulators and semiconductors, poor conducting materials.
This is so because the valence bands in such materials are filled, requiring large amounts of energy to excite electrons to the conduction band such that there is a nonvanishing current~\cite{Kittel_2004,Simon_Oxford_2013}.
In contrast, metals are good conductors because the highest valence band is partially filled.
This means that it only requires small amounts of energy to excite the electrons so that they can more easily participate in the charge flow.
In other words, in gapped undoped materials and at low temperatures, the system can only be excited from the ground state via energetically costing interband transitions, whereas metals can be excited via energetically efficient intraband transitions.
However, if the energies involved are high enough, a metal can experience interband transitions, which bring a contribution to the optical response that the Drude approach cannot capture.
Such an example is gold~\cite{Christy_1972}, which displays a Drude-like optical conductivity until a certain frequency threshold, while above that frequency the optical measurements deviate from the classical model highlighting its shortcomings.
Ad hoc corrections to the Drude model can be made~\cite{Meyer_2006}, but a complete picture is still missing.
One rapidly sees that classical models cannot explain all phenomena in solids, in particular phenomena observed in optical spectroscopy experiments detecting quantum effects with no classical analogue.

Within quantum mechanics, we can quantify the material responses that stem from interband transitions not accounted for in classical models, but that are naturally incorporated in the microscopic dielectric function through the polarizability in Eq.~\eqref{eq:polarizability_momentum_frequency}, which in fact includes both interband and intraband transitions in a single expression.
Only in this way can we expect to describe the dielectric properties of insulators and semiconductors.
However, the expression for the dielectric function obtained in the previous chapter was written in terms of a noninteracting polarizability $\chi^0$, that accounts solely for single-particle transitions.
An optical experiment, for instance a photoluminescence or optical absorption one, for frequencies above the bandgap would measure an optical response due to the excitation of electrons from the valence bands to the conduction bands when they absorb a photon.
Then, in principle, the same experiment would not detect any response when tuning the frequency to sub-gap energies.
However, that is not what happens.
One photoluminescence experiment has observed an absorption resonance for monolayer \gls{hbn} at an energy lying inside the electronic bandgap~\cite{Cassabois_2019}.
This observation can only be explained by taking into account the electron--electron interactions beyond the mean-field level, which is an effective single-particle description.
The two-body interactions can promote a quantum state that we could not predict from the machinery introduced so far---that is called a many-body state.

Up until now, we have been stressing that the full interacting response of condensed matter systems cannot be computed in practice, which is true.
That is why, in a first instance, we kept the discussion within the independent-particle picture which we can compute in practice, with the full interacting one being out of our capacity, even if in experiments we see everything that is in play.
Nonetheless, we do not need the full interacting picture to explain the experimental observations.
We just need to account for the Coulomb interaction between the electron that was excited to the conduction band, and the absence of the negative charge that it left behind in the valence band---the positively charged hole.
This interaction between the electron and the hole, just like between the proton and the electron in the hydrogen atom, can be so strong that the electron--hole pair form a bound state---the exciton~\cite{Datta_Marie_2024}.
The situation is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation} on the left, where the electron, depicted as the red filled circle, is in the conduction band, while the hole, depicted as the blue open circle, is in the valence band.
Due to Coulomb attraction, extra states inside the bandgap can form, as represented in Figure~\ref{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation} on the right.
The material can be excited from its ground state to one of several excitonic states represented in the figure by a series of discrete energy levels, if the energy of the photon coincides with one of those energies.
The minimum amount of energy required for that to happen is what defines the \textit{optical} bandgap, coinciding with the energy of the \textit{excitonic} ground state.
We can define the binding energy $E_{b, \nu}$ of a given exciton in a state $\nu$, depicted in the schematic on the right in Fig~\ref{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation}, as the difference between the energy of the electronic bandgap $E_g$ and the energy of the exciton $E_{\nu}$.
Just like the ionization energy in the hydrogen atom, it can be interpreted as the energy that we have to provide to separate the electron from the hole, resulting in their separation into an unpaired electron--hole pair, followed by the decay of the electron and its recombination with the hole.

These facts do not imply that all the techniques introduced until now in the last two chapters are not useful; actually the opposite.
As we will see, getting the Coulomb attraction right in a solid is essential in order to compute the excitons correctly.
We mean that we need to take screening into account by using an appropriate dielectric function.
Screening effects at the noninteracting level can actually describe quite well interactions in a solid constituting the base of the calculations to determine the excitons.
As we shall explain in a moment, this is crucial in \bd~materials.
Before getting too technical, let us first try to draw some conclusions and gain intuition on the problem before we appropriately tackle it.

As we shall see in this chapter, the excitonic problem that consists of finding the excitonic eigenenergies and eigenstates (or wavefunctions) can be quite intricate.
Nonetheless, we have argued that determining the excitonic properties of a material is key to describe their optoelectronic properties, in particular those related to the sub-bandgap physics of gapped materials.
At the optical frequencies considered in this work, no other properties come into play, such as phonon or magnetic excitations.
Herein, we only model the electronic and optical properties of \bd~materials, and how these relate with each other.
There are several approaches to the exciton problem, and in this chapter we provide a couple of them, going up in difficulty and complexity.

Within a very crude approximation, the problem of bound electron--hole complexes can be identified with the hydrogen-atom problem, if the energy surfaces for the electron and the hole are spherical and nondegenerate~\cite{Kittel_2004}.
From a heuristic point of view, this implies that the binding energies are given by the modified Rydberg formula

\begin{equation}
    E_{b,n} = - \frac{R^*}{n^2}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Rydberg_modified}
    R^* = \frac{2 \mu_{eh}^2 e^4}{\hbar^2 (8 \pi \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}})^2}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the modified Rydberg constant which, instead of using the electron mass, it uses the reduced mass between the electron and hole effective masses

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\mu_{eh}} = \frac{1}{m_e} + \frac{1}{m_h}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Here, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}$ is just the dielectric constant of the medium.
This description is suited for weakly bound excitons, also-called Wannier excitons~\cite{Koch_2009}, originally proposed by Wannier~\cite{Wannier_1937} and Mott~\cite{Mott_Littleton_1938,Mott_1938_II,Mott_1938_III}.
The characteristic size of the exciton is given by the modified expression for the Bohr radius

\begin{equation}
    a_B^* = \frac{4 \pi \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}} \varepsilon_0 \hbar^2}{\mu_{eh}^2 e^2}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which can extend over a couple of atomic sites.
If the exciton is tightly bound, then it is called a Frenkel exciton~\cite{Frenkel_1931_I,Frenkel_1931_II,Davydov_1971}.
In this case, the excitation is localized at a single atom.
Wannier excitons can form in inorganic semiconductors, like cuprous oxide, \ch{Cu2O}, gallium arsenide, \ch{GaAs}, or krypton, \ch{Kr}.
For \ch{GaAs}, the binding energy of the ground state exciton is $4.2$ \unit{\milli\electronvolt}~\cite{GaAs_exciton_1976}, whereas in solid krypton the ground-state excitonic binding energy is around $1.75$ \unit{\electronvolt}~\cite{Laporte_1985}.
On the contrary, Frenkel excitons are more common in ionic crystals~\cite{Knox_Inchauspe_1959}, such as \ch{CuCl} or \ch{NaCl}, or in organic molecular crystals~\cite{Nematiaram_Padula_Troisi_2021,Silinsh_1980}.
A recent theoretical work~\cite{Victor_Nuno_2025} predicted that when two materials, one hosting a Frenkel exciton and another hosting a Wannier exciton, are in close contact, the Frenkel exciton can hybridize with the Wannier exciton to form a Wannier--Mott--Frenkel exciton.

If we wish to apply the description above of Wannier excitons to \bd~materials, the excitonic binding energies are given now by the modified Rydberg formula for the \bd~hydrogen atom~\cite{2D_Hydrogen_atom_1991}

\begin{equation}
    E^{\BD}_{b,n} = - \frac{R^*}{\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^2}\,.
\end{equation}

Focusing on the ground state $n=1$, we can see that $E^{\BD}_{b,n=1} = 4 E_{b,n=1}$.
Furthermore, the \bd~Bohr radius is half of the \gls{3d} one~\cite{2D_Hydrogen_atom_1991}.
We can then predict that the exciton contribution to the optical spectrum is more prominent in \bd~materials when compared to \gls{3d} ones.
This is a consequence of the reduced dimensionality, deeming the screening of the electrostatic Coulomb interaction in \bd~materials less effective than in \gls{3d} ones~\cite{Cudazzo_2011}.
In \bd~materials, excitons can form even at room temperatures~\cite{Morozov_Wolff_Mortensen_2021}.
Therefore, the hydrogen-atom picture for the excitons does not apply to \bd~materials, as the binding energies do not follow the hydrogenic Rydberg series, and a modified Rydberg constant as per Eq.~\eqref{eq:Rydberg_modified} with a dielectric constant is not valid.
As we shall see later on, mainly in Chapter~\ref{ch:screening}, the dielectric function in \bd~gapped materials is strongly non-local.
This implies that we cannot speak of a single dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}$ as a relative permittivity, and writing for the screened potential $W(r) = v_c(r)/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}}$ is not sufficient.
Actually, it has even been seen experimentally, at least for \ch{WS2}, that the series of excitonic energies is nonhydrogenic~\cite{Chernikov_2014}, and this behavior is expected to be universal among \bd~insulators.
The \bd~material \ch{WS2} belongs to a class of semiconducting materials called \glspl{tmd}~\cite{Manzeli_Ovchinnikov_Pasquier_Yazyev_Kis_2017}, which have by chemical formula \ch{MX2}, where \ch{M} is a transition metal, such as molybdenum (\ch{Mo}) or wolfram (\ch{W}), and \ch{X} is a chalcogen, such as sulfur (\ch{S}) or selenium (\ch{Se}).
In bulk, they are layered materials and present an indirect bandgap, while in their monolayer limit the bandgap is direct.
Among them, there is molybdenum disulfide~\cite{Singh_Kumar_Late_Kumar_Patel_Singh_2018}, with chemical formula \ch{MoS2}, a prototypical \bd~material for studying excitons that we shall encounter later on in this work.

In order to explain the peculiarities of the excitons in two dimensions, we can as a first attempt adopt the hydrogen-atom model, but with a modified potential.
The excitonic states and respective binding energies can be estimated by solving the Wannier--Mott equation~\cite{Wannier_1937}, or simply Wannier equation, that reads

\begin{equation}
    E_{b, \nu} \psi_\nu (\br) =  \frac{p ^2}{2 \mu_{eh}} \psi_\nu (\br) - V(\br) \psi_\nu (\br),
\label{eq:Wannier_equation}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu_{eh}$ is the electron--hole reduced effective mass as before, and $V(\br)$ is the attractive potential.
One can notice similarities with the \gls{tise} for the hydrogen atom, but with an effective reduced mass (between two particles that have a comparable mass), and an attractive potential that includes now screening effects from the material itself.
We have seen before, in the Section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit}, that for \bd~materials the dielectric function in the static limit has a nontrivial momentum-dependence.
It is linear in $q$ as $\varepsilon(q) = 1 + r_0 q$, and we called this permittivity with the model parameter $r_0$ the Rytova--Keldysh dielectric function, giving origin to the Rytova--Keldysh potential~\cite{keldysh2024coulomb}.

The Wannier equation does not capture the full structure of the excitonic states (like nondegenerate $p$ states or the symmetries of the crystal), hence we need to resort to more accurate descriptions of the excitonic problem for complicate materials.
For instance, the Wannier model breaks down if the exciton is delocalized in reciprocal space, so that we have to account for the band structure beyond the effective mass approximation.
Nonetheless, the Wannier--Mott formalism offers good theoretical insights, even in the \bd~case where an analytical solution cannot be found.
One can go beyond Wannier--Mott by using a reciprocal space description of the excitons through the so-called \gls{bse}---an integral equation where finer details of the band structure, the Coulomb interaction and the system Hamiltonian are included.

In this work, we develop two versions of the \gls{bse}\@, one that we will apply to effective low-energy models, and another one using exact diagonalization methods.
The \abinit~version of the \gls{bse}\footnote{For a reader fond of high-energy physics, we leave here a historical note. The \gls{bse} was first introduced in the literature by Y.~Nambu in 1950~\cite{Nambu_1950}, and is named after the physicists Hans Bethe and Edwin Salpeter who derived it in 1951~\cite{Bethe_Salpeter_1951}, with posterior contributions by Gell-Mann and Low~\cite{Gell-Mann_Low_1951}. Its original formulation is in the context of quantum field theories, where composite two-fermion states can arise as well. One example is the electron--positron pair, positronium. Either in its covariant form or nonrelativistic form, the starting point to derive the \gls{bse} is defining a four-point, or two-particle, interacting Green function~\cite{Gell-Mann_Low_1951,Martin_Reining_Ceperley_2016}. Notwithstanding their differences, both formalisms can be conciliated into a relativistic many-body theory~\cite{Lindgren_2011}. Just like in Section~\ref{sec:many_particle_description} for the many-particle description of solids, where we introduced the diagram notation, one can also write the \gls{bse} in a diagrammatic form (which of course has its own particle physics version).} is not addressed here.
Besides being technically more involved, it is extremely demanding computationally, so it is necessary to come up with more efficient approaches.
Nonetheless, it can be frequently encountered in the literature, in particular in works using \abinit~methods for the optical response of materials~\cite{BerkeleyGW,Yambo_2009}.
If the reader is curious about it, we refer to the original work by M.~Rohlfing and S.~G.~Louie~\cite{Louie_2000}, or the PhD thesis by Alejandro J.~Ur\'ia-\'Alvarez~\cite{Alex_PhD} for a more recent text with a derivation adapted from~\cite{Louie_2000}.
Combined with the $GW$ method for calculating quasiparticle band structures, the state-of-the-art for exciton and optical response calculations is called the $GW$+\gls{bse} method, constituting the theoretical basis of several computational packages~\cite{Yambo_2009,BerkeleyGW,Perfetto_Stefanucci_2018,Vorwerk_Aurich_Cocchi_Draxl_2019,GPAW_2025}, which has made its way to chemistry as well~\cite{Blase_2020,Amblard_Blase_Duchemin_2023,Amblard_Blase_Duchemin_2024,TURBOMOLE_2013,TURBOMOLE_2023}.
On par with $GW$+\gls{bse}, there is another formalism for predicting the optoelectronic properties of materials, the \gls{tddft} formalism~\cite{Runge_Gross_1984,Rubio_2002}, the extension of the \gls{dft} formalism we mentioned in Section~\ref{subsubsec:abinit_methods}.
The \gls{tddft} method for linear response has also faced a recent extension to the relativistic case, which is called the quantum-electrodynamical density functional theory (QEDFT) formalism~\cite{Ruggenthaler_2025}.

In the following section, we derive the Bethe--Salpeter equation applied to effective models, followed by a derivation of the Wannier equation.
Then in Section~\ref{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_exact} we dwell once again on the \gls{bse}, this time using exact diagonalization methods as they are implemented in the code we contributed to in this thesis.
To finalize the chapter, we derive expressions to study the linear optical response of materials, with and without excitonic effects.


\section{The Bethe--Salpeter Equation: application to effective models}
\label{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_effective_models}

In this section, we derive the \gls{bse} as an integral equation written in momentum space, allowing a more accurate description of the material excitonic properties.
A $\bk$-space description of the problem feels natural, given the concepts introduced in the previous chapter.
We will use the tools therein acquired to our advantage.
This derivation is adapted from the Master's thesis of J.~C.~G. Henriques in Ref.~\cite{Henriques_thesis}, and the tutorial by M.~F.~C.~Quintela in Ref.~\cite{Mauricio_2022}, where one can look into it with more detail.
Here we explain as well how to arrive at the interaction Hamiltonian in momentum space, which is not as easy as in high-energy physics, because one has to account for the crystalline structure of the material.
Instead of working in a plane-wave representation, we have to work with a basis of Bloch states, but we explain how in a moment.

We have seen that the ground state of insulators, or semiconductors at low enough temperatures, comprises all the valence bands filled and the conduction bands empty.
In the context of many-particle physics, the ground state, that we can represent in ket notation as $\ket{\GS}$, is many times called Fermi sea.
That is why in some texts one often sees the ket $\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$ for a systems ground state.
The Fermi sea comes from populating the electronic states of the material starting from the lowest energy states, and filling up the states with increasing energy, until charge neutrality has been reached.
If there is no electronic bandgap, the energy of the highest occupied state, at zero temperature, is called the Fermi energy or Fermi level, with symbol $\EF$.
That is the case of metals, where $\EF$ lies in the middle of the partially filled highest valence band.
In semiconductors and insulators, the Fermi energy lies in the midway between the energy of the highest occupied state and the energy of the lowest unoccupied state.
More generally, it can be defined as the chemical potential $\mu$ (see Section~\ref{subsec:rpa_dielectric_function}) at zero temperature~\cite{Simon_Oxford_2013}.

Since we introduced the exciton as a bound electron--hole complex, we will assume that the unbound electron--hole states serve as a good basis in which we can expand the excitonic states.
To simplify the reasoning, we assume that only the top valence band and the lowest conduction band contribute to the exciton.
In this way, and in the second quantization formalism, we can write the generic exciton state $\nu$ as the coherent superposition of electron--hole pairs

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bosonic_exciton_operator}
    b_\nu^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\area}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) c_{c \bk}^{\dagger} c_{v \bk}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sqrt{\area}$ is the extension area of the \bd~system, $\phi_{\nu}(\bk)$ is the momentum-resolved excitonic wavefunction (that we wish to find), $\nu$ is a set of (good) exciton quantum numbers, and $c^\dagger_{\lambda \bk}$ ($c_{\lambda \bk}$) creates (annihilates) an electron with momentum $\bk$ in the band $\lambda$\footnote{In the second quantization formalism, one usually uses $c$ for fermions and $b$ for bosons. The symbol $a$ is commonly used for both fermions and bosons, depending on the context.}.
For our two-band model, $\lambda = v,c$.
In that case, $\ket{\GS}$ consists of a completely filled valence band and an empty conduction band.
This should be sufficient for a two-band model.
Here, we assume only vertical transitions due to the small wavevector carried by the photon, meaning that the exciton center of mass carries no momentum.
Often in the literature, one works with a fermionic operator for the hole as a quasiparticle, but that is not strictly necessary.
We stick to electron operators and perceive the hole as the absence of an electron.
Being quadratic on the fermion operators, the operators $b$ and $b^{\dagger}$ obey commutation relations and are therefore bosonic (see footnote~\ref{fn:second_quantization_definitions}).
The operator $b_{\nu}^{\dagger}$ can be seen as an exciton creation operator, that when acting on $\ket{\GS}$ creates one exciton in the state $| X_\nu \rangle$; in the occupation number representation, we can write
% Sometimes, we can adopt the nomenclature from atomic physics to label the exciton states, say $1s$, $2s$, $2p$, etc.
\begin{equation}
    b_{\nu}^{\dagger}|\GS\rangle=|1_{v \bk_1},1_{v \bk_2},\dots,1_{v \bk_f},0,0,\dots,\underset{\underset{X_\nu}{\downarrow}}{1},0,0,\dots,0_{c \bk_1},0_{c \bk_2},\dots,0_{c\bk_f}\rangle\,.
\end{equation}

The total Hamiltonian of the system

\begin{equation}
    \Ha = \Ho + \V
\end{equation}

\noindent has two parts, the kinetic or noninteracting one $\Ho$, and the interacting one $\V$.
We assume that we know how to diagonalize $\Ho$, so that we can write it in the $\ket{\lambda \bk}$ basis ignoring spin, as

\begin{equation}
    \Ho = \sum_{\lambda \bk} \epsilon_{\lambda \bk} c_{\lambda \bk}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda \bk}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\epsilon_{\lambda \bk}$ is the energy of an electron with momentum $\bk$ in the band $\lambda$.
For the interacting part, we start from the real-space basis reading

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_field_operators}
    \V = \frac{1}{2} \int \dr \int \dr '\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\br)\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\br') V (\br - \br')\hat{\psi}(\br')\hat{\psi}(\br) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent if we ignore the spin degree of freedom once more, and $\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\br)/\hat{\psi}(\br)$ creates/destroys an electron at position $\br$\footnote{Naturally, these operators obey commutation relations for bosons and anticommutation relations for fermions. However, in the \textit{continuous} real-space basis the Kronecker deltas become Dirac deltas like this: $[\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\br),\hat{\psi}(\br)] = \delta(\br - \br')$, if we are talking about bosons. For fermions it is similar.}.
These operators are called field operators, and we keep the hat on them to stress that they are operators and to distinguish them from the wavefunction $\psi(\br)$.
In this work, we will not worry with spinful excitons, so there is no need to include spin indices in the field and fermionic operators.
This is justified if we ignore \gls{soc} in the Hamiltonian, and if we assume that the electron--electron interaction includes only the Coulomb potential, which induces no spin flipping.
In this section, we tacitly model the Coulomb interaction by the Rytova--Keldysh potential, and that will be implicit until the end of this section.
Therefore, to ease the notation we use the symbol $V$ instead of $W$ or $V_{\RK}$.
To write both terms in the Hamiltonian in momentum space, we have to convert the interaction term to the $\bk$-space.
For that, we use the expansion of the field operators in terms of Bloch states

\begin{equation}
    \hat{\psi}(\br)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\lambda, \bk}\braket{\br}{\lambda \bk} c_{\lambda \bk}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\lambda \bk}\psi_{\lambda, \bk}(\br)c_{\lambda \bk}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $N$ is the number of unit cells (or $\bk$ points), and write the potential in terms of its Fourier transform as (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:finite_volume_two_point_inverse_Fourier} in Appendix~\ref{app:fourier_transform})

\begin{equation}
    V (\br - \br') = \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bq} \ee^{\ii \bq \vdot(\br-\br')} V(\bq)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Replacing everything in~\eqref{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_field_operators} gives

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_momentum_space_substitution}
    \begin{aligned}
        \V = & \frac{1}{2 \area N^{2}} \sum_{\bq} \sum_{\{\bk_{i},\lambda_i\}} V(\bq) c_{\lambda_1 \bk_1}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_2 \bk_2}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_3 \bk_3} c_{\lambda_4 \bk_4} \times \\
                  & \times \left(\int_{\area} \psi_{\lambda_{1}, \bk_{1}}^{*}(\br) \ee^{\ii \bq \vdot \br}\psi_{\lambda_4, \bk_4}(\br) \dr \right) \left(\int_{\area} \psi_{\lambda_2, \bk_2}^{*}(\br') \ee^{-\ii \bq \vdot \br'} \psi_{\lambda_3, \bk_3}(\br') \dr' \right),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sum_{\{\bk_{i},\lambda_i\}} \equiv \sum_{\bk_1 \bk_2 \bk_3 \bk_4} \sum_{\lambda_1 \lambda_2 \lambda_3 \lambda_4}$, and the integrals are over the plane of the material.
If using braket notation, then

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_momentum_space_substitution_option_2}
    \begin{aligned}
        \V = & \frac{1}{2 \area N^{2}}  \sum_{\bq} \sum_{\{\bk_{i},\lambda_i\}} V(\bq) c_{\lambda_1 \bk_1}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_2 \bk_2}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_3 \bk_3} c_{\lambda_4 \bk_4} \times \\
                  &  \times \langle \lambda_1, \bk_1 |\ei{\bq \vdot \br} | \lambda_4, \bk_4 \rangle  \langle \lambda_2, \bk_2 | \ei[-]{\bq \vdot \br'} | \lambda_3, \bk_3 \rangle %\mel{\lambda_1, \bk_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\lambda_4, \bk_4} \mel{\lambda_2, \bk_2}{\ei[-]{\bq \vdot \br'}}{\lambda_3, \bk_3}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent An auxiliary calculation for the matrix elements follows.
The Bloch wavefunctions, in the atomic gauge that we used to write the TB Bloch Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn}, have the form

\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\lambda, \bk}(\br)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bR,i\alpha}\ee^{\ii\bk\vdot(\bR+\bt_{i})}C_{i\alpha}^{\lambda \bk}\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Now we can go back and compute the matrix elements

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mel{\lambda_1, \bk_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\lambda_4, \bk_4} &= \int_{\area} \psi_{\lambda_{1}, \bk_{1}}^{*}(\br)\,\ee^{\ii \bq \vdot \br}\psi_{\lambda_4, \bk_4}(\br)\,\dr = \\
        & = \int_{\area} u_{\lambda_1, \bk_1}^{*}(\br)\,\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bk_{1}+\bk_{4})\vdot\br} u_{\lambda_4, \bk_4}(\br)\,\dr \approx \\
        & \approx \left \langle u_{\lambda_1, \bk_1}^{*}(\br) u_{\lambda_4, \bk_4}(\br) \right\rangle_{\mathrm{UC}} \underbrace{\int_{\area} \ee^{\ii(\bq-\bk_{1}+\bk_{4})\vdot\br} \dr }_{\area \delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}}} \,.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we used the envelope function approximation.
It consists in replacing the multiplying periodic parts of the Bloch wavefunctions (see Bloch Theorem~\ref{th:Bloch_theorem}) by their average over the unit cell.
This approximation assumes that the periodic part of the Bloch function varies fast over the length scale of the unit cell, while the exponential part varies slowly.
The average of the periodic part of the Bloch wavefunctions over a unit cell can be computed, also approximately using the point-like orbital approximation (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:point-like_orbital_approximation} in the next section), by

\begin{align}
    \label{eq:envelope_function_approximation}
    \left\langle u_{\lambda_1, \bk_1}^{*}(\br)u_{\lambda_4, \bk_4}(\br)\right\rangle _{\mathrm{UC}} =& \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \int_{\mathrm{UC}} u_{\lambda_1, \bk_1}^{*}(\br) u_{\lambda_4, \bk_4}(\br)\, \dr = \notag\\
    =& \frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \int_{\mathrm{UC}} \Bigg[\sum_{\bR,i\alpha}\ee^{-\ii\bk_{1}\vdot(\bR + \bt_{i} - \br)} \left(C_{i \alpha}^{\lambda_1 \bk_1}\right)^{*}\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)\Bigg] \times \notag \\
    & \times \Bigg[\sum_{\bR',j\beta} \ee^{\ii\bk_{4}\vdot(\bR'+\bt_{j}-\br)} C_{j \beta}^{\lambda_4 \bk_4}\phi_{\beta}\left(\br-\bR'-\bt_{j}\right)\Bigg]\dr = \notag \\
    =&\frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \sum_{\bR,\bR'}\ee^{-\ii\bk_{1}\vdot(\bR + \bt_{i} - \br)} \ee^{\ii\bk_{4}\vdot(\bR'+\bt_{j}-\br)} \sum_{i\alpha,j\beta}\left(C_{i \alpha}^{\lambda_1 \bk_1}\right)^{*}C_{j \beta}^{\lambda_4 \bk_4} \times \notag \\
    & \times \int_{\mathrm{UC}} \ee^{\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot\br}\underbrace{\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)\phi_{\beta} \left(\br-\bR'-\bt_{j}\right)}_{\approx \delta_{ij} \delta_{\alpha \beta} \delta_{\bR \bR'} \delta(\br-\bR-\bt_{i})}\dr \approx \notag\\
    \approx & \frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \sum_{\bR} \cancel{\ee^{-\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot(\bR+\bt_{i})}} \sum_{i\alpha,j\beta} \left(C_{i \alpha}^{\lambda_1 \bk_1}\right)^{*} C_{j \beta}^{\lambda_4 \bk_4} \cancel{\ee^{\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot(\bR+\bt_{i})}} = \notag \\
    =&\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \sum_{i\alpha}\left(C_{i \alpha}^{\lambda_1 \bk_1}\right)^{*} C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} = \\
    =&\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\left(\bC^{\lambda_1 \bk_1}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\lambda_4 \bk_4},\text{ since }\sum_{\bR}1=N\,.
\end{align}

\noindent Finally, the plane-wave matrix element reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mel{\lambda_1, \bk_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\lambda_4, \bk_4} & = \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\left(\bC^{\lambda_1 \bk_1}\right)^{\dagger} \bC^{\lambda_4 \bk_4} \area \,\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}} = \\
                                                                   & = N \left(\bC^{\lambda_1 \bk_1}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\lambda_4 \bk_4}\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent whereas for the other matrix element the same reasoning applies.
In this way, the interaction term of the Hamiltonian in momentum space reads


\begin{align}
    \V =  & \frac{1}{2 \area N^{2}}\sum_{\bq}\sum_{\{\bk_{i},\lambda_{i}\}} V N^{2} \left(\bC^{\lambda_1 \bk_1}\right)^{\dagger} \bC^{\lambda_4 \bk_4} \delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}} \left(\bC^{\lambda_2 \bk_2}\right)^{\dagger} \bC^{\lambda_3 \bk_3} \delta_{\bq,\bk_{3}-\bk_{2}} \times \\
          & \times  c_{\lambda_1 \bk_1}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_2 \bk_2}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_3 \bk_3} c_{\lambda_4 \bk_4} = \\
        = & \frac{1}{2 \area N^{2}}\sum_{\bq,\bk_{3},\bk_{4}} \sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}V(\bq) N^{2}\left(\bC^{\lambda_1 \bk_{4}+\bq}\right)^{\dagger} \bC^{\lambda_4 \bk_4}\left(\bC^{\lambda_2 \bk_{3}-\bq}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\lambda_3 \bk_3} \\
          & \times  c_{\lambda_1 \bk_4 + \bq}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_2 \bk_3 - \bq}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_3 \bk_3} c_{\lambda_4 \bk_4} = \\
        = & \frac{1}{2 \area}\sum_{\bq,\bk_{3},\bk_{4}}\sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}} V(\bq) F_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}}\left(\bk_{3},\bk_{4},\bq\right)c_{\lambda_1 \bk_4 + \bq}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_2 \bk_3 - \bq}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_3 \bk_3} c_{\lambda_4 \bk_4}\,,
\end{align}

\noindent where we defined the form factor $F_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}} \left(\bk,\bp,\bq\right) = \left(\bC^{\lambda_{1} \bp+\bq}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\lambda_{4} \bp}\left(\bC^{\lambda_{2} \bk-\bq}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\lambda_3 \bk_{3}}$.

For simplicity, we can take spinors of the low-energy Bloch Hamiltonian for the vectors $\bC^{\lambda \bk}$, which is not a problem if the exciton is localized around a specific point in the \bz.
In general, there may exist more than one of those points.
For instance, in the example of \gls{hbn}, to which we will apply this formalism, there are the nonequivalent $K$ and $K'$ points in the \bz~where the bandgap and the exciton are located.
As both valleys share the same value for the bandgap, the exciton states enjoy valley degeneracy.
This means that the \gls{bse} can be solved for only one of the valleys without loss of generality.
If the exciton does not enjoy valley degeneracy, then the valley degree of freedom has to be accounted for.

We assume that the system can be well described by the bosonic operators $b^\dagger$ and $b$, and that $\nu$ is a set of good quantum numbers, in the sense that the Hamiltonian is diagonal in this basis, which allows us to write

\begin{equation}
    \Ha_X = \sum_{\nu} E_{\nu} b_{\nu}^{\dagger} b_{\nu}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we introduced the notation $\Ha_X$ for the Hamiltonian written in the basis of exciton states $\nu$.
Now that we have both terms in the Hamiltonian written in momentum space, the strategy is as follows: we take the commutator $[\Ha_X, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]$ between $\Ha_X$ and the creation operator $b_{\nu}^{\dagger}$; next, we take the same commutator, but with the initial total Hamiltonian $\Ha$ instead (this step is the most involved one); finally, we equate both results, and the Bethe--Salpeter equation is then automatically extracted.
The first commutator is straightforward
    
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:H_X_commutator}
    [\Ha_X, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = E_{\nu} b_\nu^{\dagger} = E_\nu \frac{1}{\sqrt{\area}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_\nu(\bk) c_{c \bk}^{\dagger} c_{v \bk}\,,
\end{equation}\

\noindent where to write the second equality we explicitly wrote the bosonic creation operator in terms of electron--hole pairs as in~\eqref{eq:bosonic_exciton_operator}.
Now, we work with the total Hamiltonian $\Ha$ in terms of fermionic operators, and compute

\begin{equation}
[\Ha, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = [\Ho, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] + [\V, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent but we better treat both contributions separately.
First, the commutator with the kinetic component evaluates to

\begin{equation}
    [\Ho, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = \sum_{\lambda \bk} \epsilon_{\lambda \bk} \sum_{\bq} \frac{\phi_{\nu}(\bq)}{\sqrt{\area}} \left [c_{\lambda \bk}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda \bk}, c_{c \bq}^{\dagger} c_{v \bq} \right ] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\area}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) (\epsilon_{c \bk} - \epsilon_{v \bk}) c_{c \bk}^{\dagger} c_{v \bk} \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent after simplifying.
At last, we have to do the commutator with the interacting term.
This task is not hard, just very tedious.
Therefore, we describe briefly the key steps and present the final result.
For the commutator $\left[\V, b_\nu^{\dagger}\right]$, we have to compute the commutator between an operator quartic in $c$ with one quadratic in $c$; four contributions arise.
Each contribution is an operator quartic in $c$.
To make it comparable with the very first commutator in Eq.~\ref{eq:H_X_commutator}, we need to introduce an approximation that turns the quartic operators into quadratic ones---the mean-field approximation.
It can also be called the Hartree--Fock decoupling~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004}.
Applying this approximation to one of the initial contributions in $\left[\V, b_\nu^{\dagger}\right]$ results in four terms, and, in turn, each of these terms is a product between an operator quadratic in $c$ and an equilibrium average of another operator quadratic in $c$.
To better clarify, we write this decoupling using mathematical symbols.
Assuming that the fermionic operators are written in a specific state basis $| i \rangle$, we write

\begin{equation}
    c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} c_{i_4} \approx c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} \langle c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_4} \rangle  + c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_4} \langle c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} \rangle - c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_4} \langle c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} \rangle - c_{i_1}^{\dagger} c_{i_3} \langle c_{i_2}^{\dagger} c_{i_4} \rangle\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent This approximation comes from admitting that an operator of the form $c^\dagger c$ exhibits small fluctuations around its average value $\langle c^\dagger c \rangle$, which is an average computed at thermal equilibrium (without forgetting about the fermion statistics).
One can consult Ref.~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004} for more details.
This approximation has to be done for each of the four contributions in $\left[\V, b_\nu^{\dagger}\right]$.
Doing so results in a total of sixteen terms.
In each of these sixteen terms, we have to evaluate an expectation value of the form $\langle c^\dagger c \rangle$.
These expectation values are simply equilibrium thermal averages, and evaluated at zero temperature for simplicity\footnote{In the eventuality of the gapped material having a gap comparable with the thermal energy $k_B T$, then considering $T = 0 $ \unit{\kelvin} simplifies the analysis a lot, keeping the conduction bands unpopulated in the ground state.}.
Therefore, we can apply the rule

\begin{equation}
    \langle c_{\lambda_1 \bk}^{\dagger} c_{\lambda_2 \bk'}  \rangle =
    \begin{cases}
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = c, \\
        \delta_{\bk,\bk'} \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = v, \\
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = c \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_2 = v, \\
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = v \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_2 = c.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent After that, we need to gather all the contributions, while recovering the sums in momentum to simplify the expression---equal terms simply add up or cancel.
One can also smartly use the properties of the form factor, changing the order in which the $\bC^{\lambda \bk}$ arrays appear.
To arrive at the final result, a couple of shifts in momentum might also be in order.
At the end, we can set $[\Ha_X, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = [\Ho, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] + [\V, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]$, from which we can extract the Bethe--Salpeter equation for the exciton wavefunction $\phi_\nu(\bk)$ and its respective energy $E_\nu$

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        E_\nu \phi_\nu(\bk)  =&   \phi_{\nu}(\bk) (\epsilon_{c \bk} - \epsilon_{v \bk}) - \\
        & \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bp} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V(\bk-\bp) F_{cvvc}(\bk,\bp,\bk-\bp) +  \\
        & \frac{1}{\area} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) \sum_{\bp} V(\bp) \left[F_{vvvv}(\bp + \bk ,\bk,\bp) - F_{vcvc}(\bp + \bk,\bk,\bp) \right].
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

The first term on the \gls{rhs} is the gain in energy due to the transition of the system from the ground state to the excited state of an unbound electron--hole pair.
The second term accounts for the attractive interaction between the electron and the hole, making it possible for the complex to form a bound state.
The last term can be interpreted as a renormalization of the bands due to electron--electron interactions, so it can be simply absorbed into the first term.
It does not mean that it can be ignored, but we sweep it under the rug for now.
Doing so, we arrive at the simplified form of the Bethe--Salpeter equation:

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{equation}
    E_\nu \phi_\nu(\bk) = (\epsilon_{c \bk} - \epsilon_{v \bk}) \phi_{\nu}(\bk) - \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bp} \phi_\nu (\bp) V(\bk-\bp) F_{cvvc}(\bk,\bp,\bk-\bp)
    \label{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation}
\end{empheq}

\noindent The extension to multi-band systems is straightforward, but we choose to omit it here, referring to~\cite{Mauricio_2022} instead.
The exchange term in the Coulomb interaction is neglected, but this does not strongly affect the exciton binding energy~\cite{Thygesen_2017}.
The multi-band case is to be considered only in the next section, and with a different formalism altogether.
We just make the remark that the negative sign going with the term in the potential is correct, and the electron--hole interaction is for sure attractive, since we use the convention of a positive interaction potential for $V(\bk)$.

We finish this section with a derivation of the Wannier equation as in~\eqref{eq:Wannier_equation} from the \gls{bse} in Eq.~\ref{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation}.
For that, we need to introduce two approximations.
First, we make the parabolic dispersion approximation, that is

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\lambda \bk} \simeq \epsilon_{\lambda \bzero} + \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 m_{\lambda}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where  $m_\lambda$ is the effective mass of an electron/hole in the band $\lambda$, and $\bk$ is measured from the point in the \bz~where the exciton is localized.
It follows that

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{c \bk} - \epsilon_{v \bk} \simeq E_g + \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 \mu_{eh}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $E_g =  \epsilon_{c \bzero}-\epsilon_{v \bzero}$ is the bandgap, and $\mu_{eh}$ is the electron--hole reduced mass.
In the second term on the \gls{rhs} of~\eqref{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation}, we approximate $F \approx 1$, which is valid for large enough bandgaps~\cite{Ventura_2019}.
In this way, the equation~\eqref{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation} leads to

\begin{equation}
    E_{b,\nu} \phi_{\nu} (\bk) = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 \mu_{eh}} \phi_{\nu} (\bk) - \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bp} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V(\bk - \bp),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $ E_{b,\nu} = E_{\nu} - E_g$ is the binding energy of the excitonic state $\nu$.
Next, we apply a Fourier transform to the previous equation.
The term on the \gls{lhs} is direct, it becomes $ E_{b,\nu} \phi_{\nu} (\br)$.
The first term on the \gls{rhs}, after doing the inverse Fourier transform back to real-space representation, gives simply $p^2/2 \mu_{eh}$ acting on the wavefunction $\phi_{\nu} (\br)$.
The second term on the \gls{rhs} is a convolution in momentum space, so it becomes a product in real space.
To see that, let us perform the inverse Fourier transform of just that term and write\footnote{All details about Fourier transforms can be found in App.~\ref{app:fourier_transform}.}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:potential_inverse_Fourier_transform}
\begin{aligned}
     &\frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bk} \ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br} \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bp} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V(\bk - \bp) = \frac{1}{\area^2} \sum_{\bk} \sum_{\bp} \ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \br} \ee^{-\ii \bp \vdot \br} \ee^{\ii \bp \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V(\bk - \bp) = \\
    =& \frac{1}{\area^2} \sum_{\bp} \ee^{\ii \bp \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) \sum_{\bk} \ee^{\ii (\bk - \bp) \vdot \br} V(\bk - \bp) \overset{\bk \to \bk + \bp}{=} \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bp} \ee^{\ii \bp \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) \frac{1}{\area}  \sum_{\bk} \ee^{\ii (\bk) \vdot \br} V(\bk) = \\
    =& \phi_{\nu} (\br) V(\br)\,.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent In this way, we recover the Wannier equation

% \begin{equation}
%     \phi_{\nu} (\bk) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\area}} \sum_{\br} \ei{ \bk \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\br)
% \end{equation}

% \noindent and to the potential

% \begin{equation}
%     V(\bk - \bp) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\area}} \sum_{\br'} \ei{ (\bk - \bp) \vdot \br'} V (\br')
% \end{equation}

% \noindent and use the identity

% \begin{equation}
%     \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bp} \ei{ \bp \vdot (\br - \br')} = \delta_{\br,\br'},
% \end{equation}

% \noindent to recover the Wannier equation:

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{equation}
    E_{b, \nu} \psi_\nu (\br) = \frac{p^2}{2 \mu_{eh}} \psi_\nu (\br) - V(\br) \psi_\nu (\br)
    \label{eq:Wannier_equation_derived}
\end{empheq}

As we can see, the (simplified) excitonic problem is a differential equation in real space, and an integral equation in momentum space.
The real-space approach is more useful for analytic treatments (or semi-analytical if we use the Rytova--Keldysh potential).
Unfortunately, it overlooks some features due to the approximations.
As a consequence of approximating $F$ to 1, excitonic states with symmetric angular momentum numbers are degenerate.
On the contrary, the momentum space approach has no analytical solution, nor does it allow a simple semi-analytical approach, but has the advantage of providing more accurate results.

This formalism can be readily applied to isotropic systems, like \gls{hbn}.
Basically, one can separate the angular dependence of the wavefunction from the radial dependence, and in that way the problem can be cast into a \gls{1d} integral equation that, after discretizing the momentum, can be converted to an eigenvalue problem with only one momentum variable.
How to do so in practice is explained in detail in Refs.~\cite{Mauricio_2022} and~\cite{Henriques_Epstein_Peres_2022}, namely in their respective appendices.
In contrast with isotropic systems, for anisotropic systems one cannot separate the angular and radial variables, and one has then to construct a big matrix with two momentum variables.
But for such systems, the \gls{bse} formalism of the next section is more useful.
Despite this observations, in Chapter~\ref{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice} we approach the anisotropic excitonic problem for nanostructured \gls{hbn} using the Wannier formalism---with this method, we obtain interesting results with very low computational effort.
If one wishes to use a more detailed description for excitons, for instance, if a simple \gls{tb} description for the material is not possible (e.g. \ch{MoS2}, phosphorene, etc.), then the one presented in the next section is more appropriate.
It constitutes as well a playground to test the results obtained in Chapter~\ref{ch:screening} for our numerical implementation of screening.

Before we report the research outcomes in this dissertation, we still need to introduce another picture for the excitonic problem, based on the numerical diagonalization of the \gls{bse}, but still within a \gls{tb} approach.
After that, we revisit the concept of dielectric function, but now in the local and dynamic limit.
In that way, we shall reintroduce the concept of optical conductivity: the noninteracting we have already seen, and the interacting one at the excitonic level; finally, we will be able to interpret the model parameters of the conductivity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:optical_conductivity_model}, herein derived in the light of quantum mechanics.

\section{The Bethe--Salpeter Equation: exact diagonalization}
\label{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_exact}

In this section, we dwell on a second-principles-based approach to the calculation of the excitons, within an atomistic description where the \gls{tb} approximation discussed previously is key.
The discussion is adapted from~\cite{Alex_PhD} and~\cite{Xatu}.
This approach presents technical and computational advantages to first-principles-based methodologies for excitons pretty much established in the literature, such as the \GW+\bse~method~\cite{Louie_2000,BerkeleyGW,Diana_PhD,Jornada_PhD}.
Even though more numerically inefficient than solving the Wannier equation or the \bse~applied to effective models, this method is still much more efficient than \abinit~ones.
In spite of this, we can expect from it detailed insights into the excitonic problem that the ones introduced until now do not provide.
It is very useful when one is interested in bigger systems, or when the symmetries of the excitonic states are relevant.

The starting point is to write the total electronic Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Hamiltonian_start}
    \Ha = \Ho + \V = \sum_{i,j} t_{i j} \cdagger_i c_j + \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{ijkl} \cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the indices include the relevant degrees of freedom (orbital site and orbital type), the first term corresponds to the interaction between the valence electrons and the ions (with frozen degrees of freedom), and the second term corresponds to the electrostatic Coulomb interaction between the electrons.
The single-particle physics is encoded in $\Ho$, to which all the techniques and results discussed in Section~\ref{sec:single_particle_properties} apply (and the ones within the \rpa~from Section~\ref{sec:dielectric_function_quantum} as well).
In the Section~\ref{sec:single_particle_properties}, where we discussed single-particle properties of electrons in crystals,  particle--particle interactions were not completely discarded, in the sense that the electronic eigenstates---the Bloch states---that describe the ground state of the material, were previously obtained through self-consistent calculations (\gls{hf} or \gls{dft}), where interactions and correlations are treated at the mean-field level.
The many-particle physics (e.g. excitons) is encoded in $\V$, without which excitons cannot be described, in concordance with the previous section.
Instead of departing from the many-body Hamiltonian with the interacting part in terms of field operators, we depart from a Hamiltonian in real space already written in an atomistic basis, i.e., we adopt the \gls{tb} description from the Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method} of the previous chapter.

The sums in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hamiltonian_start} run over indices including orbitals and position degrees of freedom (and spin, eventually).
The parameters $t_{i j}$ are the tight-binding parameters, while $V_{ijkl}$ are the interaction matrix elements\footnote{Note that by matrix elements, we mean the symbols $V_{ijkl}$, which are matrix elements of the first quantized interaction term $V$ between single-particle states belonging to the one-particle Hilbert space, and not matrix elements of the second quantized operator $\V$ between states living in the Fock space.}

\begin{equation}
    V_{ijkl} = \mel{i,j}{V}{k,l} = \int \dr \int \dr' \phi^*_i(\br) \phi^*_j(\br') V(\br,\br') \phi_k(\br) \phi_l(\br')\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent At this point, we do not particularize how we model the screening, postponing that for later on.

\subsection{The Bethe--Salpeter equation rederived}
\label{subsec:BSE_rederived}

Now, with the purpose of describing excitons in the more general multi-band case, we need to start from a ground state $\ket{\GS}$ taking all bands into account.
For undoped \bd~semiconductors or insulators, whose ground state consists of all the valence bands filled and empty conduction bands, we can write mathematically

\begin{equation}
    \ket{\GS} = \prod_{n,\bk}^{\epsilon_{n \bk} \leq \EF} c_{n \bk}^\dagger \ket{0} ,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\ket{0}$ denotes the \enquote{vacuum} state (the crystal in the absence of valence electrons) and $\EF$ is the Fermi energy.
Exciting an electron from the valence band $v$ with momentum $\bk$ to the conduction band $c$ with momentum $\bk+\bQ$, we are before an electron--hole pair, whose state can be formally written as

\begin{equation}
    \ket{v,c,\bk,\bQ} \equiv \cdagger_{c \bk+\bQ} c_{v \bk} \ket{\GS} .
\end{equation}

\noindent As a passing note, there is no technical reason why we should adopt a quasiparticle hole description for the holes (i.e., define a hole creation/annihilation operator $h$ as $h=c/\cdagger$).
Just like in the previous section, we only use fermion operators.

If the exciton has a center of mass momentum $\bQ$, and assuming that a basis of electron--hole pairs is appropriate for the excitonic states, then we can write as an ansatz for the exciton state $\ket{X_{\bQ}}$

\begin{equation}
    \ket{X_{\bQ}} = \sum_{v,c,\bk} A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \ket{v,c,\bk,\bQ} = \sum_{v,c,\bk} A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \cdagger_{c \bk+\bQ} c_{v \bk} \ket{\GS} .
\end{equation}

\noindent In this way, the exciton state is written as a linear combination of electron--hole pair states summing over valence bands, conduction bands, and momentum.
We can consider the quantum number $\bQ$ a good one since the interaction conserves momentum, as all electron--hole pairs share the same center of mass momentum $\bQ$.
Up until now, we have just reintroduced the problem as in the beginning of Section~\ref{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_effective_models}, albeit with a different notation.
The main difference resides in the approach for determining the coefficients $A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk)$ (previously labeled by $\phi_{\nu}(\bk)$, that we identified as the exciton wavefunction).
Herein, we follow a variational approach (see the beginning of Section~\ref{subsec:born_oppenheimer_approximation}).
Hence, the expansion coefficients $A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk)$ are determined by minimization of the expectation value $\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}$  (omitting the $\bQ$ subscript momentarily) with respect to $\ket{X}$

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expectation_value_exciton}
    \fdv{E[X]}{X} = \fdv{X}  \left[\frac{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{\braket{X}{X}}\right] = \fdv{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{X} \braket{X}{X} - \underbrace{ \frac{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{\braket{X}{X}}}_{E_X}  \fdv{\braket{X}{X}}{X} = 0 \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Upon performing the variational derivatives and using the notation $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)$ for the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian $\mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ha}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} \equiv H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)$ between two electron--hole pair states, we arrive at the equation

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BSE_compact_form}
   \sum_{v',c',\bk'} H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) A^{\bQ}_{v'c'}(\bk') = E_X A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which is a different way of writing the \bse, and the symbols $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)$ are the matrix elements of what we call the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian.

The expansion of the excitonic state in a basis of electron--hole pairs is an ansatz under the assumption that the basis is adequate (or complete, technically speaking), so that we can write the true excitonic states in that basis.
Instead of considering all the possible combinations of electron--hole excitations, where several electrons would populate the conduction bands leaving as many holes behind in the valence bands, we do the calculations in a reduced Fock space where the only excited states are unbound electron--hole pairs, presuming that this is a good enough approximation.
For a more rigorous discussion of this aspect, we refer to Refs.~\cite{Xatu,Alex_PhD}.

We need to compute the matrix elements $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)$ of the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian.
Naturally, this has to be done numerically in practice, which also means that we have to truncate the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian and work with an approximate finite-matrix representation.
Physically speaking, this approximation means that we are considering only a finite number of valence and conduction bands, and a finite number of momentum points in the \bz~(or unit cells, equivalently).
Mathematically, it means that we are working with a truncated basis for $\Ho$, which in practice is not complete.
Numerically, we have to compute the matrix elements for each pair of momenta $(\bk,\bk')$ in the \bz~mesh, and for each pair $(vc,v'c')$ of pairs of valence bands $v,v'$ and of conduction bands $c,c'$ and ensure numerical convergence.
We mean that the numbers of $\bk$ points, valence bands and conduction bands included have to be enough to obtain the expected degeneracies and symmetries.
Naturally, we select only the valence and conduction bands more relevant for the excitons, those closer to the Fermi level.

Before we explain how to compute the matrix elements for the \gls{bse}, let us see how far we can go with analytical expressions.
The Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian matrix elements are given by $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ho}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} + \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ}$.
The first term corresponds to the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian at the mean-field level between two electron--hole pair states, while the second term corresponds to the interaction matrix elements.
We work out first the matrix elements of $\Ho$, as they are easier to deal with.
For that, let us assume that the operator $\Ho$ can be written in a spectral representation of Bloch states labeled by $\ket{n,\bk}$ ($|\lambda, \bk \rangle$ in the notation of the previous section).
Then, in its second-quantized form, the single-particle Hamiltonian reads

\begin{equation}
   \Ho = \sum_{n,\bk} \epsilon_{n \bk} \cdagger_{n \bk} c_{n \bk}\,,
\label{eq:Hamiltonian_single_particle}
\end{equation}

\noindent assuming that the diagonalization has been done.
Replacing the expression above in the matrix element of $\Ho$, it is not hard to show that

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:single_particle_matrix_element}
    \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ho}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = \qty(\epsilon_{c \bk + \bQ} - \epsilon_{v \bk}) \delta_{v,v'} \delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{c,c'}\, \qq{up to a shift of $\EF$.}
\end{equation}

The interaction matrix elements are tricky to compute, as the interaction operator $\V$ is a two-body operator, quartic in the creation and annihilation operators.
Nonetheless, in this section we refrain from performing any kind of mean-field approximation, and compute the matrix elements exactly.
Doing so is not hard, it is just tedious.
We do not perform all the steps, and instead explain briefly how to proceed.
For convenience, we make a brief change and abuse of notation, and relabel $(v',\bk') \to v'$, $(c',\bk' + \bQ) \to c'$, $(v,\bk) \to v$ and $(c,\bk + \bQ) \to c$, and keep the notation for $\V$ used above.
Then, we have

\begin{align*}
    \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} &= \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{ijkl} \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = \\
    &= \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{ijkl} \mel{\GS}{\cdagger_{v} c_c \cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k \cdagger_{v'} c_{c'}}{\GS} .
\end{align*}

\noindent For instance, we pick the pair $c_c \cdagger_i$ and use the anti-commutation relations to move the creation operator to the left, and the annihilation operator to the right, giving rise to two new matrix elements.
Then, in each term, we select a new pair of creation and annihilation operators $\cdagger c$ and repeat the process, making more terms appear.
Following this procedure recursively, until we exhaust all our options of pairs of creation and annihilation operators, and end up only with a matrix element of a single $\cdagger c$ pair to evaluate in each term of the final expression, we arrive at

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:many_particle_matrix_element}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = &V_{c'vv'c} - V_{c'vcv'} + \delta_{v v'} \sum_j \qty(V_{c'jcj} - V_{c'jjc}) + \\
        & \delta_{c c'} \sum_j \qty(V_{vjjv'} - V_{vjv'j}) + \delta_{v v'} \delta_{c c'} \frac{1}{2} \sum_{ij} \qty(V_{ijij} - V_{ijji})\,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\noindent Summing the two contributions~\eqref{eq:single_particle_matrix_element} and~\eqref{eq:many_particle_matrix_element}, we have for the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian matrix elements

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:BSE_Hamiltonian_matrix_elements}
    \begin{aligned}
        H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = &\delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{v,v'} \qty[\epsilon_{c \bk + \bQ}  \delta_{c,c'} + \Sigma_{c c'}(\bk + \bQ, \bk' + \bQ)] - \\
        &\delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{c,c'} \qty[\epsilon_{v \bk}  \delta_{v,v'} + \Sigma_{v' v}(\bk + \bQ, \bk' + \bQ)] - \\
        &(D - X)_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)\,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have defined the direct and exchange interaction matrix elements as

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:direct_and_exchange_terms}
    \begin{align}
        D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= V_{c\bk + \bQ,v'\bk', c'\bk' + \bQ, v\bk} \label{subeq:direct_term} \qq{and}\\
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= V_{c\bk + \bQ,v'\bk', v\bk, c'\bk' + \bQ}\,, \label{sub eq:exchange_term}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent respectively, and the self-energy accounting for the interaction between the electron (or hole) with the Fermi sea as

\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{nm} (\bk,\bk') = \sum_{j,\bk''}^{\text{occ}}\qty(V_{n\bk,j\bk'',m\bk',j\bk''} - V_{n\bk,j\bk'',j\bk'',m\bk'})\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the sum is only over occupied bands.
About the self-energy, we have to make a technical remark.
In principle, we could diagonalize the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian with matrix elements given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:BSE_Hamiltonian_matrix_elements} as is.
But that is not what we do.
The Hamiltonian that we depart from in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hamiltonian_start}, is such that the single-particle part has \gls{tb} parameters $t_{i j}$ that come from diagonalizing the Hamiltonian~\eqref{eq:HF_Hamiltonian} including interactions, but at the mean-field level via \gls{hf}~theory or \gls{dft}~\cite{Ozfidan_2014}.
In that way, we can then see the self-energy corrections as already incorporated in the quasiparticle energies $\epsilon_{n \bk}$.
On the other hand, if we were to solve the \gls{bse} as per~\eqref{eq:BSE_Hamiltonian_matrix_elements}, the Hamiltonian to diagonalize in order to obtain the single-particle properties would have to be the one in~\eqref{eq:single_particle_Hamiltonian}, the noninteracting limit of the many-body crystal Hamiltonian in the Born--Oppenheimer approximation.
Then, we would have a distinct set of \gls{tb} parameters $t'_{i j}$, and another initial band structure $\epsilon'_{n \bk}$.
The caveat is that the cancellation might not be exact.
In math notation, it means that $\epsilon_{n \bk} = \epsilon'_{n \bk} + \Sigma_{n n}(\bk + \bQ, \bk + \bQ)$ might be false.
But if we assume that it is true, then the \gls{bse} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:BSE_Hamiltonian_matrix_elements} reduces to


\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{equation}
   \qty(\epsilon_{c \bk + \bQ} - \epsilon_{v \bk}) A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) + \sum_{v',c',\bk'} K_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) A^{\bQ}_{v'c'}(\bk') = E_X A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk)
    \label{eq:exact_Bethe_Salpeter_equation}
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the energies $\{\epsilon_{n \bk}\}$ are now mean-field \qp~energies, and $K = -\qty(D - X)$ is the interaction kernel.
This \gls{bse} is similar to the one obtained with \mbpt~\cite{Jornada_PhD,Diana_PhD}.
However, our version is an already approximated version when compared to the \mbpt~one, which includes terms that render the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian non-Hermitian.
Ignoring those terms is the Tamm--Dancoff approximation~\cite{Dreuw_2005,Hirata_1999}, and it is routine in the literature.
The \gls{bse} obtained in that way resembles ours.
Nonetheless, there are two striking differences between the \bse~we solve and the one obtained within \mbpt~\cite{Jornada_PhD,Diana_PhD}.
In the latter, the problem has a frequency dependence that we do not find here.
The screening is dynamic, and that entails performing an integral in the frequency to compute the interaction matrix elements.
Here we model the screening using a static dielectric function, so we work always in the static limit $\omega \to 0$.
On one hand, this approximation is also common in \abinit~calculations in practice, although in that context it has to be justified.
On the other hand, the general case $\omega \neq 0$ hinders heavily the numerical calculations.
The other difference we can point out is that within \mbpt, the exchange term $X$ is a matrix element of the \textbf{bare} Coulomb potential, while only the direct term is a matrix element of the \textbf{screened} Coulomb potential.
In our case, both direct and exchange terms are matrix elements of the \textbf{screened} Coulomb potential, and there is no reason for the exchange term to involve the bare Coulomb potential (either way, it is usually discarded).

We have not hitherto specified the form of the Coulomb potential, even though screening was an important topic in previous chapters.
We shall discuss that right in the next section, where we explain how we can compute the interaction matrix elements in the Bethe--Salpeter equation taking screening into account.


\subsection{The interaction matrix elements and screening}
\label{subsec:interaction_matrix_elements_and_screening}

Before working out the expression for the interaction matrix elements $D$ and $X$, let us first remind the reader that we use Bloch wavefunctions, that in the lattice gauge, read

\begin{equation}
    \psi_{n, \bk} (\br) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} \sum_{i \alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \phi_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_i)\,,
\label{eq:Bloch_states_again}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the tight-binding coefficients are determined by diagonalizing the Bloch Hamiltonian (as explained in Sec.~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}), and the atomic orbitals denoted by $\phi_\alpha$ are orthogonal, as we work under the \gls{tb} formalism\footnote{In general, they are not orthogonal. If the model Hamiltonian has been obtained by a Wannier interpolation and is written in a basis of Wannier functions, then they are orthogonal by construction. However, if the Hamiltonian comes from calculations on a basis of localized functions other than Wannier functions, they may not be orthogonal. This is not a problem at the end, because one can always follow an orthogonalization procedure. The code we work with does that. We shall remind the reader again, once it comes the time of discussing the code developed.\todo[inline]{do not forget about this detail}}.
We focus first on the direct interaction term as per~\eqref{subeq:direct_term}, that in terms of Bloch states is

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:direct_term_real_space_formula}
    D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') W(\br,\br') \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br) \psi_{v \bk}(\br')\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have denoted now the screened potential by $W$ instead of $V$.
We have also omitted the region of integration, but it is implied that it is over the extension of the crystal (over its volume $\vol$ if it is \gls{3d}, or over its area $\area$ if it is \gls{2d}).
If the integration region is not explicit, then the previous statement is assumed\footnote{We justify this choice for the notation with the fact that the integrals for $D$ and $X$ will have to be computed numerically. In practice, the system has to be truncated (with periodic boundary conditions), and has as many $\bk$ points in the \gls{bz} as unit cells, $N$. The results then have to be converged with $N$, which is finite.}.
Upon substitution of the Bloch states by their expression in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_states_again}, at some point we need to evaluate four-body integrals of the form

\begin{equation}
    \int \dr \int \dr' \phi^*_{\alpha} (\br) \phi^*_{\beta} (\br') W(\br,\br') \phi_{\alpha'} (\br) \phi_{\beta'} (\br')\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the position vectors in the argument of the orbitals may have a shift in position of the form $\br \to \br - \bR - \bt_i$ that we did not write explicitly.
In \abinit~calculations, these integrals are typically evaluated by Fourier transforming the screened Coulomb potential, so that the integrals in $\br$ and $\br'$ factor out, and each integral ends up being a Fourier transform of the product between two orbitals.
Those Fourier transforms are computed numerically via fast Fourier transforms algorithms, as there are libraries providing them~\cite{FFTW05}.
However, in this work we can reduce the degree of complexity by making the point-like orbital approximation, that we have already introduced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:envelope_function_approximation} from Section~\ref{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_effective_models}.
It consists in overlooking the spatial structure of the orbitals and assuming orthogonality whenever a product between orbitals appears.
We can make the substitution

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:point-like_orbital_approximation}
    \phi_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_{i}) \phi_{\beta} (\br - \bR' - \bt_j) \approx \delta_{\alpha \beta} \delta_{\bR \bR'} \delta_{ij} \delta(\br - \bR - \bt_i)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent This allows for performing the space integrals directly and analytically, so that we obtain a relatively simple expression for the direct term:

\begin{equation}
    D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \alpha}  \sum_{j \beta} \qty(C_{i \alpha}^{c \bk+ \bQ})^* C_{i \alpha}^{c' \bk' + \bQ} \qty(C_{j \beta}^{v' \bk'})^* C_{j \beta}^{v \bk} V_{ij}(\bk' - \bk)\,,
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_real_space}
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    V_{ij}(\bk' - \bk) = \sum_{\bR} \ei{\qty(\bk' - \bk) \vdot \bR} W\qty(\bR + \bt_i; \bt_j)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which \textbf{is not} a Fourier transform.
If we model the screening using the Rytova--Keldysh potential, then we can further write $W\qty(\bR + \bt_i; \bt_j) = V_\RK (\bR + \bt_i - \bt_j)$.
The Rytova--Keldysh model is classical, as the dielectric function depends on a single momentum variable, an implication of assuming the material enjoys continuous translation invariance, which is clearly an approximation.
In the spirit of \abinit~approaches, where the matrix elements are evaluated in $\bk$-space, we can go beyond classical models for screening and take into account the inhomogeneity of the crystal.
For that, in the expression~\eqref{eq:direct_term_real_space_formula} one performs the inverse Fourier transform of the screened Coulomb potential.
According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_point_discrete_inverse_Fourier} in Appendix~\ref{app:fourier_transform}, and assuming that the system is \gls{3d} with volume $\vol$, we can write

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal}
        D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) =& \int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') W(\br,\br') \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br) \psi_{v, \bk}(\br') = \\
        =& \frac{1}{\vol} \sum_{\bq \in \mathrm{BZ}} \delta_{\bq,\bk - \bk'} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \bra{c,\bk+\bQ} \ei{(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br} \ket{c',\bk'+\bQ} W_{\bG \bG'}(\bq) \times \\
         & \bra{v',\bk'} \ee^{-\ii (\bq + \bG') \vdot \br} \ket{v \bk} = \\
        =& \frac{1}{\vol} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \qty(I^{\bG}_{c' \bk'+\bQ, c \bk+\bQ})^* W_{\bG \bG'}(\bk - \bk') I^{\bG'}_{v' \bk',v \bk}\,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the second equality was obtained by replacing the $\br$-resolved potential with its inverse Fourier transform, alongside the braket notation for the real-space integrals, and in the last equality we introduced the definition

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:plane_wave_matrix_element_definition}
    I^{\bG}_{n \bk, n' \bk'} \equiv \bra{n,\bk} \ee^{-\ii (\bk - \bk' + \bG) \vdot \br} \ket{n',\bk'} = \int \dr\, \psi^*_{n,\bk} (\br) \ee^{-\ii(\bk  - \bk' + \bG) \vdot \br} \psi_{n', \bk'} (\br)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent In this definition, if the system is \gls{3d}, then the integration is over the volume $\vol$, while if the system is \gls{2d}, then the integration is over the area $\area$.
In this way, we are ignoring the $z$ coordinate if the material is \gls{2d}.
This detail will not be a concern until we reach to Chapter~\ref{ch:screening}.

The final expression in equation~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal} is the one usually encountered in the literature pertaining to first-principle methods~\cite{BerkeleyGW,Yambo_2009}.
Those methods allow also computing the dielectric matrix and its inverse \textit{a priori}, so that one can calculate the matrix elements of the screened Coulomb potential through $W_{\bG\bG'}(\bq) = \vareps^{-1}_{\bG\bG'}(\bq) v_c(\bq + \bG')$, the static limit of Eq.~\eqref{eq:screened_dynamic_Coulomb_potential} (if dynamic screening in not implemented in the package or library).

Regarding the exchange term, even though one typically discards it, for completion we present its expression

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_exchange_reciprocal}
    \begin{split}
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') W(\br,\br') \psi_{v,\bk}(\br) \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br') = \\
                                  &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \alpha}  \sum_{j \beta} \qty(C_{i \alpha}^{c \bk+ \bQ})^*   C_{i \alpha}^{v \bk} C_{j \beta}^{c' \bk' + \bQ} \qty(C_{j \beta}^{v' \bk'})^* V_{ij}(\bQ) = \\
                                  &= \frac{1}{\vol} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \qty(I^{\bG}_{v \bk , c \bk+\bQ})^* W_{\bG \bG'}(\bQ) I^{\bG'}_{v' \bk',  c' \bk' + \bQ}\,.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

If we use for the screened Coulomb potential the Rytova--Keldysh model, which applies only to \bd~materials, and work in reciprocal space, then the expressions for the direct ($D$) and exchange ($X$) terms are given by

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_elements_reciprocal_RK}
    \begin{align}
        D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bG} \qty(I^{\bG}_{c' \bk'+\bQ, c \bk+\bQ})^* V_{\text{RK}}(\bk - \bk' + \bG) I^{\bG'}_{v' \bk',v \bk} \label{eq:direct_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK}\,\text{ and} \\
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bG} \qty(I^{\bG}_{v \bk, c \bk+\bQ})^* V_{\text{RK}}(\bQ + \bG) I^{\bG'}_{v' \bk',  c' \bk' + \bQ} \,, \label{eq:exchange_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent where $V_{\text{RK}}(\bq) = \frac{e}{2\epsz q\left(1+r_0 q\right)}$ is the momentum-resolved Rytova--Keldysh potential, encountered in this work for the first time in the Section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit}.
We also had to replace $\vol \to \area$.
A striking difference between the expressions~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_exchange_reciprocal} and~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal} and the expressions~\eqref{eq:direct_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK} and~\eqref{eq:exchange_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK}, is that in the first two there is a double sum over reciprocal lattice vectors $\bG$ and $\bG'$, while in the last two there is only a single sum over reciprocal lattice vectors, without any possibility to account for non-local field effects since they use a model potential.
The double sum introduces non-local field effects in the interaction matrix elements, while in the single sum, the inclusion of the reciprocal lattice vectors has the purpose of preserving the symmetry in the interaction between electrons~\cite{Ridolfi_2015}. % with momenta $\bk$ and $\bk'$~\cite{Ridolfi_2015}---the \bd~Fourier transform of the Rytova--Keldysh potential from the reciprocal space to the real space must involve a sum over all momenta in the reciprocal lattice to recover the correct $\br$ dependence, and the sum over all momenta can be decomposed into a sum over momenta in the \bz~with a sum over all reciprocal lattice vectors, so that the entire reciprocal lattice is covered.

\section{Optical effects in two-dimensional semiconductors}
\label{sec:optical_effects}

In this section, we provide the theory for the optical conductivity at the noninteracting level, and at the interacting level due to excitonic effects.
The expressions derived are designed for \gls{3d} materials, as they are based on the dielectric function in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency}, with the polarizability given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:polarizability_momentum_frequency} which applies to the \gls{3d} case.
Nonetheless, they can be readily adapted for \bd~materials by analogy (not quite, as one can see in Appendix~\ref{app:2D_optical_conductivity} that the permittivity--conductivity equivalence is different in the \bd~case, but we leave such details for later).
Therefore, this section serves as a technical base for what will be discussed in Chapters~\ref{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice} and~\ref{ch:screening}, when we shall put in practice the tools developed until now.

\begin{align}
    \varepsilon_{\bzero\bzero}(\bq\to\bzero,\omega) &= 1-\frac{e^{2}}{\epsz q^{2} \vol}
    \sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk}) - f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk} - \epsilon_{n,\bk}) + \ii \eta}\langle n,\bk|-\ii \bq \vdot \br |n',\bk\rangle\langle n',\bk|\ii \bq \vdot \br|n,\bk\rangle= \notag \\ % \notag suppresses the
    % equation number
    &=1-\frac{e^{2}}{\epsz \vol}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk}) - f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk} - \epsilon_{n,\bk}) + \ii \eta}\left|\uvec{e}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\br|n',\bk\rangle\right|^2 ,
\end{align}


\noindent where to arrive at the last expression, we expanded the exponential to first order in $\bq$, and defined the unit vector with the direction of $\bq$ as $\uvec{e}_{\bq}=\bq/q$.
The expansion of the exponential only up to first order is called the dipole approximation\footnote{The optical transition is induced by the dipole operator $-e\phantom{.}\br$, hence the name of the approximation.}.
It is valid as long as the wavelength is larger than typical length scales of the system, for instance, the unit cell, if the context is solid state physics.
This approximation simplifies calculations a lot in other contexts, such as optics~\cite{Hohenester_2020,Novotny_Hecht_2012,Agarwal_2012} or atomic physics~\cite{Sakurai,Zettili_2009,Foot_2004} (which, for a matter of fact, are not completely unrelated).
Using the famous relation $\varepsilon=1+\ii\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsz}$, mentioned before for the first time in Eq.~\eqref{eq:equivalent_permittivity} from Section~\ref{sec:dielectric_function_classical}, we find

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:optical_conductivity_manipulation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \sigma(\omega)&=-\ii\omega\epsz[\varepsilon(\omega)-1] = \ii\frac{\omega e^{2}}{\vol}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk} - \epsilon_{n,\bk}) + \ii \eta}\left|\uvec{e}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\br|n',\bk\rangle\right|^2 \stackrel{\textcolor{red}{!1}}{=}\\
        &=\ii\frac{\omega e^{2}}{\vol}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk} - \epsilon_{n,\bk}) + \ii \eta}\frac{\left|\uvec{e}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\left[H_0,\br\right]|n',\bk\rangle\right|^2}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}}\stackrel{\textcolor{black}{\checkmark}}{=} \\
        &=\ii\frac{\omega e^{2} \hbar^2}{\vol}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk} - \epsilon_{n,\bk}) + \ii \eta}\frac{\left|\uvec{e}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\mathbf{v}|n',\bk\rangle\right|^2}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}} \stackrel{\textcolor{black}{\checkmark}}{=} \\
        &=\frac{\ii\hbar}{\vol}\sum_{nn',\bk}\hbar\omega\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk} - \epsilon_{n,\bk}) + \ii \eta}\frac{\left|\uvec{e}_{\bq}\vdot\langle n,\bk|\mathbf{j}|n',\bk\rangle\right|^2}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}} \stackrel{\textcolor{red}{!2}}{=} \\
        &=\frac{\ii e^{2}\hbar}{\vol}\sum_{nn',\bk}\hbar\omega\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk} - \epsilon_{n,\bk}) + \ii \eta}\frac{\left|\uvec{e}_{\bq} \vdot \mel{n,\bk}{\frac{\bp}{m}}{n',\bk} \right|^2}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}}\,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent  where we have used the identities $\mathbf{v} = \frac{\ii}{\hbar} \left[H_0,\br\right]$\footnote{If the reader is not very familiar with concepts of quantum mechanics, this identity might require justification. There is in quantum mechanics the Heisenberg picture, where the states are static, but the operators evolve in time. Given an operator $\hat{O}_\mathrm{S}$ in the picture we have been working with so far, the Schr\"{o}dinger picture, the same opertor in the Heisenberg picture reads $\hat{O}_{\mathrm{H}} = \hat{U}^{\dagger} (t) \hat{O}_{\mathrm{S}}(t) \hat{U}(t)$, where $\hat{U}(t)$ is the time-evolution operator. By using the \gls{tdse}, one can show that $\frac{\dd \hat{O}_{\mathrm{H}}}{\dd t} = \ii/\hbar [H_{\mathrm{H}},\hat{O}_{\mathrm{H}}] + \left(\frac{\partial \hat{O}_{\mathrm{S}}}{\partial t}\right)_{\mathrm{H}}$. If the Hamiltonian has no explicit time-dependence, which could be that of a crystal, then one can show that if $\hat{O}_{\mathrm{S}}$ is also time-independent, then (by virtue of the time-evolution operator as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:time_evolution_operator}) $H_{\mathrm{H}} = H_{\mathrm{S}} \equiv H$ and $\frac{\dd \hat{O}_{\mathrm{H}}}{\dd t} = \ii/\hbar [H_{\mathrm{H}},\hat{O}_{\mathrm{H}}]$. This is the quantum version of the identity $\frac{\dd A}{\dd t} = \{A,H\}$ in classical mechanics, where $A$ is an observable with no explicit $t$-dependence, $H$ the Hamiltonian function and $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$ are the Poisson brackets~\cite{goldstein:mechanics,Landau1976Mechanics}.},
 $\mathbf{j}=-e\mathbf{v}$, and also $\bp = m \mathbf{v}$, with $m$ being the electron mass.
The Hamiltonian at hand $H_0$ is the Hamiltonian in the noninteracting limit.
We advert here the reader for important technical details commonly overlooked in the literature.
In the equation above, only the equalities marked with \textcolor{black}{\checkmark} are exact.
The others marked with exclamation marks are not, because in general, the position $\br$, velocity $\mathbf{v}$ and momentum $\bp$ operators are not interchangeable when doing calculations for extended systems.
In the context of atomic physics, there is no issue at all, but the same cannot be said about infinite crystalline solids.
For instance, the relation $\bp = m \mathbf{v}$ is only true if the potential entering the Hamiltonian is local\footnote{And also if \gls{soc} is not  included~\cite{Esteve_PhD}.}, since in such a case $-\ii \hbar \mathbf{v} = [H_0,\br] = [p^2/2m,\br] + [V(\br),\br] = - \frac{\ii \hbar}{ m}\bp + 0 = - \frac{\ii \hbar}{m} \bp$, by virtue of the identity $[AB,C] = A[B,C] + [A,C] B$ and the canonical commutation relation $[x_i,p_j] = - \ii \hbar \delta_{i j}$~\cite{Griffiths_2023}.
If the potential is nonlocal, an issue that may arise in \abinit~calculations using the pseudopotentials~\cite{Martin_2004}, then we have undesired terms in the commutator $[V(\br,\br'),\br] \neq 0$, making the passage marked with \textcolor{red}{!2} not exact.
Moreover, we highlighted the equality from the second line to the third line with \textcolor{red}{!1} because it is not exact as well, if the matrix elements are computed for a finite volume (with periodic boundary conditions).
If the matrix element is computed in a finite volume, according to B.~Gu and collaborators~\cite{Gu_2013}, there is an extra term $\mathcal{C}$ in the relation between the position and velocity matrix elements, reading

\begin{equation}
    \langle n,\bk|\mathbf{v}|n',\bk'\rangle_{\vol} = \frac{\ii}{\hbar} (\epsilon_{n,\bk} - \epsilon_{n',\bk'}) \langle n,\bk|\br|n',\bk'\rangle_{\vol} + \mathcal{C}_{n \bk, n' \bk'}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent with the term $\mathcal{C}_{n \bk, n' \bk'}$ coming from an integral over the boundary of the solid.
Notice that we have used the subscript $\vol$ to represent a bounded integration region.
Retaining only the first term in the \gls{rhs} is incorrect, because the integral of the position operator over a finite volume depends on the choice of the origin, while the \gls{lhs} clearly does not.
The second term on the \gls{rhs} compensates for this.
For the position matrix elements to not depend on the placement of the origin, we could extend the integral to infinity.
However, the Bloch states $\psi_{n, \bk}(\br)$ do not decay to zero at infinity, henceforth the integral is ill-defined.
To circumvent this technical difficulty, Blount in Ref.~\cite{Blount_1962} obtained an expression for the position matrix elements, where only the diagonal ones depend on the origin, by choosing a distribution normalization for the Bloch states, instead of normalizing them to a finite volume.
That is, instead of the finite case with $\langle n,\bk | n',\bk'\rangle_{\vol} = \delta_{n,n'} \delta_{\bk,\bk'}$, one has $\langle n,\bk | n',\bk'\rangle_{\infty} = \delta_{n n'} \delta(\bk-\bk')$, with the $\infty$ in subscript to denote an integral over all space.
The work by J.~J.~Esteve and J.~J.~Palacios in Ref.~\cite{Esteve_2023} showed that the relation

\begin{equation}
    \langle n,\bk|\mathbf{v}|n',\bk'\rangle_{\infty} = \frac{\ii}{\hbar} (\epsilon_{n,\bk} - \epsilon_{n',\bk'}) \langle n,\bk|\br|n',\bk'\rangle_{\infty}
\end{equation}

\noindent is true when the integrals are computed for an infinite system with open boundary conditions.
Only in this way the relation above matches with the one from atomic physics.
On a closing note, it is also possible to compute the momentum matrix elements in practice using the representation $\bp = -\ii/\hbar \nabla_{\br}$.
However, that involves taking spatial derivatives of atomic orbitals, if we work with a Bloch state basis within \gls{lcao}, which is not favorable.
We decide then to keep going with velocity or position matrix elements, which we will calculate in practice later on.
The framework consisting of using only position matrix elements is called the \underline{length gauge}.
It is called so because the interaction Hamiltonian between the material and the impinging electromagnetic field is written in terms of the dipole operator, $-e \br$.
This is valid in the dipole approximation, that we mentioned above.
On the contrary, one can work with momentum matrix elements, if we determine the light--matter interaction Hamiltonian by using the minimal substitution in the canonical momentum as $\bp \to \bq + e/c \mathbf{A}$~\cite{goldstein:mechanics}, where $\mathbf{A}$ is the vector potential~\cite{Jackson:electrodynamics}.
This is called the \underline{velocity gauge}.
Nonetheless, it is possible to go from one gauge to the other~\cite{Ventura_Peres_2017,Ventura_Peres_2018}.
For a discussion on both gauges we recommend Ref.~\cite{Pedersen_2017}, while for a more thorough discussion of all these technical details we refer to the PhD thesis by J.~J.~Esteve Parede in Ref.~\cite{Esteve_PhD}.

In the manipulation of the expression for the optical conductivity in Eq~\eqref{eq:optical_conductivity_manipulation}, we took the chance to write it in terms of the current operator as well.
We will use that expression to make a bridge between the \rpa~formalism adopted here, and the Kubo or linear response formalism~\cite{Kubo_1957}.
We start by introducing the conductivity tensor $\rttensor{\sigma}$ with components $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$ given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:conductivity_tensor_components}
    \sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\frac{\ii\hbar}{\vol}\sum_{nn',\bk}\hbar\omega\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk} - \epsilon_{n,\bk}) + \ii \eta}\frac{\langle n,\bk|j_{\alpha}|n',\bk\rangle\langle n',\bk|j_{\beta}|n,\bk\rangle}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We just generalized the matrix element of the current operator in the fourth line of Eq.~\eqref{eq:optical_conductivity_manipulation} in absolute value squared, to a product between the matrix elements of two current operator components $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
The initial conductivity can be obtained by simply extracting the diagonal component of the conductivity tensor defined just now.
In symbols, $\sigma(\omega) = \uvec{e}_{\bq} \vdot \rttensor{\sigma} \vdot \uvec{e}_{\bq}$.
We manipulate now the components of the conductivity tensor.
By introducing the Sokhotski--Plemelj identity~\cite{Muskhelishvili_1977}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sokhotski_plemelj}
    \frac{1}{(x-a) \pm \ii \eta}=\mathrm{P.V.}\!\left(\frac{1}{x-a}\right) \mp \ii\pi\delta(x-a),\quad \eta=0^+,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathrm{P.V.}$ denotes Cauchy principal value, in the first fraction inside the sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:conductivity_tensor_components}, we obtain the so-called Kubo--Greenwood formula for the real part of the conductivity

\begin{equation}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi \hbar}{\vol}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}}  j^{\alpha}_{n n'}(\bk)  j^{\beta}_{n' n}(\bk) \delta[\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})] \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent if written in terms of the current operator matrix elements $j^{\alpha}_{n n'}(\bk) \equiv \langle n,\bk|j_{\alpha}|n',\bk\rangle$, where we also did the trick of replacing $\hbar \omega \to \epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}$ in the first factor inside the sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:conductivity_tensor_components} to cancel the frequency in the numerator, which is allowed due to the presence of the Dirac delta function.
That is why the difference $\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}$ in the denominator appears with power one, and not two.
The Kubo--Greenwood formula for the conductivity is actually a particular case of the more general Kubo formula, which accounts for all possible state transitions among all many-body states of the system~\cite{Mahan,Coleman_2015}.
Being in practice impossible to calculate at the full interacting level, normally some sort of approximation is needed.
The Kubo--Greenwood formula is a particular case of the more general Kubo formula~\cite{Mahan}, accounting only for transitions between single-particle states---this is called the \gls{ipa}~\cite{Aversa_Sipe_1995}.
Particularizing for a semiconductor at zero temperature, such that the Fermi--Dirac distribution becomes a Heaviside step function, and simplifying, we can write the real part of the conductivity tensor, now in terms of velocity operator matrix elements, as

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:non_interacting_Re_optical_conductivity}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi e^{2}\hbar}{\vol}\sum_{vc,\bk} \frac{v^{\alpha}_{c v}(\bk)  v^{\beta}_{v c}(\bk)}{\epsilon_{c \bk}-\epsilon_{v \bk}}   \delta[\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{c \bk}-\epsilon_{v \bk})]
\end{empheq}

\noindent where we used for the velocity matrix elements the notation $v^{\alpha}_{c v}(\bk) \equiv \langle c,\bk|v_{\alpha}|v,\bk\rangle$.
At this point we will not explain how to compute the position or velocity matrix elements in practice.
They will be addressed in two different ways in chapters~\ref{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice} and~\ref{ch:screening}, so we postpone the explanation to those two chapters.


\subsection{Excitonic optical conductivity}
\label{subsec:excitonic_conductivity}

In this section we are concerned with the contribution of the excitons to the optical conductivity.
Only going beyond the \gls{ipa} can we compare the theoretical predictions with the optical measurements for frequencies lying inside the bandgap, as we discussed in the Section~\ref{sec:introduction} of this chapter.

If the material is subject to a drive from a spatially uniform time-dependent electric field composed of a single harmonic $\omega$, the linear optical conductivity, according to the work of T.~Pedersen in~\cite{Pedersen_2015}, is given by

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{align}
    \sigma (\omega) = \frac{e^2}{8 \pi^3 \ii \hbar} \sum_{\nu} \qty[E_{\nu} \frac{\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} \vdot \vectorbold{\Omega}^*_{\nu} }{E_{\nu} - \hbar \omega + \ii \hbar \Gamma} + E_{\nu} \frac{\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} \vdot \vectorbold{\Omega}^*_{\nu} }{E_{\nu} + \hbar \omega - \ii \hbar \Gamma}]
    \label{eq:optical_conductivity_def}
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the sum is over the excitonic states, $E_{\nu}$ being the energy of the $\nu$-th state, and $\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu}$ is the Berry curvature given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Berry_curvature_definition}
    \vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} = \sum_{cv,\bk} \psi^{\nu}_{c v} (\bk) \langle v, \bk | \br \vdot \hat{\vectorbold{e}} | c, \bk \rangle,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\br$ is the dipole operator, $\uvec{e}$ is the polarization unit vector, and $\psi^{\nu}_{c v} (\bk)$ is the excitonic wavefunction for the pair of bands $(c,v)$.
If, once more, we wish to define the optical conductivity as a tensor, then we can deduce an expression similar to~\eqref{eq:non_interacting_Re_optical_conductivity}, we just have to replace the transition energy $\epsilon_{c \bk}-\epsilon_{v \bk}$ by the exciton energy $E_{\nu}$, and the velocity matrix elements by their interacting version given by a weighted sum over momentum of the noninteracting matrix elements, with the weight coinciding with the excitons wavefunction, similarly to what is written in~\eqref{eq:Berry_curvature_definition}.
We can therefore write\todo{Caution with this expression, since the material is \bd, come back here again after chapters 5 and 6.}

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{align}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi e^{2}\hbar}{\vol}\sum_{\nu}\frac{1}{E_{\nu}} V^{\alpha}_{\nu} (V^{\beta}_{\nu})^{*} \delta(\hbar \omega - E_{\nu})
    \label{eq:excitonic_Re_optical_conductivity}
\end{empheq}

\noindent if we define the interacting velocity matrix elements as $V^{\alpha}_{\nu} = \sum_{cv,\bk} \psi^{\nu}_{c v} (\bk) v^{\alpha}_{c v}(\bk) $.
