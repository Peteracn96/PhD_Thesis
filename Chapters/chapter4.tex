\chapter{Excitonic effects in Two-dimensional Semiconductors}
\label{ch:Excitons}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Optical spectroscopy is an important experimental tool for studying the band structure of crystals.
Its utility stems from the possibility for a crystal to absorb a photon with energy $\hbar \omega$ by exciting an electron.
If the crystal is an insulator, and the photon energy exceeds the \textit{electronic} band gap, then the electron can be promoted to the conduction band, leaving a hole in the valence band, experiencing a change in energy of $\hbar \omega = E_c(\bk) - E_v(\bk)$, where $E_v(\bk)$ was the energy the electron had while populating the valence band $v$, and $E_c(\bk)$ the energy the electron has after being excited to the empty conduction band $c$, just like depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation}.
This direct interband transition may occur any point $\bk$ in the Brillouin zone for which energy is conserved.
But it can also happen that the electron absorbs the photon energy if it is just below the gap energy.
The minimum amount of energy required for that to happen is what defines the \textit{optical} band gap.
This confers a structure to the absorption and reflection spectra for energies inside the band gap, 
which can only be explained at the interacting level beyond the single-particle picture, when we allow for the valence electrons experience Coulomb repulsion.
As we will see in section ???, a repulsive interaction between the electrons can actually give rise to an attractive one, between the electron in the conduction band (red circle in Fig.~\ref{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation}) and the hole it left behind in the valence band (red circunference filled with white in Fig.~\ref{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation}).
This attraction is in the origin of the formation of a bound electron--hole pair, the so-called exciton.
The binding energy of a given exciton in a state $\nu$, $E_{b, \nu}$, is then difference between the energy of the electronic band gap, $\Delta$, and the optical one, $E_X$, which coincides with the energy of the excitonic ground state (measuring the energies setting the zero at the top of the valence band).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{Chapters/Figures/Chapter_4/exciton_formation}
    \mycaption[Formation of an electron--hole pair.]{Formation of an electron--hole pair.}
    \label{fig:electron_hole_pair_formation}
\end{figure}

The excitonic problem, consisting in finding the excitonic eigenenergies and eigenstates or wavefunctions, coincides with the hydrogen atom problem if the energy surfaces for the electron and the hole are spherical and nondegenerate, as stated in the book by Kittel~\cite{Kittel_2004}.
From a heuristic point of view, the energy levels are given by a modified Rydberg formula

\begin{equation}
    E_{X,n} = E_g - \frac{R^*}{n^2},
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    R^* = \frac{2 \mu^2 e^4}{\hbar^2 (8 \pi \varepsilon)^2}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the modified Rydberg constant, which instead of using the electron mass like usual, it uses the reduced mass between the electron and the hole effective masses:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{m_e} + \frac{1}{m_h}.
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $\varepsilon$ is just the dielectric constant of the medium.
This description is suited for weakly bound excitons, also called Wannier excitons.
The characteristic size of the exciton is given by a modified expression for the Bohr radius:

\begin{equation}
    a_B^* = \frac{4 \pi \epsilon \hbar^2}{\mu^2 e^2}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which can extend over a couple of atomic sites.
If the exciton is tightly bound, then it is called a Frenkel exciton.
In this case, the excitation is localized at a single atom.
Wannier excitons can be found in inorganic semiconductors, like cuprous oxide, \ch{Cu2O}, or  gallium arsenide, \ch{GaAs}.
For \ch{GaAs}, the binding energy of the ground state exciton is $3.4$ \unit{\milli\electronvolt}.
Frenkel excitons are more common in crystalline inert gases, like crystalline krypton.
In solid krypton, the ground state exciton has a binding energy of $1.5$ \unit{\electronvolt}.
\todo{find references for the numbers above.}

If we wish to apply the description above of Wannier excitons to 2D materials, the excitonic binding energies are given now by the modified Rydberg formula for the 2D hydrogen atom

\begin{equation}
    E^{\BD}_{X,n} = - \frac{R^*}{\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^2}.
\end{equation}

Focusing on the ground state $n=1$, we can see that $E^{\text{2D}}_{X,n=1} = 4 E_{X,n=1}$.
Furthermore, the Bohr radius for 2D materials is half of that of the 3D case.
We can then predict that the exciton contribution to the optical spectrum is more prominent in 2D materials when compared to 3D ones.
Furthermore, the Coulomb interaction in 2D materials is less screened than in 3D ones.
In 2D materials excitons can form even at room temperatures~\cite{Morozov_Wolff_Mortensen_2021}.

In a more accurate approach to the excitonic problem, the excitonic states and respective binding energies can be estimated by solving the Wannier equation~\cite{Wannier_1937} that writes

\begin{equation}
    E_{b, \nu} \psi_\nu (\br) =  \frac{p ^2}{2 \mu} \psi_\nu (\br) - V(\br) \psi_\nu (\br),
\label{eq:Wannier_equation}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu$ is the electron--hole reduced effective mass as before, and $V(\br)$ is the attractive potential that binds the electron and the hole together.
It is just like the time-independent Schrödinger equation, but with an effective reduced mass.
In 2D materials, the potential that describes accurately the screening effect has the form of the Rytova--Keldysh potential, that was already derived in section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit} when we were introducing classical effective models for the dielectric function. 

The Wannier equation does not capture the full structure of the excitonic states (like nondegenerate $p$ states, or the symmetries of the crystal), hence we need to resort to more accurate descriptions of the excitonic problem.
That is possible using a reciprocal space description of the excitonics, with oone can write the \gls{bse}---an integral equation where finer details of the band structure and the Coulomb interaction are included.
In this work, we mention two versions of the \gls{bse}.
One for effective low-energy models, and another one using exact diagonalization of the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian.
The \abinit~version of the \gls{bse} is not addressed here.
Besides being technically more involved it is  extremely computationally demanding, so it is instructive to look for more efficient approaches.
Nonetheless it can be frequently encountered in the literature, in particular works that employ \abinit~methods for the optical response of materials\todo{include citations and refer textbooks}.
If the reader is curious about it, we refer to.

In the following section we derive the Bethe--Salpeter equation for effective models, and after derive the Wannier equation from it.


\section{The Bethe--Salpeter Equation: application to effective models}
\label{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_effective_models}

For a two band model, we define the exciton creation operator as

\begin{equation}
    b_\nu^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) c_{\bk, c}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, v}.
\end{equation}

\noindent $A$ is the area of the monolayer, $\phi_{\nu}(\bk)$ is the Fourier transform of the exciton wave function (unknown at this point), and $\nu$ is the set of the exciton quantum numbers.

Here we assume only vertical transitions due to the small momentum carried by the photon.
We denote by $\ket{\GS}$ the excitonic ground (or vacuum) state of the material, with a completely filled valence band and an empty conduction band.
The operator $b_{\nu}^{\dagger}$ acting on the ground state creates an exciton in the state $| X_\nu \rangle$:

\begin{equation}
    b_{\nu}^{\dagger}|\GS\rangle=|1_{v \bk_1},1_{v \bk_2},...,1_{v \bk_f},\underset{\underset{X_\nu}{\downarrow}}{1},0,0,..,0_{c \bk_1},0_{c \bk_2},...,0_{c\bk_f}\rangle,
\end{equation}

\noindent which has energy $E_{\nu}$.
Since the operators $b$ and $b^{\dagger}$ are quadratic in fermionic operators, they obey commutation relations, hence they are of bosonic nature.

The Hamiltonian of the system

\begin{equation}
    \hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \hat{V}
\end{equation}

\noindent has two components, the kinetic one ($\lambda=v,c$ is the band index),

\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_0 = \sum_{\bk, \lambda} E_{\bk, \lambda} c_{\bk, \lambda}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, \lambda},
\end{equation}

\noindent and the interacting one, in terms of the field operators, writes

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_field_operators}
    \hat{V} = \frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\br\int\mathrm{d}\br'\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\left(\br\right)\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\left(\br'\right)V\left(\br - \br'\right)\hat{\psi}\left(\br'\right)\hat{\psi}\left(\br\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent if we ignore the spin degree of freedom.
In this work we will not worry with spniful excitons, so there is no need to include spin indices in the field and fermionic operators.
To convert it to momentum space, we use the expansion of the field operators in terms of Bloch states

\begin{equation}
    \hat{\psi}\left(\br\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bk,\lambda}\braket{\br\,}{\bk,\lambda} c_{\bk,\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bk,\lambda}\psi_{\bk,\lambda}\left(\br\right)c_{\bk,\lambda}
\end{equation}

\noindent and writing the potential in terms of its Fourier transform

\begin{equation}
    V\left(\br - \br'\right) = \sum_{\bq} \ee^{\ii \bq \vdot(\br-\br')} V(\bq)\,.
\end{equation}

Replacing everything in~\eqref{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_field_operators}, we get

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_momentum_space_substitution}
    \hat{V}=\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{\bq}\sum_{\{\bk_{i}\}}
    \sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}
    V(\bq)
    \left(\int\psi_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)\ee^{\ii\bq\vdot\br}\mathrm{\psi_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br)d}\br\right)\left(\int\psi_{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}^{*}(\br')\ee^{-\ii\bq\vdot\br'}\mathrm{\psi_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}(\br')d}\br'\right)c_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}
\end{equation}

\noindent or using braket notation

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_hamiltonian_momentum_space_substitution_option_2}
    \hat{V}=\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{\bq}\sum_{\{\bk_{i}\}}
    \sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}
    V(\bq)
    \mel{\bk_1,\lambda_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\bk_4,\lambda_4} \mel{\bk_2,\lambda_2}{\ei[-]{\bq \vdot \br'}}{\bk_3,\lambda_3} c_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}
\end{equation}

An auxiliary calculation for the matrix elements follows.
The Bloch wavefunctions, in the atomic gauge that we used to write hBN’s TB Bloch Hamiltonian, have the form

\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\bk,\lambda}\left(\br\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\br,i\alpha}\ee^{\ii\bk\vdot(\br+\bt_{i})}C_{i\alpha}^{\bk,\lambda}\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)
\end{equation}


So now we can compute the matrix elements

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mel{\bk_1,\lambda_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\bk_4,\lambda_4} &=\int\psi_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)\ee^{\ii\bq\vdot\br}\mathrm{\psi_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br)\,d}\br = \\
        & = \int u_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bk_{1}+\bk_{4})\vdot\br}u_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br) \mathrm{d}\br\approx \\
        & = \left \langle u_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)u_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br) \right\rangle_{\mathrm{UC}} \underbrace{\int\ee^{\ii(\bq-\bk_{1}+\bk_{4})\vdot\br}\mathrm{d}\br}_{A\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}}} \,.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we employed the envelope function approximation.
It consists in replacing the product of periodic parts of the Bloch functions by their average over the unit cell.
This approximation assumes that the periodic part of the Bloch function varies fast over the length scale of the unit cell, while the exponential part varies slowly.
The average of the periodic part of the Bloch wavefunctions can be computed, also approximately using the point-like orbital approximation, by


\begin{align*}
    \left\langle u_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)u_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br)\right\rangle _{\mathrm{UC}} & =\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\int u_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{*}(\br)u_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}(\br)\:\mathrm{d}\br=\\
    & =\frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\int\left(\sum_{\bR,i\alpha}\ee^{-\ii\bk_{1}\vdot(\br+\bt_{i}-\br)}\left(C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*}\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)\right) \\
    & \left(\sum_{\bR',j\beta}\ee^{\ii\bk_{4}\vdot(\br'+\bt_{j}-\br)}C_{j\beta}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\phi_{\beta}\left(\br-\bR'-\bt_{j}\right)\right)\:\mathrm{d}\br=\\
    & =\frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\sum_{\bR,\bR}\ee^{-\ii\bk_{1}\vdot(\br+\bt_{i})}\ee^{\ii\bk_{4}\vdot(\br'+\bt_{j})}\sum_{i\alpha,j\beta}\left(C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*}C_{j\beta}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} \\
    &\int\ee^{\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot\br}\underbrace{\phi_{\alpha}\left(\br-\bR-\bt_{i}\right)\phi_{\beta}\left(\br-\bR'-\bt_{j}\right)}_{\approx\delta_{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\br\br'}\delta(\br-\bR-\bt_{i})}\:\mathrm{d}\br\approx\\
    & \approx \frac{1}{N A_{\mathrm{UC}}} \sum_{\bR} \cancel{\ee^{-\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot(\br+\bt_{i})}} \sum_{i\alpha,j\beta} \left(C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*} C_{j\beta}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}} \cancel{\ee^{\ii(\bk_{1}-\bk_{4})\vdot(\br+\bt_{i})}} =\\
    & =\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\sum_{i\alpha}\left(C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*}C_{i\alpha}^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\left(\bC^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}},\text{ since }\sum_{\bR}1=N.
\end{align*}

\noindent Finally, the plane-wave matrix element writes

\begin{equation}
    \mel{\bk_1,\lambda_1}{\ei{\bq \vdot \br}}{\bk_4,\lambda_4} = \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\left(\bC^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{*}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}A\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}}=N\left(\bC^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}},
\end{equation}

\noindent while for the other matrix element the same reasoning applies.
In this way, the interaction term of the Hamiltonian, in momentum space, writes


\begin{align*}
    \hat{V} & =\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{\bq}\sum_{\{\bk_{i}\}}\sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}V\text{(\ensuremath{\bq})}N^{2}\left(\bC^{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\delta_{\bq,\bk_{1}-\bk_{4}}\left(\bC^{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}\delta_{\bq,\bk_{3}-\bk_{2}}c_{\bk_{1},\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{2},\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}=\\
    & =\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{\bq\bk_{3}\bk_{4}}\sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}V\text{(\ensuremath{\bq})}N^{2}\left(\bC^{\bk_{4}+\bq,\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}\left(\bC^{\bk_{3}-\bq,\lambda_{2}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4}+\bq,\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3}-\bq,\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}}=\\
    & =\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bq,\bk_{3},\bk_{4}}\sum_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}V\text{(\ensuremath{\bq})}F_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}}\left(\bk_{3},\bk_{4},\bq\right)c_{\bk_{4}+\bq,\lambda_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3}-\bq,\lambda_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{\bk_{3},\lambda_{3}}c_{\bk_{4},\lambda_{4}},
\end{align*}

\noindent where we have defined the form factor $F_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}}\left(\bk,\bp,\bq\right) = \left(\bC^{\bp+\bq,\lambda_{1}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bp,\lambda_{4}}\left(\bC^{\bk-\bq,\lambda_{2}}\right)^{\dagger}\bC^{\bk,\lambda_{3}}$.

For simplicity, we can take spinors of the low-energy Bloch Hamiltonian for the coefficients $C^{\bk,\lambda}_{i \alpha}$.
We assume that the system can be described by the bosonic $b$ operators, and that the numbers $\nu$ are good quantum numbers, that is, the Hamiltonian is diagonal in terms of these operators in this basis:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_X = \sum_{\nu} E_{\nu} b_{\nu}^{\dagger} b_{\nu}.
\end{equation}

\noindent Now the strategy is the following: we take the commutator between $\hat{H}_X$ and the creation operator $b_{\nu}^{\dagger}$, $[\hat{H}_X, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]$; next, we take the same commutator, but with the initial total Hamiltonian instead (this step is the most involved one); finally, we equate both results---the Bethe--Salpeter equation is then automatically extracted.
The first commutator is straightforward:

\begin{equation}
    [\hat{H}, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = E_{\nu} b_\nu^{\dagger} = E_\nu \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_\nu(\bk) c_{\bk, c}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, v}.
\end{equation}

Now in terms of fermionic operators, we need to compute

\begin{equation}
[\hat{H}, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = [\hat{H}_0, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] + [\hat{H}_{\text{int}}, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}],
\end{equation}

\noindent but we better compute the two contributions separately.
First the commutator with the kinetic component:

\begin{equation}
    [\hat{H}_0, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = \Bigg[\sum_{\bk, \lambda} E_{\bk, \lambda} c_{\bk, \lambda}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, \lambda},\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\bq} \phi_{\nu}(\bq) c_{\bq,c}^{\dagger} c_{\bq,v} \Bigg] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\bk} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) (E_{\bk,c}-E_{\bk,v}) c_{\bk, c}^{\dagger} c_{\bk, v}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent after simplifying.
Now we do the commutator with the interacting term.
This task is not hard, just very tedious.
So we describe briefly by words the key steps, and present the final result.
For the commutator $\left[\hat{H}_{\text{int}}, b_\nu^{\dagger}\right]$, we have to compute the commutator between an operator quartic in $c$ with one quadratic in $c$---four contributions arise.
Each contribution is an operator quartic in $c$.
To make it comparable with the very first commutator, we need to introduce an approximation that turns the quartic operators into quadratic ones, which is the mean-field approximation.
Applying this approximation to one of the initial contributions results in four terms.
We had four contributions, so in total we have sixteen terms.
In each of these sixteen terms, we have to evaluate an expectation value of operators of the form $c^\dagger c$.
These expectation values are simply equilibrium thermal averages, and at zero temperature for simplicity\footnote{In the eventuality of the gapped material has a gap so low that it is considered a semiconductor, then considering $T = 0 $ K simplifies the analysis a lot.}.
Therefore, we can apply the rule

\begin{equation}
    \langle c_{\bk, \lambda_1}^{\dagger} c_{\bk', \lambda_2}  \rangle =
    \begin{cases}
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = c, \\
        \delta_{\bk,\bk'} \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = v, \\
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = c \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_2 = v, \\
        0 \quad \text{if} \quad \lambda_1 = v \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_2 = c.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent After that, we need to gather all the contributions, while recovering the sums in momentum to simplify the expression---equal terms simply add up, or just cancel.
Exploring the properties of the form factor (we can change the order in which the $\bC$ arrays appear in a smart way).
To arrive at the final result, a couple of shifts in momentum might also be in order.
At the end, we can set $[\hat{H}_X, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] = [\hat{H}_0, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}] + [\hat{H}_{\text{int}}, b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]$, from which we can extract the Bethe--Salpeter equation for the exciton wavefunction $\phi_\nu(\bk)$ and its respective energy $E_\nu$:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        E_\nu \phi_\nu(\bk)  =&   \phi_{\nu}(\bk) (E_{\bk,c}-E_{\bk,v}) \\
        & - \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V_{\bk-\bp} F_{cvvc}(\bk,\bp,\bk-\bp)   \\
        & + \frac{1}{A} \phi_{\nu}(\bk) \sum_{\bp} V_{\bp} \left[F_{vvvv}(\bp + \bk ,\bk,\bp) - F_{vcvc}(\bp + \bk,\bk,\bp) \right].
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

We can interpret the last term as a renormalization of the single-particle bands when electron-electron interactions are accounted for, shifting the bands in energy, so it can be simply absorbed into the first term.
It does not mean that it can be ignored, but we sweep it under the rug this time.
Doing so, we arrive at the simplified form of the Bethe--Salpeter equation:

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{equation}
    E_\nu \phi_\nu(\bk)  =  (E_{\bk,c}-E_{\bk,v}) \phi_{\nu}(\bk)  - \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \phi_\nu (\bp) V_{\bk-\bp} F_{cvvc}(\bk,\bp,\bk-\bp)
    \label{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation}
\end{empheq}

\noindent An extension to multi-band systems is straightforward, but we refrain from doing so.
The multi-band case is to be considered only in the next section.

In this section we will derive the Wannier equation as in (\ref{eq:Wannier_equation}) from the BSE (we will focus only on the two bands case). For that we need to introduce two approximations. First we make the approximation of parabolic bands near zero momentum, that is,

\begin{equation}
    E_{\bk,\lambda} \simeq E_{\vectorbold{0},\lambda} + \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 m_{\lambda}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\lambda$ is a band index and $m_\lambda$ is the effective mass in band $\lambda$. It follows that

\begin{equation}
    E_{\bk,c}-E_{\bk,v} \simeq E_g + \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 \mu},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $E_g =  E_{\vectorbold{0},c}-E_{\vectorbold{0},v}$ is the energy difference between the top and bottom of the valence and conduction bands, respectively, and $\mu$ is the electron--hole effective reduced mass, as usual.
in the right hand side of (\ref{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation}) to 1, which is valid for large band gaps. In this way equation (\ref{eq:Bethe_Salpeter_equation}) reads

\begin{equation}
    E_{b,\nu} \phi_{\nu} (\bk) = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2 \mu} \phi_{\nu} (\bk) - \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \phi_{\nu} (\bp) V_{\bk - \bp},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $ E_{b,\nu} = E_{\nu} - Eg$ is the binding energy of the excitonic state $\nu$.
Now we apply a Fourier transform to the wavefunction

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\nu} (\bk) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\br} \ei{ \bk \vdot \br} \phi_{\nu} (\br)
\end{equation}

\noindent and to the potential

\begin{equation}
    V_{\bk - \bp} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{A}} \sum_{\br'} \ei{ (\bk - \bp) \vdot \br'} V (\br')
\end{equation}

\noindent and use the identity

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{A} \sum_{\bp} \ei{ \bp \vdot (\br - \br')} = \delta_{\br,\br'},
\end{equation}

\noindent to recover the Wannier equation:

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{equation}
    E_{b, \nu} \psi_\nu (\br) = \frac{p^2}{2 \mu} \psi_\nu (\br) - V(\br) \psi_\nu (\br)
    \label{eq:Wannier_equation_derived}
\end{empheq}

The excitonic problem is a differential equation in real space, and an integral equation in momentum space.
The real space approach is more useful for analytic treatments, but it overlooks some features due to the approximations, like the lack of degeneracy between excitonic states with symmetric angular quantum numbers, since in the real space approach we set $F$ to 1.
The momentum space approach is heavier numerically and more intricate, but produces more accurate results.

\section{The Bethe--Salpeter Equation: exact diagonalization}
\label{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_exact}

In this section we dwell on a second principles approach to the calculation of the excitonic spectrum of insulators and semiconductors, within an atomistic description where the tight-binding approximation discussed previously is key.
The discussion is adapted from~\cite{Xatu} and \textcolor{red}{Alex's thesis to include in the references}.
This approach presents technical and computational advantages to first principles based methodologies, something pretty much established in the literature, such as the \GW-\bse~ method~\cite{Louie_2000,BerkeleyGW,Diana_PhD,Jornada_PhD}.
Even though more numerically inefficient as solving the Wannier equation or the\bse~applied to effective models, this method is still much more efficent as \abinit~ones, but nonetheless we can expect with it more detailed insights into the excitonic problem.
It can be useful if, for instance, one is interested in the symmetries of the excitonic states, which in principle are inherited from the symmetries of the crystal, something we cannot capture with effective models, since the symmetries of the initial Hamiltonian may not be preserved when taking the low-energy limit.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Hamiltonian_start}
    \Ha= \Ho + \V = \sum_{i,j} t_{i,j} \cdagger_i c_j + \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{i,j,k,l} \cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the first term corresponds to the interaction between the valence electrons and the ionic species constituting the crystalline structure of the solid at hand (but freezing their degrees of freedom), and the second term corresponds to the electrostatic Coulomb interaction between the electrons.
The single-particle physics is encoded in $\Ho$, while many-particle physics, in particular excitons, is encoded in $\V$, just as in the previous section.
However, instead of departing from the many-body Hamiltonian in real space in terms of field operators, we depart from a Hamiltonian still in real space, but the working basis is an atomistic one, that is, we adopt the tight-binding description from section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method} of the previous chapter.
However, there we considered only the single-particle properties of crystalline solids, while here we turn on now the two-body interactions.
Therefore, the sums in Eq.\eqref{eq:Hamiltonian_start} run over indices including orbitals and position degrees of freedom (and eventually spin).
The sums in Eq.\eqref{eq:Hamiltonian_start} run over indices including orbitals and position degrees of freedom (and eventually spin).
The parameters $t_{i,j}$ are the hopping or tight-binding parameters, while $V_{i,j,k,l}$ are the interaction matrix elements\footnote{Note that by matrix elements, we mean the symbols $V_{i,j,k,l}$, which are matrix elements of the first quantized interaction term $V$ between single-particle states belonging to the one-particle Hilbert space, and not matrix elements of the second quantized operator $\V$ between states living in the Fock space.}

\begin{equation}
    V_{i,j,k,l} = \mel{kl}{V}{ij} = \int \dr \int \dr' \phi^*_i(\br) \phi^*_j(\br') V(\br,\br') \phi_k(\br) \phi_l(\br'),
\end{equation}

\noindent At this point, we do not particularize how we model the screening, and postpone that for later on.

\subsection{The Bethe--Salpeter equation rederived}

\todo{say somewhere that this approach is variational}

Now, with the purpose of describing excitons, we need to start from a good enough description of the ground state of the system, $\ket{\GS}$.
For undoped 2D semiconductors or insulators, whose ground state consists of all the valence bands completely filled, and all the conduction bands empty, we can write mathematically for the groundstate, or Fermi sea,

\begin{equation}
    \ket{\GS} = \prod_{n,\bk}^{\epsilon_{n \bk} \leq \EF} c_{n \bk}^\dagger \ket{0} \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\ket{0}$ denotes the \enquote{vacuum} state (the crystal in the absence of electrons), and $\EF$ is the Fermi energy.
Exciting an electron from the valence band $v$ with momentum $\bk$ to the conduction band $c$ with momentum $\bk+\bQ$, we are before an electron--hole pair, whose state can be formally written as

\begin{equation}
    \ket{v,c,\bk,\bQ} \equiv \cdagger_{c, \bk+\bQ} c_{v, \bk} \ket{\GS}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent As a passing note, there is no need in our description for adopting a quasi-particle hole description for the holes.
We see it simply as the absence of a negative elementary charge in the Fermi sea.

If the exciton has center of mass momentum $\bQ$,and assuming that a basis of electron--hole pairs is appropriate for the excitonic states, then we write for excitonic state $\ket{X_{\bQ}}$

\begin{equation}
    \ket{X_{\bQ}} = \sum_{v,c,\bk} A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \ket{v,c,\bk,\bQ} = \sum_{v,c,\bk} A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \cdagger_{c, \bk+\bQ} c_{v, \bk} \ket{\GS} \,.
\end{equation}

In this way, the exciton state is written as a linear combination of electron--hole pair states summing over valence bands, conduction bands and momentum.
We can consider the quantum number $\bQ$ a good one since the interaction conserves momentum, as all electron--hole pairs share the same center of mass momentum $\bQ$.
The expansion coefficients $A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk)$ are determined by minimization of the expectation value (omitting the $\bQ$ subscript momentarily) $\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}$ with respect to $\ket{X}$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expectation_value_exciton}
    \fdv{E[X]}{X} = \fdv{X}  \left[\frac{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{\braket{X}{X}}\right] = \fdv{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{X} \braket{X}{X} - \underbrace{ \frac{\mel{X}{\Ha}{X}}{\braket{X}{X}}}_{E_X}  \fdv{\braket{X}{X}}{X} = 0 \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Upon performing the variational derivatives and defining the following symbol $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) \equiv \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ha}{v',c',\bk',\bQ}$ for the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian between two electron--hole pair states, we arrive at the equation

\begin{equation}
   \sum_{v',c',\bk'} H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) A^{\bQ}_{v'c'}(\bk') = E_X A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which is a different way of writing the \bse, and the symbols $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)$ are matrix elements of what we call the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian.

The expansion of the excitonic state in a basis of electron--hole pairs is in fact an ansatz, or an assumption that the basis is adequate enough (or complete, technically speaking) to be able to write the true excitonic states.
Instead of considering all the possible combinations of electron--hole excitations, where several electrons would populate the conduction bands leaving as many holes behind in the valence bands, instead of doing the calculation in the full Fock space, something unfeasible in practice, we are do the calculations within a reduced subspace of the full Fock space, spanned by only states where only one electron from a valence band with momentum $\bk$ is excited to a conduction band with momentum $\bk + \bQ$.

We need to compute the matrix elements $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)$.
Naturally, it has to be done numerically in practice, which also means that we have to truncate the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian and work with an approximate finite matrix representation.
Physically speaking, this approximation means that we are considering only a finite number of valence and conduction bands, and a finite number of momentum points in the \bz~(or unit cells, equivalently).
Mathematically, it means that we are working with a truncated basis for $\Ho$, which in practice is not complete.
In other words, we have to compute the matrix elements for each pair of momentum points $(\bk,\bk')$ in the \bz~mesh, for each pair $(vc,v'c')$ of $(vc)$ pairs of valence and conduction bands.
The results later on will have to be converged against the number of points in the \bz~and the number of valence and conduction bands included, in oder to obtain the expected degeneracies and symmetries.
Obviously, we select only the valence and conduction bands more relevant for the excitonic physics, those closer to the Fermi level $\EF$.

Even if they are to be evaluated numerically at the end, let us see how far we can go with only analytical expressions.
The Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian matrix elements are given by $H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ha}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ho}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} + \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ}$, matrix elements between states of uncoupled electron--hole pairs.
The first term corresponds to the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian at the mean-field level between two electron--hole pair states, while the second term corresponds to the interaction matrix elements.
We work out first the matrix elements of $\Ho$, as they are easier to deal with.
For that, let us assume that the operator $\Ho$  can be written in a spectral representation of Bloch states labeled by $\ket{n,\bk}$.
Then, in its second-quantized form, the single-particle Hamiltonian reads

\begin{equation}
   \Ho = \sum_{n,\bk} \epsilon_{n \bk} \cdagger_{n \bk} c_{n \bk}\,,
\label{eq:Hamiltonian_single_particle}
\end{equation}

\noindent assuming that the process of diagonalizing it has been effectuated.
Replacing the expression above in the matrix element of $\Ho$, it is not hard to show that

\begin{equation}
    \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\Ho}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = \qty(\epsilon_{c, \bk + \bQ} - \epsilon_{v, \bk}) \delta_{v,v'} \delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{c,c'} \qq{up to a global shift of}\EF \,.
\end{equation}

The interaction matrix elements are tricky to compute, as the interaction operator $\V$ is a two-body operator, quartic in the creation and annihilation operators.
Nonetheless, in this section we refrain from performing any kind of mean-field approximation, and compute the matrix elements exactly.
Doing so is not hard, but it is, once again, very tedious.
We do not perform all the steps, but just explain briefly how to proceed.
For convenience, we make a brief change and abuse of notation, and relabel as $(v',\bk') \to v'$, $(c',\bk' + \bQ) \to c'$, $(v,\bk) \to v$ and $(c,\bk + \bQ) \to c$, and keep the notation for $\V$ used above.
Then we have

\begin{align*}
    \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} &= \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{i,j,k,l} \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = \\
    &= \sum_{i,j,k,l} V_{i,j,k,l} \mel{\GS}{\cdagger_{v} c_c \cdagger_i \cdagger_j c_l c_k \cdagger_{v'} c_{c'}}{\GS}\,.
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element}
\end{align*}

\noindent We pick, for instance, the pair $c_c \cdagger_i$ and use the anti-commutation relations to move the creation operator to the left, and the annihilation operator to the right, giving rise to two new terms.
Then, in each term, we select a new pair of creation and annihilation operators $\cdagger c$, and repeat the process, making more new terms appear.
Following this procedure recursively, until we exhaust all our options for pairs of creation and annihilation  operators, and end up only with a matrix element of a single $\cdagger c$ pair to evaluate, we arrive at

\begin{align*}
    \mel{v,c,\bk,\bQ}{\V}{v',c',\bk',\bQ} = &V_{c'vv'c} - V_{c'vcv'} + \Kd{v v'} \sum_j \qty(V_{c'jcj} - V_{c'jjc}) + \Kd{c c'} \sum_j \qty(V_{vjjv'} - V_{vjv'j}) + \\
    &+ \Kd{v v'} \Kd{c c'} \frac{1}{2} \sum_{ij} \qty(V_{ijij} - V_{ijji})\,.
\end{align*}

\noindent Summing the single-particle and two-particle contributions, we have for the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian matrix elements

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        H_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = &\delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{v,v'} \qty[\epsilon_{c, \bk + \bQ}  \delta_{c,c'} + \Sigma_{c c'}(\bk + \bQ, \bk' + \bQ)] - \\
        &-\delta_{\bk,\bk'} \delta_{c,c'} \qty[\epsilon_{v, \bk}  \delta_{v,v'} + \Sigma_{v' v}(\bk + \bQ, \bk' + \bQ)] - \\
        &-(D - X)_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ)\,,
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:BSE_Hamiltonian_matrix_elements}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have defined the direct and exchange interaction matrix elements, as

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:direct_and_exchange_terms}
    \begin{align}
        D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= V_{c\bk + \bQ,v'\bk', c'\bk' + \bQ, v\bk} \label{subeq:direct_term} \qq{and}\\
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= V_{c\bk + \bQ,v'\bk', v\bk, c'\bk' + \bQ}\,, \label{sub eq:exchange_term}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent respectively and the self-energy, accounting for the interaction between the electron (or hole) with the Fermi sea, as

\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{nm} (\bk,\bk') = \sum_{j,\bk''}^{\text{occ}}\qty(V_{n\bk,j\bk'',m\bk',j\bk''} - V_{n\bk,j\bk'',j\bk'',m\bk'})\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the sum is only over occupied bands.
About the self-energy, we have to make a technical remark.
As a sanity check, it is instructive to look closely at~\eqref{eq:BSE_Hamiltonian_matrix_elements}, and check for hermiticity by replacing $v \leftrightarrow v'$, $c \leftrightarrow c'$ and $\bk \leftrightarrow \bk'$.
If we do so, we arrive at the conclusion that the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian is not hermitian, with the self-energy terms being the culprits.
We can avoid this problem, if we work at the mean-field level, using a single Slater determinant for the \gs~ of~\eqref{eq:Hamiltonian_start}, or equivalently, making a \hf~approximation.
We can then see the self-energy corrections as incorporated in the \qp~energies $\epsilon_{n \bk}$, and without any worries assume that any quasi-particle energy corrections has already been performed \textit{a priori} through in the diagonalization of the single-particle Hamiltonian by \abinit~methods.
In this way, the Bethe--Salpeter equation writes


\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{equation}
   \qty(\epsilon_{c, \bk + \bQ} - \epsilon_{v, \bk}) A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk) + \sum_{v',c',\bk'} K_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) A^{\bQ}_{v'c'}(\bk') = E_X A^{\bQ}_{vc}(\bk)
    \label{eq:exact_Bethe_Salpeter_equation}
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the energies $\{\epsilon_{n, \bk}\}$ are now \hf~\qp~energies, and where we have defined the interaction kernel as $K = -\qty(D - X)$.
This approximation in the \bse~is commonly called in the literature the Tamm--Dancoff approximation~\cite{Dreuw_2005,Hirata_1999}, and allows to directly obtain the excitonic states without any complex diagonalization procedures.
We would like also to note that the Tamm--Dancoff approximation was for the first time introduced in the context of \mbpt~, where a similar issue for the \bse~ arises.
Nonetheless, there are two striking differences between the \bse~ we solve and the one obtained with \mbpt~\cite{Jornada_PhD,Diana_PhD}.
In the latter, the problem has a frequency dependence, that we do not find here.
The screening is dynamic, and that entails performing an integral in frequencies to compute the interaction matrix elements.
Here we model the screening using a static dielectric function, so we work always in the static limit $\omega \to 0$.
On one hand, this approximation is also common in \abinit~ calculations in practice, however in that context it has to be justified.
On the other hand, the general case $\omega \neq 0$ hinders heavily the numerical calculations.
The other difference we can point out, is that within \mbpt~, the exchange term is a matrix element of the \textbf{bare} Coulomb potential, while only the direct term is a matrix element of the \textbf{screened} Coulomb potential.
In our case, both direct and exchange terms are matrix elements of the same Coulomb potential, and there is no reason for the exchange term to involve the bare Coulomb potential (it is usually discarded either way).

We have not hitherto specified the form of the Coulomb potential, even though screening was a key concept in previous chapters.
We shall discuss that right in the next section, where we explain how we can compute the interaction matrix elements in the Bethe--Salpeter equation.

\noindent where the tight-binding coefficients are determined by diagonalizing the Bloch Hamiltonian (as explained in Sec.~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}), and the atomic orbitals denoted by $\phi_\alpha$ are orthogonal, as we work under the tight-binding formalism\footnote{They in general may not be orthogonal. However, this is not a problem, because one can always follow an orthogonalization procedure. The code we work with does just that. We shall remind the reader again once it comes the time of discussing the code developed.}.
Focusing first on the direct interaction term, per~\eqref{subeq:direct_term}, in terms of Bloch states is

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:direct_term_real_space_formula}
    D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') W(\br,\br') \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v,\bk}(\br')\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have denoted now the screened potential by $W$ instead of $V$.
Upon replacing the Bloch states by their expression~\eqref{eq:Bloch_states_again}, at some point we need to evaluate four-body integrals of the form

\begin{equation}
    \int \dr \int \dr' \phi^*_{\alpha} (\br) \phi^*_{\beta} (\br') W(\br,\br') \phi_{\alpha'} (\br) \phi_{\beta'} (\br')\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent with the position vectors as arguments of the orbitals may have a shift in position of the form $\br \to \br - \bR - \bt_i$.
In \abinit~calculations......
Considering point-like orbitals where their spatial structure is overlooked and asuming orthogonality, whenever a product between orbitals appears we can make the substitution

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_{i}) \phi_{\beta} (\br - \bR' - \bt_j) \approx \delta_{\alpha \beta} \delta_{\bR \bR'} \delta_{ij} \delta(\br - \bR - \bt_i)\,
\end{equation}

\noindent This allows for performing the space integrals directly and analitically, so that we can obtain a relatively simple expression for the direct term:

\begin{equation}
    D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \alpha}  \sum_{j \beta} \qty(C_{i \alpha}^{c \bk+ \bQ})^* C_{i \alpha}^{c' \bk' + \bQ} \qty(C_{j \beta}^{v' \bk'})^* C_{j \beta}^{v \bk} V_{ij}(\bk' - \bk)\,,
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_real_space}
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    V_{ij}(\bk' - \bk) = \sum_{\bR} \ei{\qty(\bk' - \bk) \vdot \bR} W\qty(\bR + \bt_i; \bt_j)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which \textbf{is not} a Fourier transform.
If we model the screening using the Rytova--Keldysh potential, then we can further write $W\qty(\bR + \bt_i; \bt_j) = V_\RK (\bR + \bt_i - \bt_j)$: the Rytova--Keldysh model is classical, the dielectric function is assumed to be a single momentum function, implying one has assumed continuous translation invariance, which is clearly an approximation.
To go beyond classical models for screening and take into account the inhomogenety of the crystal, we can develop the expression~\eqref{eq:direct_term_real_space_formula} by Fourier transforming the screened Coulomb potential, and write

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal}
        &D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \\
        &\int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') \left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bq,\bq'} \ei{\bq \vdot \br} W(\bq,\bq')\ee^{-\ii \bq' \vdot \br'}\right) \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v,\bk}(\br') = \\
        &=\int \dr \int \dr' \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') \left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bq \in \mathrm{BZ}} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \ei{(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br} W(\bq + \bG,\bq + \bG')\ee^{-\ii (\bq + \bG') \vdot \br'}\right) \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br) \psi^*_{v,\bk}(\br') = \\
        &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bq \in \mathrm{BZ}} \sum_{\bG,\bG'}
        \qty(\int \dr  \psi^*_{c,\bk+\bQ}(\br) \ei{(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br} \psi_{c',\bk'+\bQ}(\br))  W_{\bG \bG'}(\bq) \qty(\int \dr' \psi^*_{v',\bk'}(\br') \ee^{-\ii (\bq + \bG') \vdot \br'}  \psi^*_{v,\bk}(\br')) = \\
        &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bq \in \mathrm{BZ}} \delta_{\bq,\bk - \bk'} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \bra{c,\bk+\bQ} \ei{(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br} \ket{c',\bk'+\bQ} W_{\bG \bG'}(\bq) \bra{v',\bk'} \ee^{-\ii (\bq + \bG') \vdot \br} \ket{v,\bk} = \\
        &=  \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \qty(I^{\bG}_{c \bk+\bQ, c' \bk'+\bQ})^* W_{\bG \bG'}(\bk - \bk') I^{\bG'}_{v \bk, v' \bk'}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we introduced the definition

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:plane_wave_matrix_element_definition}
    I^{\bG}_{n \bk, n' \bk'} = \int \dr\, \psi^*_{n,\bk} (\br) \ee^{-\ii(\bk  - \bk' + \bG) \vdot \br} \psi_{n' \bk'} (\br)\,.
\end{equation}

The final expression in equation~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal} is the one usually encountered in the literature pertaining to first-principle methods~\cite{BerkeleyGW,Yambo_2009}.
These methods allow also to compute the dielectric matrix, and its inverse so that one can calculate the matrix elements of the screened Coulomb potential through $W_{\bG\bG'}(\bq) = \vareps^{-1}_{\bG\bG'}(\bq) v_c(\bq + \bG')$, the static limit of~Eq.~\eqref{eq:screened_dynamic_Coulomb_potential}.

Regarding the exchange term, even though we typically will discard, for completion we present its expression, that is

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_element_exchange_reciprocal}
    \begin{split}
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \alpha}  \sum_{j \beta} \qty(C_{i \alpha}^{c \bk+ \bQ})^*   C_{i \alpha}^{v \bk} C_{j \beta}^{c' \bk' + \bQ} \qty(C_{j \beta}^{v' \bk'})^* V_{ij}(\bQ)  \\
                                  &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \qty(I^{\bG}_{c \bk+\bQ, v \bk})^* W_{\bG \bG'}(\bQ) I^{\bG'}_{c' \bk' + \bQ, v' \bk'}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

If instead we use for the screened Coulomb potential the Rytova--Keldysh model, but work in reciprocal space, then the expressions for the direct and exchange terms write

\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:interaction_matrix_elements_reciprocal_RK}
    \begin{align}
        D_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bG} I^{\bG}_{c \bk+\bQ, c' \bk'+\bQ} V_{\text{RK}}(\bk - \bk' + \bG) (I^{\bG}_{v \bk, v' \bk'})^* \label{eq:direct_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK} \\
        X_{vc,v'c'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bG} I^{\bG}_{c \bk+\bQ, v \bk} V_{\text{RK}}(\bQ + \bG) (I^{\bG}_{c' \bk' + \bQ, v' \bk'})^* \,, \label{eq:exchange_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent where $V_{\text{RK}}(\bq) = \frac{e}{2\epsz q\left(1+r_0 q\right)}$ is the momentum resolved Rytova--Keldysh potential, encountered in this work for the first time in section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit} of the very first chapter of this thesis.
A striking difference between the expressions~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_elements_reciprocal_RK} and~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal} and~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_exchange_reciprocal}, is that in the latter there is a double sum over reciprocal lattice vectors $\bG$ and $\bG'$, while in the former there is only a single sum over reciprocal lattice vectors.
The double sum allows for including non-local field effects in the interaction matrix elements.
In the single sum, the inclusion of the reciprocal lattice vectors has the purpose of preserving the symmetry in the interaction between electrons with momenta $\bk$ and $\bk'$~\cite{Ridolfi_2015}---the \bd~Fourier transform of the Rytova--Keldysh potential from recirprocal space to real space must involve a sum over all momenta in the reciprocal lattice to recover the correct $\br$ dependence, and sum over all momenta can be decomposed in a sum over momenta in the \bz~with a sum over all reciprocal lattice vectors, so that the entire reciprocal lattice is covered.

\subsection{Noninteracting optical conductivity}
\label{subsec:non_interacting_conductivity}


We could follow a more technical strategy to derive the expression for the optical conductivity as in Refs~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004,Mahan}, but since we have at our disposal the dielectric function, we will simply use it instead.
We start from the dynamical longitudinal dielectric function as per~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency}, that we recall here:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \varepsilon_{\bG\bG'}(\bq,\omega) &= \delta_{\bG\bG'}-v_{c}(\bq+\bG)\chi_{\bG\bG'}^{0}(\bq,\omega)=\\
        &=\delta_{\bG\bG'}-\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}|\bq+\bG|^{2}V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk+\bq})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk+\bq}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii\hbar \alpha} \times \\

    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we used the \gls{3d} Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential, and recovered the square in the electron charge for convenience, while $V$ is the volume of the system, assumed to be \gls{3d}.
Taking the local limit $\bq\to\bzero$ in the $\bG=\bG'=\bzero$ matrix element, we get

%\begin{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\frac{\ii\hbar}{V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\hbar\omega\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk})+\ii\hbar \alpha}\frac{\langle n,\bk|j_{\alpha}|n',\bk\rangle\langle n',\bk|j_{\beta}|n,\bk\rangle}{\left(\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}\right)^{2}}
\end{equation}

\noindent Using the Sokhotski--Plemelj identity

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sokhotski_plemelj}
    \frac{1}{x\pm \ii \eta}=\mathrm{P.V.}\!\left(\frac{1}{x-a}\right) \mp \ii\pi\delta(x-a),\quad \eta=0^+,
\end{equation}

\noindent the real part of the conductivity, in terms of matrix elements of the velocity operator, writes

\begin{equation}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi e^{2}\hbar}{V}\sum_{nn',\bk}\frac{f(\epsilon_{n',\bk})-f(\epsilon_{n,\bk})}{\epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}}  v^{\alpha}_{n n'}(\bk)  v^{\beta}_{n' n}(\bk) \delta(\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n',\bk}-\epsilon_{n,\bk}))
\end{equation}

\noindent where we used the trick of replacing $\omega \to \epsilon_{n,\bk}-\epsilon_{n',\bk}$ to cancel the frequency in the numerator, which is allowed due to the presence of the Dirac delta function, and defined $v^{\alpha}_{n n'}(\bk) = \langle n,\bk|v_{\alpha}|n',\bk\rangle$.
Particularizing for a semiconductor at zero temperature and simplifying, we are allowed to write

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:non_interacting_Re_optical_conductivity}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi e^{2}\hbar}{V}\sum_{vc,\bk}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{c,\bk}-\epsilon_{v,\bk}}   v^{\alpha}_{c v}(\bk)  v^{\beta}_{v c}(\bk)\delta(\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{c,\bk}-\epsilon_{v,\bk}))
\end{empheq}

\subsection{Excitonic optical conductivity}
\label{subsec:excitonic_conductivity}

In this section we are concerned with the contribution of excitons to the optical conductivity.
If the material is subject to a drive from a uniform time-dependent electric field composed of a single harmonic $\omega$, the expression for the optical conductivity up to leading order, is given by~\cite{Pedersen_2015}


\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{align}
    \sigma (\omega) = \frac{e^2}{8 \pi^3 \ii \hbar} \sum_{\nu} \qty[E_{\nu} \frac{\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} \vdot \vectorbold{\Omega}^*_{\nu} }{E_{\nu} - \hbar \omega + \ii \hbar \Gamma} + E_{\nu} \frac{\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} \vdot \vectorbold{\Omega}^*_{\nu} }{E_{\nu} + \hbar \omega - \ii \hbar \Gamma}]
    \label{eq:optical_conductivity_def}
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the sum is over the excitonic states, $E_{\nu}$ being the energy of the $\nu$-th state, and $\vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu}$ is the Berry curvature given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Berry_curvature_definition}
    \vectorbold{\Omega}_{\nu} = \sum_{cv,\bk} \psi^{\nu}_{c,v} (\bk) \langle v, \bk | \br \vdot \hat{\vectorbold{e}} | c, \bk \rangle,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\br$ is the dipole operator, $\hat{\vectorbold{e}}$ is the polarization unit vector and $\psi^{\nu}_{c,v} (\bk)$ is the excitonic wavefunction for the pair of bands $(c,v)$.
If once more we wish to define the optical conductivity as a tensor, then we can deduct an expression similar to~\eqref{eq:non_interacting_Re_optical_conductivity}, we just have to replace the transition energy $\epsilon_{c,\bk}-\epsilon_{v,\bk}$ by the exciton energy $E_{\nu}$, and the velocity matrix elements by their interacting version given by a weighted sum over momentum of the noninteracting matrix elements, with the weight coinciding with the excitons wavefunction, similarly to what is written in~\eqref{eq:Berry_curvature_definition}.
We can therefore write

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{align}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi e^{2}\hbar}{V}\sum_{\nu}\frac{1}{E_{\nu}} V^{\alpha}_{\nu} (V^{\beta}_{\nu})^{*} \delta(\hbar \omega - E_{\nu})
    \label{eq:excitonic_Re_optical_conductivity}
\end{empheq}

\noindent if we define the interacting velocity matrix elements as $V^{\alpha}_{\nu} = \sum_{cv,\bk} \psi^{\nu}_{c,v} (\bk) v^{\alpha}_{c v}(\bk) $.
