\chapter{Quantum Mechanics of Electrons in Crystalline Solids}
\label{ch:quantum_mechanics_solids}

We have seen in the previous chapter that, even though classical models explain many phenomena in electrodynamics fairly well, both qualitatively and quantitatively, classical physics cannot predict the precise values of their respective parameters, nor can it explain why they work so well for certain materials, while failing to describe others.
To have a deeper understanding of these models and their regimes of validity, or to faithfully predict material properties in general, one has to adopt a microscopic description of the systems we want to study.
This means that we have to look at the chemical composition of the material and at the spatial arrangement of the atoms making it up.
One has to look at the detailed physics at length scales of the order of the angstrom, where classical physics is insufficient.

The tools that allow us to surpass the shortcomings of classical physics are provided by quantum mechanics.
It is said that the father of quantum mechanics is Max Planck, the German physicist who solved the problem of the ultraviolet catastrophe---an ideal black body radiates an infinite amount of energy at higher frequencies deep into the ultraviolet range of the electromagnetic spectrum.
This was the prediction by the classical Rayleigh--Jeans law, which is obviously unphysical.
It was the hypothesis by Planck that radiation is emitted in discrete amounts of energy, called quanta, that solved the mystery of the black body.
This grand landmark gave birth to a new field in physics, which catalyzed a scientific revolution in the previous century.
Quantum mechanics allowed solving other open problems in physics.
Such an example was knowing why the electron does not fall into the nucleus of the atom in its orbital motion until it loses all its energy, as predicted by classical electrodynamics.
It was the danish physicist Niels Bohr, who came up with an atomic model that explained the stability of the atoms and their discrete energy levels.

With the machinery provided by the principles and laws of quantum mechanics, many techniques have been developed since last century to approach different quantum systems.
We keep our discussion narrowed to solids with a periodic atomic structure, called crystals, until the end of this thesis, and leave all other types of systems (amorphous solids, such as glasses, crystalline solids with defects, quasi-crystals, few-particle systems, etc.) out of the discussion.

In the field of solid state physics, it is customary to separate the techniques based in first-principles, also called \abinit~methods, that are usually very computational intensive, but with a more fundamental starting point.
In brief terms, by providing only the chemical species and how the atoms bond among each other in the material, we can get the lengths of the chemical bonds, and different material properties, for instance electronic, magnetic, and thermal, among others.
Those based in second principles, while much less computationally demanding, they depart from some information that we already know about our system, like model parameters obtained experimentally or via first-principles calculations.
In this thesis, \abinit~approaches will be discussed on a passing note, as those can easily materialize into a thesis in itself, but are nonetheless very relevant for our work, which is essentially based in second-principles calculations.

We start the chapter by introducing the Schr\"{o}dinger equation, the workhorse of basic quantum mechanics, alongside the concept of Hamiltonian operator.
Next, we particularize the study to crystals, by writing the full many-body Hamiltonian of a crystal, followed by key approximations to reduce the complexity of the problem.
Afterwards, we define the concepts of (direct) lattice and reciprocal lattice, and discuss single-particle properties of electrons in periodic solids, such as Bloch states and the band structure; these define the dispersive behavior of electronic waves in materials.
First-principle methods at the mean-field level, such as \dft, are discussed in brief as well.
We extend the discussion on first-principle methods to the many-particle description of electrons in solids, provided by the machinery of \gls{mbpt}\@.
With all these concepts understood, the reader should be capable of grasping the microscopic dielectric function, reserved for the last section of this chapter.
The concept of dielectric function, introduced in the previous chapter on a classical footing, will be therein revisited through the lens of quantum mechanics.

\section{The Hamiltonian of a Crystal}
\label{sec:crystal_hamiltonian}

\subsection{The Schr\"{o}dinger equation}
\label{subsec:schrodinger_equation}

In classical mechanics, we follow all the degrees of freedom of a system, whose dynamics is governed by the Lagrangian function in the Lagrangian formalism, or equivalently by the Hamiltonian function in the Hamiltonian formalism.
Both formalisms provide a set of equations governing the time evolution of the degrees of freedom of the system, that together with the initial conditions determine the state of the system at any time in the future in the configuration space for the Lagrangian formalism, or in the phase space for the Hamiltonian formalism~\cite{goldstein:mechanics}.
When the length scale of a system is too small to be considered classic, and is thus deemed quantum, then it is not possible to follow the exact trajectory of the system.
The state of the system can no longer be identified with a set of coordinates and (canonical) momenta with precise values at any time instant.
Instead, the state of the system is a vector, vector state, or simply state living in a Hilbert space, that we can denote by a ket $\ket{\Psi}$ in the famous braket notation.
The corresponding bra is written as $\bra{\Psi}$, and it acts on kets in the same Hilbert space, in the sense that the bra $\bra{\Psi}$ acting on a ket $\ket{\Phi}$ gives the complex number $\braket{\Psi}{\Phi}$, which is the inner product between the states $\ket{\Psi}$ and $\ket{\Phi}$.
A Hilbert space is thus any vector linear space endowed with an inner product\footnote{For us physicists, it suffices to say that a Hilbert space is a vector space with an inner product, which is not pathological. For mathematicians, we have to add the condition of the space being complete for the distance induced by the (norm induced by the) inner product. The curious reader can consult Ref.~\cite{Kreyszig_1991} for more details.}.
We can call an inner product also a scalar product as a synonym.
That same state can evolve in time, and the time evolution is governed by the \gls{tdse}, which reads~\cite{Griffiths_QM_2018,Sakurai}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:time_dependent_Schrodinger_equation}
    \ii \hbar \pdv{t} \ket{\Psi(t)} = \Ha \ket{\Psi(t)},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\hbar = h / 2\pi$ is the reduced Planck constant, and $\Ha$ is the Hamiltonian operator---no longer a function---of the system.
The hat above the symbol $H$ is used to stress that we are dealing with an operator, not a function.
We use the term operator to denote a linear transformation that maps a vector in a Hilbert space to another vector in another Hilbert space; eventually, both Hilbert spaces coincide.
It is the generalization of a linear transformation from linear algebra.
At some point we shall drop the hat for ease of notation.

If in the \gls{tdse} the Hamiltonian has no explicit dependence on time, then we can easily integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:time_dependent_Schrodinger_equation} and write a formal solution for the state as


\begin{equation}
    \ket{\Psi(t)} = \ei[-]{\Ha (t-t_0)/\hbar} \ket{\Psi(t_0)},
\end{equation}

\noindent if we know the state of the system $\ket{\Psi(t_0)}$ at the initial time $t_0$.
We can therefore factor out the time dependence of the state, which is encoded in the new operator $\exp{-\ii \Ha (t-t_0)}$, commonly called the time-evolution operator.
Now we assume that the time-independent part of the state of the system, $\ket{\Psi(t_0)}$, can be written as a linear combination of basis states $\ket{\phi_n}$, which are eigenstates of the Hamiltonian with eigenvalues $E_n$, such that $\ket{\Psi(t_0)} = \sum_n c_n \ket{\phi_n}$, with $\Ha \ket{\psi_n} = E_n \ket{\psi_n}$.
We may also assume that the basis set $\{\ket{\phi_n}\}$ is orthonormal and complete.
A set of basis states being complete means that any vector state in the Hilbert space can be written as a linear combination of the states $| \phi_n \rangle$.
The coefficients $c_n$ can then be computed by projecting $\braket{\phi_m}{\Psi(t_0)} = c_m$.
However, to know the coefficients $c_n$, we need to know the eigenstates and the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian operator, which can be found by solving the eigenvalue problem

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:time_independent_Schrodinger_equation}
    \Ha \ket{\phi_n} = E_n \ket{\phi_n},
\end{equation}

\noindent which is called the \gls{tise}.
The eigenvalues $E_n$, being eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian operator, can be interpreted as energies and called eigenenergies.
Solving the \gls{tise} is then a key problem in quantum mechanics, whenever $\partial_t \Ha = 0$.
If the problem is simple enough that the Hamiltonian has a finite matrix representation, in other words, if the Hilbert space is finite-dimensional, then it is a typical eigenvalue/eigenvector problem from linear algebra.
On the contrary, if the Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional, then the \gls{tise} cannot be solved analytically, apart from a small set of examples, like the particle in a box, the quantum harmonic oscillator, the hydrogen atom~\cite{Griffiths_QM_2018}, among others.
Henceforth, since the birth of quantum mechanics, physicists have developed many techniques to study different systems, mainly depending on the aspect of the Hamiltonian.
In analogy with classical mechanics, in many cases the Hamiltonian of a system is the sum of two contributions; one coming from the kinetic energy of the particle or particles involved, $\hat{T}$, and the other from the potential energy $\hat{V}$.
In the next subsection, we try to write an expression for the Hamiltonian of a crystal as $\Ha = \hat{T} + \hat{V}$.

\subsection{The many-body Hamiltonian of a crystal}
\label{subsec:many_body_crystal_hamiltonian}

In a solid, we have many particles composing the quantum system, namely the heavy nuclei of the atoms composing the material, the core electrons tightly bound to the nuclei and the outer valence electrons, the last determining most of the material properties at energy scales typical in condensed matter systems at low-temperatures, say from the \unit{\milli\electronvolt} up to the \unit{\electronvolt}.
We will consider that the core electrons act simply to screen the potential felt by the valence electrons from the nuclei, treating them as positively charged ions.
If the valence number of the atom is $Z$, then the ion charge is $+Ze$, where $e$ is the elementary charge.
The total Hamiltonian of a solid is then the sum of several contributions as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Hamiltonian_crystal}
    \Ha = \hat{T}_e + \hat{T}_{\mathrm{ion}} + \hat{V}_{e-\mathrm{ion}} + \hat{V}_{e-e} + \hat{V}_{\mathrm{ion-ion}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\hat{T}_e$ is the sum of the kinetic energies coming from the valence electrons, $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{ion}}$ is total kinetic energy of the ions, $\hat{V}_{e-\mathrm{ion}}$ is the potential energy coming from the Coulomb interaction between the outer electrons and the ionic nuclei, $\hat{V}_{e-e}$ is the potential energy coming from the pairwise interaction among the electrons, and finally $\hat{V}_{\mathrm{ion-ion}}$ is the potential energy coming from the interaction among the ions.
If we write each contribution using math notation, then, the total many-body Hamiltonian, in units $e^2/4\pi \epsz = 1$, dropping all the hats and ignoring relativistic effects, is

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:many_body_Hamiltonian}
    H = \sum_i \frac{p_i^2}{2 m} + \sum_I \frac{P_I^2}{2 M_I} + \sum_{i,I} \frac{-Z_I }{\qty|\br_i - \bR_I|} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i \neq j} \frac{1}{|\br_i - \br_j|} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{I \neq J} \frac{Z_I Z_J}{\qty|\bR_I - \bR_J|}
\end{empheq}

\noindent if we write $m$ for the electron mass, $\br_i$ and $\bp_i$ for the position and momentum of the $i$-th electron, respectively, while $\bR_I$, $\mathbf{P}_I$, $M_I$ and $Z_I$ are the $I$-th ion position, momentum, mass, and number of valence electrons, respectively.
The factor of $1/2$ in the last two terms is used to avoid double counting, and the sums are over all pairs of particles (both pairs $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$ are included in the sum; the same for the ions).
This Hamiltonian is written in terms of the  real-space coordinates of the particles, meaning that in the \gls{tise} it acts on a function depending on all the coordinates, called the \wf.
If the problem is that of a single particle that can be found in the state $| \psi \rangle$, then the \wf~is defined by $\psi(\br) \equiv \langle \br\, | \psi \rangle $---we are just saying that the state of the system can be written in a representation of basis functions belonging to the Hilbert space of all the square-integrable complex valued functions defined in real space\footnote{Being square-integrable means that the integral of the absolute value squared over their domain is finite.} normalized to 1.
With symbols, normalized to 1 reads $\langle \psi | \psi \rangle = \int \dr |\psi(\br)|^2 = 1$.
Obviously, we can see that solving for the eigenstates and eigenenergies of this Hamiltonian is a herculean task; some approximations are needed if we want to even aspire to describe the physics of this system.
Not only that, but even if we could solve this problem in practice, the \wf~would have so many variables (of the order of $10^{23}$), that there would not be anything useful we could do with so much information.
In the next subsections, we simplify our problem until we can somewhat describe the physics it contains.
We can start already by saying that we work under low enough temperatures and small enough energies, such that the system we are dealing with is in fact a solid---the ions are bound to their neighbors through strong chemical bonds, making the whole material rigid~\cite{Simon_Oxford_2013}.

\subsection{Born--Oppenheimer approximation}
\label{subsec:born_oppenheimer_approximation}

In this chapter, we are concerned with the eigenstate with the lowest energy possible, called the ground state (of the crystal).
A possible way to find the ground state, $| \GS \rangle$, of any system, is by applying the variational principle---for a Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ acting on states on a given Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$, then for every state $| \psi \rangle \in \mathcal{H}$ the ground state energy verifies $E_{\GS} \leq \langle \psi | \hat{H} | \psi \rangle$~\cite{Griffiths_QM_2018}.
The variational method consists in admitting a trial state $\ket{\Psi}$ depending on a given set of system parameters.
In a crystal, let us say that the parameters are the ion positions $\{\bR_I\}$.
Then, we compute the expectation value of the Hamiltonian $\mel{\Psi}{H}{\Psi} \equiv E$, and minimize it with respect to the parameters by solving $\partial_{\bR_I} E = 0$ for all $\bR_I$.
This is the philosophy of the so-called \abinit~methods, which will be discussed on passing in this work.
Among other properties to be discussed later in this chapter, they permit finding the equilibrium positions of the atomic nuclei.
We will simply assume that the arrangement of the ions in the solid is known, for instance, from X-ray experiments~\cite{Kittel_2004,Simon_Oxford_2013}.

To further narrow down the types of systems under consideration, we will only treat solids where the arrangement of the ions forms a periodic structure, the crystal.
Other types of solids can be found in nature, such as amorphous solids, like glasses, or solid materials with rotational symmetry but no translation symmetry, the quasicrystals~\cite{Chaikin_Lubensky_1995}.
Herein, the ions occupy positions in the crystalline structure, also called the lattice, such that the potential felt by the electrons $\hat{V}_{e-\mathrm{ion}}(\br)$ from the ions is a periodic function with the periodicity of the lattice.
We shall define more precisely what a lattice is in section~\ref{sec:single_particle_properties}.
In this work, we will assume that the ionic degrees of freedom are frozen, where the ions are stationary, forming a background of positive charge.
This is the adiabatic or Born--Oppenheimer approximation~\cite{Kittel_2004,Simon_Oxford_2013,Martin_2004}.
Within this approximation, the electronic and ionic degrees of freedom decouple, and the problem of the electronic structure and excitations can be treated separately from finding the ground state of the lattice and its excitations, also called phonons.
Interactions between electrons and phonons are thus disregarded in this work, as throughout this text electronic excitations occur at energy scales higher than those where lattice vibrations are relevant\footnote{An example where the electron--phonon interaction~\cite{Giustino_2017} is important is in the \gls{bcs} theory of superconductivity; the microscopic theory that explains why some materials can conduct electricity without dissipation at very low temperatures (or very high pressures). For the curious reader, one can consult Refs~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004} and~\cite{Mahan} for in-depth technical treatments, or~\cite{Cohen_Louie_2016} for a not as technical discussion.}.
Therefore, the ions will remain static in their equilibrium positions at all times, and their role is just to act as a periodic potential on the valence electrons that form the electron gas.
In the next subsection, we apply one more approximation---the independent electron approximation.

\subsection{Noninteracting limit}
\label{subsec:non_interacting_limit}

Upon ignoring in the full many-body Hamiltonian~\eqref{eq:many_body_Hamiltonian} the contributions from the kinetic energy of the ions and their mutual interaction, and further ignoring the electron--electron interactions, we arrive at the Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:single_particle_Hamiltonian}
    H = \sum_i  \frac{p_i^2}{2 m} + V_{e-\mathrm{ion}}(\br) \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent If we write the potential term in the previous expression as a sum over all electrons as $V_{e-\mathrm{ion}}(\br) = \sum_i v (\br_i)$ (with $v$ including a sum over all ions), then we have reduced the full many-body problem of Eq.~\eqref{eq:many_body_Hamiltonian} to a sum of single-particle Hamiltonians that are uncoupled, which is a noninteracting problem of identical particles.
In that case, the problem encompassing all the electrons is separable.
The \wf~including all $\mathcal{N}$ electrons, $\Psi(\br_1,\br_2,...,\br_{\mathcal{N}})$, can be written as a product of all the single-particle \wf s $\psi_i(\br_i)$ as $\Psi(\br_1,\br_2,...,\br_{\mathcal{N}})= \psi_1(\br_1) \psi_2(\br_2)...\psi_{\mathcal{N}}(\br_{\mathcal{N}})$, with each single \wf~satisfying the \gls{tise}


\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:single_particle_Schrodinger_equation}
    \left[ \frac{p^2}{2 m} + v(\br) \right] \psi_i(\br) = \epsilon_i \psi_i(\br)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\epsilon_i$ represents the energy of a single electron, and the total energy of the system is simply the sum of all energies $\epsilon_i$.
We also omit the quantum number for spin, since it is not relevant for the discussion.

In fact, to account for the statistics of the electrons, which are fermions, the \wf~of all electrons must be antisymmetric upon permutation of any two electrons.
Say we permute electron $i$ with electron $j$, then \wf~must obey $\Psi(\br_1,\br_2,...,\br_i,...,\br_j,...,\br_{\mathcal{N}}) = -\Psi(\br_1,\br_2,...,\br_j,...,\br_i,...,\br_{\mathcal{N}})$.
Therefore, the total \wf~is the Slater determinant of all single-particle \wf s~\cite{Griffiths_QM_2018}, which is the determinant of the matrix that has by entries $\psi_i(\br_j)$, with $i,j=1,...,\mathcal{N}$, along with a factor of $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{N}\,!}$ for normalization\footnote{As an example, the determinantal wavefunction for a two-particle system is given by $\Psi(\br_1,\br_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2!}}[\psi_1(\br_1) \psi_2(\br_2) - \psi_1(\br_2) \psi_2(\br_1)]$.}.
The noninteracting \wf s can then serve as a basis for the interacting problem.
With them, we can estimate the ground state energy of the interacting electron gas in a crystalline solid by using the Slater determinant as a variational \wf.
For that, one computes the expectation value of the interacting Hamiltonian

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:HF_Hamiltonian}
    H = \sum_i \left[\frac{p_i^2}{2 m} + v(\br_i)\right] + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i \neq j} \frac{1}{|\br_i - \br_j|}
\end{empheq}

\noindent for the Slater determinant, and minimize it with respect to the single-particle \wf s $\psi_i(\br_j)$.
This is called the \hf~method~\cite{Cohen_Louie_2016,Grosso_2014}, commonly introduced in introductory condensed matter physics courses as a first attempt in describing the ground state of the interacting electron gas\footnote{A very first attempt consists in assuming the
symmetrized wavefunction, mentioned at the end of the paragraph right before Eq.~\eqref{eq:single_particle_Schrodinger_equation}. That is called the Hartree method, which is faulty, as it does not account for exchange nor correlation effects.}.
With the \hf~method, we transform the interacting problem into an effective single-particle problem described by a Schr\"{o}dinger equation of the type~\eqref{eq:single_particle_Schrodinger_equation}, where each electron feels an effective potential due to all the other electrons.
There is a much more sophisticated way of doing so, within the framework of \gls{dft}, the foundation of \abinit~methods to calculate the electronic, mechanic, magnetic, thermal, etc., properties of extended or molecular systems.
By electronic properties, or structure, in extended materials, we mean the electronic dispersion defining how the energy of electrons traveling in it depends on their momentum $\bk$.
The electronic dispersion has been hinted at in the previous chapter, in analogy with the photonic dispersion.
Within quantum mechanics, subatomic particles have a dual particle--wave behavior.
Therefore, we can perceive traveling electrons as propagating waves, with their wavevector $\bk$ given by the de Broglie relation $\bp = \hbar \bk$.
Traditionally, in solid state physics the quantities ``momentum'' and ``wavevector'' are used interchangeably, with $\bk$ being the predominant notation (quite commonly putting $\hbar = 1$).
Therefore, by abuse of language and notation, from here onwards, the symbols $\bp/\bq/\bk$ might denote wavevector instead of momentum without notice, and vice-versa.
After all, the conversion between these quantities is simply a multiplication factor.
In the next section, we thoroughly discuss the concept of electronic dispersion in crystalline materials.

\section{Single-Particle Properties of Electrons in Crystals}
\label{sec:single_particle_properties}

In the previous section, we have touched upon the concept of electronic dispersion in materials, which pertains to the nontrivial dependence of an electron energy on momentum.
Before we address that problem, there are still some concepts we need to clarify.
The first is the concept of lattice.
It can be defined as an infinite, but discrete, set of points in space, such that all points are equivalent.
Regardless of where one stands in the lattice, the landscape is the same.
Let us look at an example, the triangular lattice, depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:triangular_lattice_and_copper_unit_cell}\textbf{(a)} below.
As one can see, this set of points enjoys discrete translational symmetry.
We call any translation vector $\bR$ that leaves the lattice invariant a lattice vector.
In other words, a lattice vector connects any two points in the lattice.
We call the smallest possible lattice vectors the primitive lattice vectors.
In two dimensions, we need two primitive lattice vectors in order to span the entire lattice.
In Fig.~\ref{fig:triangular_lattice_and_copper_unit_cell}\textbf{(a)}, the red vectors denoted by $\ba_1$ and $\ba_2$ are a possible choice for the primitive lattice vectors.
Evidently, the choice is not unique.
Therefore, any lattice vector can be written as an integer linear combination of these vectors.
Mathematically, we write

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lattice_vector_definition}
    \bR = n_1 \ba_1 + n_2 \ba_2, \qq{with} n_1,n_2 \in \mathbb{Z}\,.
\end{equation}

The shaded gray area in the same figure represents a portion of the lattice, such that upon all possible translations by a lattice vector, we can generate the entire crystal.
We call such a region a unit cell.
The unit cell is, of course, not unique---we can choose different shapes and sizes, as long as the whole lattice can be generated by translations of the tile.
In Fig.~\ref{fig:triangular_lattice_and_copper_unit_cell}\textbf{(a)}, we display a tile with the smallest possible area.
Such a tile is called a primitive unit cell.
If a unit cell has a larger area than the primitive one, it can be called a conventional unit cell, and is usually chosen to highlight the symmetries of the crystal.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \begin{minipage}[t]{0.5\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Chapters/Figures/Chapter_3/triangular_lattice_new}
        \subcaption{Triangular lattice.}
        \label{subfig:triangular_lattice}
    \end{minipage}
    \hspace{0.052\textwidth}%
    \begin{minipage}[t]{0.5\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[scale=2.88]{Chapters/Figures/Chapter_3/copper_unit_cell}
        \subcaption{Conventional unit cell of copper.}
        \label{subfig:copper_unit_cell}
    \end{minipage}
    \mycaption[Triangular lattice and the unit cell of copper]{Subfigure \pnl{a} is a schematic of the triangular lattice. The vectors $\ba_1$ and $\ba_2$ are primitive lattice vectors, and $\bR = - \ba_1 + 2 \ba_2$ is a (nonprimitive) lattice vector. The shaded gray area represents a possible primitive unit cell, and the lattice extends to infinity along the plane of the page. Subfigure \pnl{b} displays the conventional unit cell of copper, which is the region contained inside the cube delimited by the black lines.}
    \label{fig:triangular_lattice_and_copper_unit_cell}
\end{figure}

We have defined a lattice as a set of points that are all equivalent.
An example that can be found in nature is copper, the chemical element with symbol \ch{Cu}, which is a metal with lots of applications in industry, has the conventional unit cell represented in Fig.~\ref{fig:triangular_lattice_and_copper_unit_cell}\pnl{(b)}.
Its crystalline structure is called the face-centered cubic lattice, one of the possible 14 Bravais lattices in \gls{3d}~\cite{Kittel_2004}.
Nonetheless, we can find in nature many crystalline materials composed of more than one chemical element, therefore we cannot say that all points are equivalent in those materials.
We can even have a crystal composed of a single chemical element, but cannot be classified as a lattice in the way we defined it.
A striking example is graphene~\cite{Novoselov_Geim_2004}.
Then, let us denote by Bravais lattice the way we defined a lattice initially~\footnote{We gave two examples, among others. However, there are not infinitely many, since the total number of possible Bravais lattices is finite. In two dimensions there are only five, while in three dimensions there are in total fourteen.}.
A lattice will then be a Bravais lattice plus a basis, or motif, where the unit cell is that of the underlying Bravais lattice, but including the atomic sites that ``spoil'' the equivalence of all points in the lattice.
As an example, there is the hexagonal or honeycomb lattice, where the atomic sites are arranged in a hexagonal pattern.
It can be seen as two interpenetrated triangular lattices, each one forming a sublattice.
Graphene and \gls{hbn} can be found, as monolayers, in this configuration, which is depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_lattice}.
The latter material is very relevant in this work, as we shall see later.

As lattices enjoy translation symmetry, we can guess that momentum is a good quantum number to describe electronic states in these materials.
Therefore, it is useful to have a description of the lattice in terms of wavevectors and set a framework to write periodic functions in Fourier series.
In other words, we need an equivalent to the lattice in reciprocal space.
Let us define the reciprocal lattice as the set of all vectors $\bG$ such that $\ei{\bG \vdot \bR} = 1$, for any lattice vector $\bR$~\cite{Simon_Oxford_2013,Kittel_2004,Ashcroft_Mermin,Bruus_Flensberg_2004}.
In two dimensions, any reciprocal lattice vector can be written as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:reciprocal_lattice_vector_definition}
    \bG = m_1 \bb_1 + m_2 \bb_2, \qq{with} m_1,m_2 \in \mathbb{Z}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\bb_1$ and $\bb_2$ are primitive vectors of the reciprocal lattice.
They can be obtained by solving the set of equations of the form $\ba_i \vdot \bb_j = 2 \pi \delta_{ij}$, with $i,j=1,2$.
In this way, given the expression for any lattice vector~\eqref{eq:lattice_vector_definition} and any reciprocal lattice vector~\eqref{eq:reciprocal_lattice_vector_definition}, the condition $\ei{\bG \vdot \bR} = 1$ is always fulfilled.
The real space lattice is composed in general by a Bravais lattice and a basis, but it is always the direct underlying Bravais lattice defining the reciprocal lattice.

We define now the first \gls{bz}, or simply \gls{bz}, as the set of points $\bk$ such that $|\bk| < |\bk - \bG|$ for all $\bG \neq \bzero$~\cite{Simon_Oxford_2013,Ashcroft_Mermin}.
Explained in another way, let us fix a primitive unit cell of the reciprocal lattice, and set the origin inside that cell, say at $\bk = \bG = \bzero$.
Then, if any point $\bk$ in that cell is closer to the origin than to any other point in the reciprocal lattice equivalent to the origin, then that cell is the \gls{bz}~\cite{Grosso_2014}.
Any momentum in the reciprocal space can then be defined by another momentum in the \gls{bz} plus a reciprocal lattice vector.
We can say that the momentum conservation in a crystal conserves the momentum modulo a vector of the reciprocal lattice.
Therefore, we can speak of crystal momentum as the relevant quantum number in crystals, instead of momentum~\cite{Simon_Oxford_2013}.
We do not wish for the language used in this thesis to be too pedantic, in detriment of clarity.
By (one more) abuse of language, let us speak of momentum when we are in fact dealing with crystal momentum.

In this way, if we are working with a periodic function $f(\br)$ with the periodicity of the direct lattice, then it can be expanded in Fourier series as $f(\br) = \sum_{\bG} f_{\bG} \ee^{\ii \bG \vdot \bR}$, with the coefficients given by $f_{\bG} =1/A_{\mathrm{UC}} \int_{\mathrm{UC}} \dr \exp{\ii \bG \vdot \br} f(\br)$, an integral over the unit cell, hence the symbol $\mathrm{UC}$ for the domain of integration, which has area $A_{\mathrm{UC}}$.
These concepts will be key for understanding the Bloch theorem and its importance, to be explained in the next subsection.

\subsection{Bloch Theorem}
\label{subsec:bloch_theorem}

We have at this point all the necessary tools to state and understand Bloch theorem\footnote{Its original form is actual for \gls{1d} systems, or equivalently, for ordinary differential equations, and is due to the mathematician Gaston Floquet. The very same mathematician of the Floquet formalism of time-periodic Hamiltonians in quantum mechanics~\cite{Holthaus_2015}.\label{fn:Floquet_formalism}}, named after the physicist Felix Bloch.
It has different formulations and ways to be proved.
The formal proofs are here omitted, but we provide a short motivation for its statements below.
Until it becomes relevant for the discussion, we shall ignore the spin of the electron.

\begin{theorem-non}[Bloch Theorem]
    \label{th:Bloch_theorem}
    Let $H$ be a Hamiltonian with a potential $V(\br)$ periodic in space, $\psi_{\bk}(\br)$ the eigenfunction of an electron with momentum $\bk$ and $\epsilon(\bk)$ its eigenenergy.
    Let $\bR$ be any lattice vector.
    Then, the following statements hold and are equivalent:

    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),ref=(\arabic*)]
        \item The \wf~can be written in terms of a modulated plane wave $\psi_{\bk}(\br) = \ei{\bk \vdot \br} u_{\bk}(\br)$, with the function $u_{\bk}(\br)=u_{\bk}(\br + \bR)$ having the periodicity of the lattice;  \label{Bloch_theorem_I}
        \item The \wf~$\psi_{\bk}(\br)$ satisfies $\psi_{\bk}(\br + \bR) = \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} \psi_{\bk}(\br)\,$. \label{Bloch_theorem_II}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem-non}

The eigenfunction $\psi_{\bk}(\br)$ is known as the \underline{Bloch \wf}, while its periodic part $u_{\bk}(\br)$ is called the \underline{Bloch function}.
This theorem can be motivated in a simple ad-hoc way, by assuming that the \wf~of the electron $\psi_{\bk}(\br)$ can be appropriately expanded in a basis of plane waves as $\psi_{\bk}(\br) = \sum_{\bG} C^{\bk}_{\bG} \ei{(\bk - \bG) \vdot \br}$.
If we factor out the exponential $\ei{\bk \vdot \br}$ from the sum, then the first statement of the theorem becomes evident.
The second statement is straightforward to prove from the first one.
From the second statement, by identifying $u_{\bk}(\br) = \ei[-]{\bk \vdot \br} \psi_{\bk}(\br)$, we can in turn demonstrate the first statement.
Statement~\ref{Bloch_theorem_II} can also be proved by translational symmetry arguments and basic concepts of group theory~\cite{Cohen_Louie_2016,Martin_2004}.

We have studied in this subsection the properties of electronic eigenstates in a crystal subject to a periodic potential.
The dynamics of the electrons are governed by the noninteracting single-particle Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:single_particle_Hamiltonian}.
For that reason, the properties here discussed are said to be at the single-particle level.
The state the electron with momentum $\bk$ occupies is a single-particle state, also called a Bloch state in the context of solids.
Bloch states will serve as a basis for the interacting problem, as we will see in the next chapter.
In fact, for a single momentum $\bk$, we can have several energy states the electrons can populate.
If we label them by an integer $n$, then a general Bloch state in braket notation reads $|\psi_{n \bk}\rangle$, or more concisely, $\ket{n,\bk}$.
Then, the allowed energies are also labeled in the same way as $\epsilon_{n \bk}$.
For a fixed $n$, we call the entire set of energies $\epsilon_{n \bk}$ with $\bk$ varying in the \bz~an energy band, or just a band.
Then, the set of all bands $\{\epsilon_{n \bk}\}$ is called the (electronic) band structure of the material.
These bands define the dispersive behavior of electron waves in a crystal.
The ground state of the $\mathcal{N}$-electron system is obtained by filling the bands, starting from the bands with lower energies, until there are no valence electrons left.
In the following subsection, we discuss different methods to calculate the band structure.


\subsection{Electronic band structure: tight-binding and \abinit~methods}
\label{subsec:electronic_band_structure}

One central problem in condensed matter physics is to calculate the electronic band structure of a given solid material.
This means calculating all the bands at every point $\bk$ in the \gls{bz}\@.
An electron in a specific band $n$ with crystal momentum $\bk$ has kinetic energy $\epsilon_{n \bk}$.
In analogy with optics, we can call the $\bk$ dependence of the energy electronic dispersion, with the wave being now electronic instead of electromagnetic.
From here onwards, when referring to the band structure $\{\epsilon_{n \bk}\}$, we will always implicitly mean electronic band structure, as there are bands of other kinds (phononic, photonic as we have seen in the previous chapter, etc.).
Once the band structure, a ground state characteristic of the material, is determined, one is invited to do post-calculations to find other ground state or excited state properties.
But first, we need to better understand the concepts related to the bands of a crystalline solid.

\subsubsection{Tight-binding method}
\label{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}

There is a number of different descriptions for material band structures.
Two simple ones are the nearly free electron model and the tight-binding model.
In the first, one assumes the electron moves freely from atom to atom, so that it can be found in a plane wave state with parabolic dispersion, and the crystalline structure is seen as a small perturbation.
The electronic dispersion in the case of the nearly free electron model is simply given by $\varepsilon_{\bG} (\bk) = \hbar^2 \abs{\bk - \bG}^2 / 2m$, with the reciprocal lattice vectors $\bG$ labeling the bands.
This model is particularly good for standard metals, like aluminum~\cite{Levinson_aluminum}, that have a compact or close-packed crystal structure.

In the opposite limit, where the crystal is not compact and the atoms are further apart, electrons have more difficulty in moving from atomic site to atomic site, and then we say that they are tightly-bound to the atoms.
We usually associate this type of crystals with materials with poor conducting properties, those presenting a bandgap---the range of forbidden energies for which propagating electronic states are not allowed.
These can be classified as insulators or semiconductors, depending on how the size of the bandgap compares with the thermal energy, as we have explained before in section~\ref{subsec:polaritonics_dispersion_relation}.

Within this formalism, the Bloch \wf s are linear combinations of the atomic orbitals, instead of a superposition of plane waves.
This is called the \gls{lcao} method or approximation.
In math notation, we write for the \wf~of an electron in the Bloch state of band $n$ with momentum $\bk$

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:Bloch_state_LCAO}
    \psi_{n \bk} (\br) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} \sum_{i \alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \phi_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_i)
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the sum in $\bR$ is over the vectors of the lattice (not over all the atomic sites), or over all $N$ unit cells, $i$ is an index that labels the atomic species at the position $\bt_i$ in the motif, and $\alpha$ is an index that labels the type of the atomic orbital ($1s,2s,2p,3s,\dots$).
Usually, we sum only over the atomic orbitals in the outer shells of the atom that are populated by the valence electrons.
This is justifiable, as the valence electrons are the only ones that participate in the physics we are looking at, as explained in the beginning of section~\ref{subsec:many_body_crystal_hamiltonian}.

We introduce now the concept of Bloch Hamiltonian, a very useful one in the tight-binding method, and in this work overall.
We start by rewriting the Bloch state in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_state_LCAO}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Bloch_state_LCAO_auxiliar}
    \psi_{n \bk} (\br) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\bR,i\alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we defined the new function $\Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br) = \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} \phi_\alpha (\br - \bR - \bt_i)$.
The Bloch state~\eqref{eq:Bloch_state_LCAO_auxiliar} is an eigenstate of the Hamiltonian with energy $\epsilon$ (omitting band and momentum indices for now):

\begin{equation}
    H \psi_{n \bk} (\br) = \epsilon\, \psi_{n \bk} (\br) \Leftrightarrow \sum_{i \alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} H\Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br) = \epsilon \sum_{i \alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br) \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Now, we multiply by $\Phi^*_{j \beta} (\bk,\br)$ and integrate in space

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i \alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \underbrace{\int_{\vol} \dr\, \Phi^*_{j \beta} (\bk,\br) H \Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br)}_{H_{j \beta, i \alpha} (\bk)} = \epsilon \sum_{i \alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \underbrace{ \int_{\vol} \dr\, \Phi^*_{j \beta} (\bk,\br) \Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br)}_{S_{j \beta, i \alpha}(\bk)}
\end{equation}

\noindent to obtain the eigenvalue problem

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Bloch_eigenvalue_problem}
    \sum_{i \alpha}  H_{j \beta, i \alpha}(\bk) C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} = \epsilon \sum_{i \alpha}  S_{j \beta, i \alpha}(\bk) C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \Leftrightarrow H(\bk) \bC^{n \bk} = \epsilon S(\bk) \bC^{n \bk}\,,
\end{equation}


\noindent where we defined the arrays $\bC^{n \bk}$ with entries $(\bC^{n \bk})_{i \alpha} = C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk}$, and the matrices $H(\bk)$ and $S(\bk)$ with matrix elements

\begin{equation}
    H_{j \beta, i \alpha}(\bk) = \mel{\Phi_{j \beta} (\bk,\br)}{H}{\Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br)} \qq{and} S_{j \beta, i \alpha}(\bk) = \braket{\Phi_{j \beta} (\bk,\br)}{\Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br)} ,
\end{equation}

\noindent respectively.
We call the $S$ matrix the overlap matrix, and for the matrix elements we can write

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        S_{j \beta, i \alpha}(\bk) &= \braket{\Phi_{j \beta} (\bk,\br)}{\Phi_{i \alpha} (\bk,\br)} = \frac{1}{N} \int_{\vol} \dr\, \sum_{\bR'\bR} \ei[-]{\bk \vdot (\bR'  - \bR) } \phi_\beta^*(\br - \bR' - \bt_j) \phi_\alpha (\br - \bR - \bt_i) = \\
        &= \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} \braket{\phi_{j \beta} (\br)}{\phi_{i \alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_i)} = \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} s_{j \beta, i \alpha} (\bR)\,,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where from the 1st to the second line we fixed $\bR' = \bzero $, which is valid since both sums are over all vectors of the Bravais lattice.
In matrix form, we can write

\begin{equation}
    S(\bk) = \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} s(\bR)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Herein, we typically work in a basis of orthogonal orbitals, such that any two different orbitals do not overlap.
This holds in particular for the case where the localized orbitals are not atomic orbitals, but Wannier functions instead, which are orthogonal by construction\footnote{The Wannier functions result from transforming, by linear superpositions, the basis of the Bloch \wf s~\eqref{eq:Bloch_state_LCAO} into a basis of localized functions~\cite{Marzari_2012}.}.
This makes $S(\bk) = \mathds{1}$, simplifying the eigenvalue problem~\eqref{eq:Bloch_eigenvalue_problem}.
Following an analogous procedure for the Hamiltonian, one obtains

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Bloch_Hamiltonian_definition}
    H(\bk) = \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} H(\bR) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\bk$ denotes crystal momentum as usual, the sum is over all vectors of the Bravais lattice $\bR$, and $H(\bR)$ is the real space Hamiltonian at the site $\bR$, commonly called a Fock matrix, and has matrix elements $H_{j \beta, i \alpha} (\bR) = \langle \phi_{\beta} (\br - \bt_j) | H | \phi_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_i) \rangle$.
These matrix elements are called transfer integrals.
If $\bR \neq \bzero$, then they are a measure for how likely it is for an electron to hop from one orbital to another one in a different unit cell, and are called hopping parameters in the jargon of the tight-binding approach.
The matrix $H(\bk)$ presented just now is the so-called Bloch Hamiltonian.
Its eigenvalues form the band structure $\{\epsilon_{n \bk}\}$, and its eigenvectors are the arrays (or vectors) $\bC^{n \bk}$, whose elements are usually called tight-binding coefficients.

The Bloch Hamiltonian deserves a pertinent technical remark, pertaining to the sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_Hamiltonian_definition} and the argument of the imaginary exponential.
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_Hamiltonian_definition} we use the Fock matrices summing over unit cells with positions $\bR$.
There is, in fact, an alternative definition for the Bloch Hamiltonian, where the sum is over all lattice sites.
The first case is called the \enquote{periodic} form or the \enquote{lattice-gauge} form, as it has the periodicity of the reciprocal lattice.
It means that $H(\bk) = H(\bk + \bG)$ for any reciprocal lattice vector $\bG$, but it is not unique, as it depends on the choice of the unit cell.
The alternative case is called the \enquote{canonical} form, or the \enquote{atomic-gauge} form, and it is unique, but in general $H(\bk) \neq H(\bk + \bG)$ for a reciprocal lattice vector $\bG$.
If there is only one atom per unit cell, which is the case of Bravais lattices, the two forms coincide, but if the unit cell contains at least two non-equivalent sites, one can still go from one gauge to the other through a unitary transformation.
Nonetheless, in all cases in this work the results do not change with the chosen gauge; problems may arise, though, when dealing with geometric phases or topological invariants~\cite{Cayssol_2021}.
We present now a simple tight-binding model for a key \bd~material in this work, monolayer \gls{hbn}\@.
\\
\\
\textit{\underline{Monolayer hexagonal boron nitride: a minimal tight-binding model}}
\\
\\
In its multilayer form, \gls{hbn} is a rather interesting material.
For instance, it is hyperbolic in the mid-\gls{ir}---besides its refractive index being anisotropic, the components of the permittivity tensor pertaining to the off-plane and in-plane directions have opposite signs~\cite{Caldwell_2019}.
As a consequence, its isofrequency surfaces, the surfaces in momentum space over which the frequency is constant, are hyperboloids instead of spheres as in isotropic materials.
The isofrequency surfaces, or contours, help visualise what happens to the polaritonic dispersion, which is relevant for developing applications in nanophotonics.
This makes \gls{hbn} a promising material for photonic applications in the mid-\gls{ir}, such as hyperlensing.
In this same range, it presents hyperbolic phonon--polaritons~\cite{Sun_2015}, which have been already experimentally observed in nanostructures~\cite{Brown_Davanco_2018}, and even in its monolayer form~\cite{Dai_Fang_2019,Li_2021}.
In similarity with the plasmon--polariton discussed in the previous chapter, the phonon--polariton is the result of the hybridization between the excitation of the lattice, the phonon, and the photon.
When it comes to transport properties, it is a wide-bandgap semiconductor, with an indirect gap of around $5.955$~\unit{\electronvolt}~\cite{Cassabois_Valvin_Gil_2016}.
However, in its monolayer limit, the bandgap is direct~\cite{Elias_2019} and slightly higher, around $6.08 \pm 0.2$~\unit{\electronvolt}~\cite{Roman_2021}.
We emphasize that these are experimental observations.

Theoretically, monolayer \gls{hbn} can be described by a minimal tight-binding model with staggered on-site energies and a single hopping amplitude between nearest-neighbours.
This material is composed of boron and nitrogen atoms, alternated in a lattice arranged in hexagons, like in Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_lattice}\pnl{a} below.
In the language of section~\ref{sec:single_particle_properties}, it is a triangular lattice with a basis, having the unit cell two atoms(shaded gray area in Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_lattice}\pnl{a}).
A typical choice for its \bz~is the one depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_lattice}\pnl{b}, with a hexagonal shape.

In Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_lattice}\pnl{a}, if $a$ is the length of the chemical bonds, we have for the primitive lattice vectors $\ba_1$ and $\ba_2$

\begin{equation}
    \ba_1 = \frac{a}{2}(3,\sqrt{3})\,, \qq{and} \ba_2 = \frac{a}{2}(3,-\sqrt{3}),
\end{equation}

\noindent and the nearest-neighbour positions with respect to the blue sites are given by

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\delta}_1 = a(1,0)\,, \, \boldsymbol{\delta}_2 = \frac{a}{2}(-\sqrt{3},-1)\,,\,\text{and}\,\, \boldsymbol{\delta}_3 = \frac{a}{2}(-\sqrt{3},1)\,,
    \label{eq:delta_vectors}
\end{equation}

\noindent which are represented by the green vectors, and the primitive reciprocal lattice vectors $\bb_1$ and $\bb_2$ in blue in Fig~\ref{fig:hBN_lattice}\pnl{b} are given by

\begin{equation}
    \bb_1 = \left(\frac{2 \pi}{3 a},\frac{2 \pi}{\sqrt{3} a} \right) \qq{and} \bb_2 = \left(\frac{2 \pi}{3 a},-\frac{2 \pi}{\sqrt{3} a} \right)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The coordinates of the high-symmetry points $K$ and $K'$ are given by

\begin{equation}
    \bK = \left(\frac{2 \pi}{3 a},\frac{2 \pi}{3\sqrt{3} a} \right) \qq{and} \bK' = \left(\frac{2 \pi}{3 a},-\frac{2 \pi}{3\sqrt{3} a} \right).
\end{equation}

A minimal tight-binding Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn}, in second quantization\footnote{In second quantization, we promote a ket state $\ket{\alpha}$ to an operator $\hat{a}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}$ that creates a particle in the state $\alpha$. A bra corresponds then to an annihilation operator $\hat{a}_{\alpha}$, destroying a particle in the state $\alpha$. These operators act on states living in the Fock space, and obey commutation/anti-commutation relations for bosons/fermions. The states that belong to the Fock space describe how many particles populate each ket state living in its corresponding single-particle Hilbert space. The second quantization formalism is also called the occupation number representation~\cite{Fetter_Walecka}.}, reads

\begin{equation}
    H = \sum_i E_A a_i^{\dagger} a_i + \sum_j E_B b_j^{\dagger} b_j - t \sum_{\langle i j\rangle}  \left(a_i^{\dagger} b_j+b_j^{\dagger} a_i\right) ,
    \label{eq:graphene_tight_binding_Hamiltonian}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the operator $a_i^{\dagger}/a_i$ creates/annihilates an electron in the A sublattice at the position $\br_i$, and $b_j^{\dagger}/b_j$ creates/annihilates an electron in the B sublattice at the position $\br_j$.
The symbol $\langle i j \rangle$ under the sum symbol means that the sum is over nearest-neighbours.
It can be rewritten in a more comprehensive form

\begin{equation}
    H = \sum_i E_A a_i^{\dagger} a_i + \sum_j E_B b_j^{\dagger} b_j - t \sum_{i \in A} \sum_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\left(a_i^{\dagger} b_{i+\boldsymbol{\delta}}+b_{i+\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\dagger} a_i\right).
    \label{eq:graphene_tight_binding_Hamiltonian_comprehensive}
\end{equation}

\noindent Performing the change of basis

\begin{equation}
    a_i^{\dagger} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\bk} \ei{\bk \vdot \br_i} a_{\bk}^{\dagger}\,, \qq{analogously for} b_j^{\dagger},
    \label{eq:second_quantization_change_of_basis}
\end{equation}

\noindent and simplifying, we arrive at

\begin{equation}
    H = \sum_{\bk} \Psi_{\bk}^{\dagger} H(\bk) \Psi_{\bk}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we can define the Bloch Hamiltonian $H(\bk)$, the spinor $\Psi_{\bk}$ and the function $\Delta_{\bk}$ as

\begin{equation}
    \begin{array}{l}
        H(\bk)=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
                           E_A & -t \Delta_{\bk} \\
                           -t \Delta_{\bk}^* & E_B
        \end{array}\right), \quad \Psi_{\bk}=\left(\begin{array}{l}
                                                 a_{\bk} \\
                                                 b_{\bk}
        \end{array}\right), \quad \Delta_{\bk} = \sum_{\boldsymbol{\delta}} \ei[-]{\bk \vdot \boldsymbol{\delta}}\,.
    \end{array}
\end{equation}

\noindent Recalling the vectors $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_vectors}, we have

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Delta_of_k}
    \Delta_{\bk} =\ei[-]{k_x a}\left[1+2 \ei{3 k_x a / 2} \cos \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} k_y a\right)\right].
\end{equation}

The dispersion relation for \gls{hbn} is then given by~\cite{ribeiro_stability_2011}

\begin{equation}
    E(\bk) = E_0 \pm \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{E^2_g + 4 t^2 + 16 t^2 \cos \left(\frac{3 a }{2} k_x\right) \cos \left(\frac{\sqrt{3} a}{2}  k_y \right) + 16 t^2 \cos^2 \left(\frac{\sqrt{3} a}{2} k_y \right)}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $E_0 = (E_A + E_B)/2$ is the energy reference (which is arbitrary) and $E_g = E_A - E_B$ is the bandgap.
One can see clearly the opening of a gap (in the corners of the Brillouin zone $K$ and $K'$) in Figure \ref{fig:monolayer_hBN_bands}.
Even though identical to graphene in the crystal structure, contrary to it, monolayer \gls{hbn} is an insulator as we have said before.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Chapters/Figures/Chapter_3/hBN_TB_bands}
    \caption[Tight-binding band structure of monolayer hBN]{Band structure of monolayer \gls{hbn} in the tight-binding approximation. In panel \pnl{a} we display a \gls{3d} representation of the bands, while in \pnl{b} we plot the bands along the high-symmetry line $\Gamma-K-M-\Gamma$.     \label{fig:monolayer_hBN_bands}
\end{figure}

Sometimes, a low-energy approximation suffices for describing the material at hand.
Below, we do an analysis of the low-energy limit, or continuum limit, of monolayer \gls{hbn}\@.
Linearizing~\eqref{eq:Delta_of_k} with respect to momentum around the momentum $\bK = (2 \pi / 3 a, 2 \pi / 3 \sqrt{3} a)$, and defining the relative momentum $\bq$ by $\bk = \bK + \bq$, with $|\bq| \ll |\bK|$, we have

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\bK + \bq} \simeq -\ii\, \ei[-]{\frac{2 \pi }{3} a} \frac{3 a}{2}(q_x+\ii q_y) \rightarrow - \frac{3 a}{2} (q_x + \ii q_y) .
\end{equation}

\noindent Replacing now in the Bloch Hamiltonian, we define the following low-energy Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:low_energy_hamiltonian_hBN}
    \mathcal{H}_K \equiv H(\bK + \bq) \simeq  \frac{3 a t}{2 \hbar} (q_x \sigma_x - q_y \sigma_y) + M \sigma_z = \hbar \vF \bar{\bq} \vdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + M \sigma_z ,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $M$ is half the bandgap, $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$ and $\sigma_z$  are the Pauli matrices, and $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = (\sigma_x,\sigma_y)$ is Pauli matrices vector, $\vF$ is the Fermi velocity

\begin{equation}
    \vF = \frac{3 a t}{2 \hbar}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent and

\begin{equation}
    \bar{\bq} = (q_x,-q_y,0)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Following the same procedure, but for the $\bK'$ point, we obtain

\begin{equation}
    H(\bK' + \bq) = \hbar \vF \bq \vdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + M \sigma_z,
\end{equation}

\noindent where now

\begin{equation}
    \bq = (q_x,q_y,0)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent So we can write

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\tau \bK + \bq} \simeq - \frac{3 a}{2}(q_x+\ii \tau q_y),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\tau = \pm 1$, with $\tau=1$ for the $K'$ point and $\tau = -1$ for the $K$ point, represents the valley degree of freedom.
In this way, the low-energy dispersion relation reads

\begin{equation}
    E_{\pm} (\bq) = \pm \sqrt{E_g^2 + \hbar^2 \vF^2 |\bq|^2}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent $+$ for the top/conduction band, and $-$ for the bottom/valence band.
Now it is explained why the dispersion relation near the points $\bK$ and $\bK'$ look like parabolas for low energies; the dispersion is quadratic in the vicinity of those points, which can be seen if we further expand the previous expression up to leading order in $|\bq|$.
The energy spectrum enjoys spin degeneracy at the $K$ and $K'$ values, because the spin--orbit coupling in this system is negligible, since boron and nitrogen are very light elements.

We can as well compute the effective masses of the electrons with momentum in the vicinity of $\bK$ (or $\bK'$), through

\begin{equation}
    m^* = \left( \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\frac{\dd^2 E_{\pm}(q)}{\dd q^2} \bigg|_{q=0} \right)^{-1} = \pm \frac{E_g}{2 \vF^2},
    \label{eq:effective_mass_formula}
\end{equation}

We will use now a different, but equivalent, configuration for the lattices (rotated by 30 degrees).
The eigenvectors are

\begin{equation}
    \bC^{v \bk} =

\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \theta_k = \arctan \frac{k_y}{k_x} \qq{and}  \xi_k = \arctan \frac{2 \hbar \vF k}{E_g}.
\end{equation}

Even though the tight-binding Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn} provides a simple, yet insightful theoretical tool, we still need somehow to use realistic values for the parameters $t$ and $E_g$.
These can be obtained through fitting the tight-binding band structure to a more realistic one, typically obtained through the first-principle methods, that we describe in the next section.

\subsubsection{\textit{Ab-initio} methods}
\label{subsubsec:abinit_methods}

If we wish to describe the electronic structure of a crystal, we are faced with the stationary many-body Schr\"{o}dinger equation

\begin{equation}
    H \Psi(\br_1,\br_2,\dots,\br_{\mathcal{N}}) = E \Psi(\br_1,\br_2,\dots,\br_{\mathcal{N}}) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $H$ is the many-body Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:many_body_Hamiltonian}, and $\Psi(\br_1,\br_2,\dots,\br_{\mathcal{N}})$ is the many-body \wf.
As previously stated, there is no hope in solving the previous equation for the many-body \wf.
That was a motivation for introducing the nearly-free electron model and the tight-binding model.
But then, we are faced with the shortcomings of these models---they describe approximate opposing limits, and provide no way of determining the model parameters.

To overcome this issue, we have to take a different look into the many-body problem.
Quite surprisingly, it is possible to describe a ground state of a crystal in terms of a function of a single variable, which is the electronic density $n(\br)$, instead of dealing with $~10^{23}$ variables in the many-body \wf.
Physicists hypothesized that the ground state energy could be written as a functional of the electronic density.
This statement, which is a consequence of the first Hohenberg--Kohn theorem, was put in rigorous grounds by Hohenberg and Kohn~\cite{Hohenberg_Kohn_1964}, the founders of \gls{dft}\@.
Together with the second Hohenberg--Kohn theorem, they constitute the pillars of \dft\@.
The two theorems are very strong statements, that we proclaim below.

\begin{theorem-non}[Hohenberg--Kohn Theorem I]
    \label{th:HK_theorem_I}
    The properties of any physical system, at zero temperature, are completely determined by its ground state electronic density.
    This statement is a consequence of the following claims:

    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),ref=(\arabic*)]
        \item The mapping from the potential $V_{e-\mathrm{ion}}(\br)$ to the electronic ground state density $n(\br)$ is one-to-one;\label{itm:HK_I_1}
        \item The many-body \wf~of the ground state of a system is solely determined by its many-body Hamiltonian, which in turn is determined solely by the potential $V_{e-\mathrm{ion}}(\br)$ (and the number of electrons $\mathcal{N}$);\label{itm:HK_I_2}
        \item Per~\ref{itm:HK_I_1} and~\ref{itm:HK_I_2}, the ground state density $n(\br)$ determines uniquely the many-body \wf, and consequently, the ground and excited states of the system.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem-non}

As said before, this theorem implies that the ground state energy is a functional of the electronic density, $E[n(\br)]$.
By application of the variational principle, we can state the second Hohenberg--Kohn theorem below.

\begin{theorem-non}[Hohenberg--Kohn Theorem II]
    \label{th:HK_theorem_II}
    The ground state density of a system minimizes the energy functional $E[n(\br)]$.
    The ground state can therefore be found by minimizing the ground state energy functional with respect to the density.
\end{theorem-non}

The two Hohenberg--Kohn theorems contextualize the problem of finding the electronic structure of any material using \abinit~methods.
Nonetheless, even though the two theorems are powerful claims, they do not provide a practical method of finding the ground state density, and therefore, the properties of the system.
In order to do that, Kohn and Sham~\cite{Kohn_Sham_1965} proposed a method, called the Kohn--Sham scheme.
Without going into much detail, the Kohn--Sham scheme is a self-consistent method, where the full interacting many-body problem is mapped into an auxiliary noninteracting one, but with the same ground state density as the initial problem.

The many-body effects are encoded in a contribution to the ground state energy functional, called the exchange--correlation functional, denoted by $E_{\mathrm{xc}}[n(\br)]$.
Even though this functional is universal, its exact analytical form (if it exists) is unknown.
Therefore, different degrees of approximation are used, like the \gls{lda}, or the \gls{gga}, the latter being a non-local approximation.
It is well known that traditional \dft~suffers from a fundamental problem, called the bandgap problem.
In many materials, \dft~calculations underestimate the bandgap, even drastically in some cases.
In order to improve the outcome from \dft, there are some schemes that go beyond the Kohn--Sham one.
One idea is to use hybrid functionals, consisting in mixing an approximated exchange--correlation functional, say \gls{lda} or \gls{gga}, with the exact Fock exchange.
For a thorough treatment of these subjects, we refer the reader to Refs.~\cite{Martin_2004,Kohanoff_2006}.
Later on in this work, we will mention the use of hybrid functionals in the first-principle calculations necessary for the framework developed for the screening in \bd~materials, pertaining to Chapter~\ref{ch:screening}.
Our implementation for the screening is actually based on key concepts of the next section, where we present briefly the \acrlong{mbpt} formalism, which can also be used to predict electronic properties of materials.


\section{Many-Particle Description of Solids}
\label{sec:many_particle_description}

Up until now, we have been discussing essentially single-particle properties of solids.
Even though matter is composed of many particles, the single-particle problem serves as a good basis for the full many-body problem.
In the single-particle picture, pairwise interactions are either discarded altogether, or treated at the mean-field level.
Doing this can be a good starting point to study material properties, but it leaves out emergent phenomena coming from interactions.
Just like in high-energy physics, where particles can be created or destroyed, in condensed matter some effects can only be described by introducing the concept of quasiparticle---a composite state coming from the coupling between different or identical particles in a solid, that can explain ground state or excited state properties we could not understand otherwise.
For that, we need \glsfirst{mbpt} formalism, which is the non-relativistic cousin of quantum field theory.
As quasiparticles can be created or destroyed, the second quantization formalism, also called the canonical quantization formalism, is necessary.
It is extremely useful in condensed matter, not only for single-particle descriptions of matter (looking at our example of a tight-binding model in section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}), but also to incorporate interactions in the model Hamiltonian.
Within \gls{mbpt}, the single-particle problem without interactions is assumed to be already solved (through the methods discussed in section~\ref{subsubsec:abinit_methods}, most frequently), and the interactions are treated as a perturbation.
Apart from a couple of simple models, a full \abinit~treatment of the full interacting many-body problem beyond perturbation theory is not possible.
Nonetheless, the methods of \gls{mbpt} can explain exotic physical phenomena in condensed matter, such as a microscopic description for superconductivity as explained by the \gls{bcs} theory, the Fermi liquid theory, quantum magnetism, among others.

In this section, we discuss the main concepts needed for future parts of this work, but in a briefly manner.
A detailed discussion of this topic would be too technical and long for the purpose of this thesis, and is beyond the scope of this text.

\subsection{Many-body perturbation theory and $GW$ approximation}
\label{subsec:many_body_perturbation_theory}

In physics, the concept of Green function is very important, with applications throughout many fields.
In the context of condensed matter physics, its role in \gls{mbpt} is ubiquitous.
The Green function, or propagator as in particle physics, is a quantity that helps providing a formal solution to the Schr\"{o}dinger equation for the interacting problem.

In the context of \gls{mbpt}, working with units such that $\hbar = 1$, it can be defined as

\begin{equation}
    \left[\ii \partial_t - \hat{H}\right] \mathcal{G} (\br \sigma t;\br' \sigma' t') = \delta(t-t') \delta(\br - \br') \delta_{\sigma \sigma'} \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent if we represent the second-quantized Hamiltonian operator $\hat{H}$ in real space, and $\sigma$ representing the spin degree of freedom.
We keep the hat for operators in the canonical quantization formalism, and their respective first-quantized version without the hat.
The Hamiltonian is assumed to have two parts, a noninteracting part $\hat{H}_0$, and an interaction part $\hat{V}$.
In the real space basis representation, the total Hamiltonian reads

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:canonical_many_body_Hamiltonian}
    \hat{H} = \sum_{\sigma} \int \dd{\br} \, \hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(\br) H_0 \hat{\psi}_{\sigma}(\br) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\sigma,\sigma'} \int \dd{\br} \int \dd{\br'} \, \hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(\br) \hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\sigma'}(\br') W(\br , \br') \hat{\psi}_{\sigma'}(\br') \hat{\psi}_{\sigma}(\br)
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the interaction potential $W(\br , \br')$ is the screened Coulomb interaction, if we are assuming that it is the only interaction in play.
If the interaction allows spin flipping, then the potential should have two spin indices as well.
The operators $\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(\br)$ and $\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}(\br)$ create and annihilate particles with spin $\sigma$ at position $\br$, respectively.
They are commonly called the field operators.

It is useful to also define the noninteracting Green function $\mathcal{G}^0$, which obeys the equation

\begin{equation}
    \left[\ii \partial_t - \hat{H}_0\right] \mathcal{G}^0 (\br \sigma t;\br' \sigma' t') = \delta(t-t') \delta(\br - \br') \delta_{\sigma \sigma'} \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The noninteracting Green function can be written in the frequency domain as

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}^0  = \frac{1}{\omega - H_0} \equiv

In the Feynman diagrammatic jargon, it is also called the free fermion propagator.

The polarizability and the dielectric function are the topic discussed in the next section.

\section{Dielectric Function and Screening: Quantum Picture}
\label{sec:dielectric_function_quantum}

In this work, we have to compute the inverse of the dielectric function to properly account for electrostatic screening in semiconductors and insulators.
Having an accurate description of screening, one can readily compute the excitonic states.
But first, we have to properly define the dielectric function, or the microscopic dielectric function to be more precise.
In the next subsection, we derive an expression for the \rpa~dielectric function from the \rpa~polarizability, which is routine in the literature~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004,Martin_Reining_Ceperley_2016,Louie_1986}, since the full interacting quantities are not possible to compute in practice.
For that, let us take advantage of the \gls{mbpt} formalism introduced previously.

\subsection{A derivation of the RPA dielectric function}
\label{subsec:RPA_dielectric_function_derivation}

We start by making a change of notation, and write for the polarizability $\chi^{\irr}$ instead of just $\chi$.
This means that the polarizability introduced in the previous section is the irreducible polarizability, which is distinct from the full, or reducible, polarizability that we will soon introduce.
Other changes of notation we make is to write $V_c$ instead of simply $v_c$, and rewrite the convolutions from the previous section as mere multiplications, but that is just for the purpose of simplifying the writing.
We would like to describe the screened potential $W$ in terms of the bare one $V_c$, since the former is the one we want to find, and the latter the one we know.
Let us then rewrite the Dyson equation for the Coulomb interaction~\eqref{eq:Dysons_eq_Coulomb} more compactly as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:W_as_of_vc}
    W = V_c + V_c \chi^{\irr} W \equiv \varepsilon^{-1} V_c \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent by defining the (microscopic) \underline{inverse} dielectric function as

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon^{-1} = 1 + \chi^{\irr} W\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent This definition is not immediately useful, as it is still written in terms of the unknown $W$.
Let us then manipulate the same Dyson equation once more, and cast the term in $W$ from the \gls{rhs} to the \gls{lhs}, and solve for $W$ to find

\begin{equation}
    W = \frac{V_c}{1 - V_c \chi^{\irr}} \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Now, it is just a matter of replacing the previous expression in the definition of the inverse dielectric function to get

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:inverse_dielectric_function}

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - V_c \chi^{\irr}} \Rightarrow \varepsilon = 1 - V_c \chi^{\irr} \,.
\end{equation}

We have then found an expression for the microscopic dielectric function that, after recovering the notation from the previous section, reads

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:microscopic_dielectric_function_def}
    \varepsilon (b,a) = \delta(b-a) - \int \dd{1} v_c(b,1) \chi^{\irr}(1,a) \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Finally, to find the final expression for the microscopic dielectric function we work with later on, we use the \rpa~irreducible polarizability, which reads $\chi^{\irr} \approx \chi^{\irr,\RPA} = \chi^0$, where $\chi^0$ is the noninteracting polarizability, that we shall discuss with detail in the next subsection.
Within \rpa, the irreducible polarizability written in the Hedin's equation~\eqref{eq:polarizability_vertex} reduces to $\chi^{\irr,\RPA} (1,2) = \chi^0 (1,2) = - \ii \int \dd{3} \mathcal{G}_0(1,3) \mathcal{G}_0(3,2)$, whose diagram we have found before in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dyson_equation_W_Feynman_RPA}.
Henceforth, if we recover the explicit spacetime-dependence, we can write

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:dielectric_function_space_time}
    \varepsilon^{\RPA} (\br,\br';t , t') = \delta(\br - \br') \delta(t-t') - \int \dr'' \int \dd t' v_c(\br - \br'') \chi^0(\br'',\br';t,t')
\end{empheq}

After computing the polarizability $\chi^0$, not in the $\br$-space but in the reciprocal $\bk$-space, we can \textit{a posteriori} compute the dielectric function as per~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_space_time}, but in the $\bk$-space as well.
Only after computing the dielectric function, which will have a matrix form as we shall see soon, we can then invert it to obtain the inverse dielectric function.

As an alternative to computing the dielectric function and then inverting it, one could rewrite~\eqref{eq:Dysons_eq_Coulomb} within \rpa~as $W(b,a) =\int \dd{c} \varepsilon_{\RPA}^{-1}(b,c) v_c(c,a)$, where~\cite{Martin_Reining_Ceperley_2016}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dielectric_function_inverse}
    \varepsilon_{\RPA}^{-1}(a,b) = \delta(a-b) + \int \dd{c} v_c(a,c) \chi^{\RPA}(c,b) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent and $\chi^{\RPA}$ is the interacting reducible polarizability at the \rpa~level, that, when resorting to the definition for the full polarizability~\eqref{eq:full_polarizability_definition} but within \rpa, reads

\begin{equation}
    \chi^\RPA(1,2) = \chi^0(1,2) + \int \dd{3} \dd{4} \, \chi^0(1,3) v_c(3,4) \chi^\RPA(4,2) \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We could have written the Dyson equation for the \rpa~reducible polarizabilities above in a diagrammatic notation.
However, we do not work with this relationship in practice, and we would need an extra diagram for the \rpa~version of the reducible polarizability.
At the end, it is only the \rpa~irreducible polarizability $\chi^0$ that we work with, and all the other polarizabilities were introduced for self-contained discussion.
But if we worked with it, the inverse dielectric function within \rpa~could then be obtained by replacing the reducible polarizability in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_inverse} with its \rpa~form to write

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:inverse_dielectric_function_RPA}
    \varepsilon_\RPA^{-1}(a,b) = \delta(a-b) + \int \dd{c} v_c(a,c) \chi^\RPA(c,b) \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent From now on, we omit the label $\RPA$ in the dielectric function or its inverse, and it will always be implicit that the dielectric function is computed within the \rpa, and the polarizability is the noninteracting irreducible one $\chi^0$ given by the Hartree diagram.

For a thorough discussion of these topics, we refer to Richard Martin's book in Ref.~\cite{Martin_Reining_Ceperley_2016} and to the books by H.~Bruus and K.~Flensberg~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004}, and the one by Gerald D.~Mahan~\cite{Mahan}.
The first reference studies the many-electron interacting problem in depth, providing also computational schemes to solve it in practice.
The latter references address the interacting problem, with Ref.~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004} working essentially with the jellium model for the electron gas (which enjoys continuous translation symmetry).
This allows for deep theoretical insights via analytical calculations.
On the other hand, Ref.~\cite{Mahan} is more general, and discusses at times the inhomogeneous electron gas.


\subsection{The RPA dielectric function in reciprocal space}
\label{subsec:rpa_dielectric_function}

We recapitulate the \rpa~dielectric function in real-space and time domain, which reads~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004,Cohen_Louie_2016}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dielectric_function_real_time}
    \varepsilon (\br,\br';t-t') = \delta(\br - \br') \delta(t-t') - \int  \int v_c(\br - \br'') \chi^0 (\br'',\br';t-t') \dr'' \dt' ,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $v_c$ denotes the bare (unscreened) Coulomb potential, and $\chi^0 (\br,\br';t-t')$ is the \rpa~irreducible polarizability, for which we have assumed time translation invariance\footnote{Even though we can perceive the dielectric function as a response to an external potential perturbation of the form $\phi_0 \exp{\ii(\bk \vdot \br - \omega t)}$, which is a time-periodic perturbation, we treat it within the linear regime. This is valid as long as the amplitude $\phi_0$ is small enough. If it is too big, such that perturbative methods do not apply, then we have to account for the whole time dependence, as stated previously in footnote~\ref{fn:time_invariance_ch_2} of Chapter~\ref{ch:classical_electrodynamics}. If it still has periodicity, albeit discrete, we can work under the Floquet formalism of quantum mechanics, already mentioned too~\ref{fn:Floquet_formalism} in this chapter. If the perturbation is not periodic, then we need a full non-equilibrium framework. That can be done, for instance, using the non-equilibrium Green function formalism, also called the Keldysh formalism~\cite{Di_Ventra_2008}.}.
The \rpa~irreducible polarizability, in the frequency domain, within a self-consistent field approach is given by~\cite{Cohen_Louie_2016}

            % Bubble diagram

            \node [scale = 1.6] at (C) {$\chi$};
        \end{scope}
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \equiv \chi^0 (\br,\br';\omega) = \sum_{i,j} (f_i - f_j) \frac{\phi_i^*(\br) \phi_j(\br) \phi_j^*(\br') \phi_i(\br')}{\epsilon_i - \epsilon_j + \hw + \ii \eta}\,,
    \phantom{\chi(\br,\br';\omega)}
\end{equation}


\noindent where $\eta$ is a small infinitesimal parameter, $f_i$ denotes the Fermi--Dirac distribution computed at the energy $\epsilon_i$, and $\phi_i(\br)$ is the $i$-th state, with eigenenergy $\epsilon_i$, that can be obtained within a mean-field approach, such as within \hf~theory or the Kohn--Sham framework of \dft\@.
The many-body \wf~of the associated independent particle system is then constructed through the Slater determinant of the orbitals $\phi_i(\br)$, for which we do not specify the subjacent basis denoted by the indices $i$ and $j$.

We notice that the expression in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_real_time} is a two-time function with (continuous) time translation invariance.
Whence, we may time-Fourier transform it to the frequency domain and write

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dielectric_function_real_frequency}
    \varepsilon (\br,\br';\omega) = \delta(\br - \br') - \int   v_c(\br - \br'') \chi^0 (\br'',\br';\omega) \dr''  \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent since the Fourier transform of the Dirac delta is the identity function, and the Fourier transform of a convolution translates to a multiplication.
The microscopic dielectric function enjoys discrete translation symmetry, so that

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon (\br,\br';\omega) = \varepsilon (\br + \bR,\br' + \bR;\omega)
\end{equation}

\noindent is true for any lattice vector $\bR$, in a perfect crystal where \textbf{discrete} translation symmetry is preserved due to the absence of defects or boundaries.
The same can be said about the polarizability $\chi^0$.
This contrasts with the case of the previous chapter, where translation invariance was continuous.
We are still allowed to Fourier transform in space, but we need to keep track of two momenta, say $\bk$ and $\bk'$, instead of a single one, so that the polarizability is a two-point function in momentum when Fourier transformed from real to reciprocal space, $\mathcal{F}\{\chi^0(\br,\br')\} (\bk,\bk') \equiv \chi^0(\bk,\bk')$.
Luckily, as the polarizability still enjoys discrete translation symmetry, one can show that $\chi^0(\bk,\bk')$ is non-zero only if $\bk' = \bk + \mathbf{g}$, where $\mathbf{g}$ is a reciprocal lattice vector.
Furthermore, we can write any momentum $\bk$ in terms of a vector $\bq$ in the first \gls{bz} plus a vector of the reciprocal lattice $\bG$, $\bk = \bq + \bG$.
The same can be said for the other momentum $\bk' = \bq + \bG'$, with $\bG' = \bG + \mathbf{g}$ just being another vector of the reciprocal lattice.
This allows us to write $\chi^0(\bk,\bk') = \chi^0(\bq + \bG,\bq' + \bG') \equiv \chi^0_{\bG \bG'}(\bq)$, where we can see the polarizability as an infinite matrix for each point $\bq \in \BZ$.
For every momentum $\bq \in \BZ$, each pair of reciprocal lattice vectors $(\bG,\bG')$ corresponds to the matrix element $\chi^0_{\bG \bG'}(\bq)$.

In a basis of Bloch states and at zero temperature, doing the substitution for the indeces $i \to n,\bk$ and $j \to n',\bk'$ in~\eqref{eq:polarizability_RPA_def}, the polarizability reads

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:polarizability_RPA_Bloch_states}
    \chi^0 (\br,\br';\omega) = \sum_{n \bk, n'\bk'} \left[ \frac{\psi_{n' \bk'}(\br) \psi_{n \bk}^*(\br) \psi_{n \bk}(\br') \psi_{n' \bk'}^*(\br') }{\hw -(\epsilon_{n' \bk'} - \epsilon_{n \bk})   + \ii \eta} - \frac{ \psi_{n \bk}(\br) \psi_{n' \bk'}^*(\br) \psi_{n' \bk'}(\br') \psi_{n \bk}^*(\br') }{\hw + (\epsilon_{n' \bk'} - \epsilon_{n \bk}) + \ii \eta} \right]\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Its translation invariance can be proof checked by the identity $\psi_{n \bk}(\br + \bR) = \ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \bR} \psi_{n \bk}(\br)$, coming from the second statement~\ref{Bloch_theorem_II} of Bloch theorem.
The expression for the matrix elements $\chi^0_{\bG \bG'}(\bq)$ can be obtained by direct Fourier transform of the previous expression to obtain

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:polarizability_momentum_frequency}
    \chi^0_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega) = \frac{1}{\vol} \sum_{n,n'} \sum_{\bk,\sigma} (f_{n,\bk} - f_{n',\bk + \bq}) \frac{\mel{n,\bk}{\ee^{-\ii(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br}}{n',\bk + \bq} \mel{n',\bk + \bq}{\ee^{\ii(\bq + \bG') \vdot \br}}{n,\bk}}{\epsilon_{n,\bk} - \epsilon_{n',\bk + \bq} + \hw + \ii \eta}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\vol$ is the volume of the crystal, $n$ and $n'$ are band indices, $\bG$ and $\bG'$ are reciprocal lattice vectors, $\sigma$ represents spin and the sum over momentum $\bk$ is over the \bz.

Defining the polarizability in reciprocal space as a matrix allows doing the same for the dielectric function, that Fourier transformed to reciprocal space reads

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency}
    \varepsilon_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega) = \delta_{\bG \bG'} - v_c(\bq + \bG) \chi^0_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega)
\end{empheq}

\noindent This expression is usually introduced in condensed matter physics courses through the Lindhard function, which is the previous formula in the case of the jellium model, the free homogeneous electron gas.
The matrix elements in Eq.~\eqref{eq:polarizability_momentum_frequency} are equal to 1 when $n=n'$, the dispersion is quadratic and there is no crystalline structure, hence the polarizability and dielectric matrices are scalar functions of $\bq$.
The Lindhard function then reads~\cite{GoncalvesPeres:2016,Cohen_Louie_2016,Ashcroft_Mermin}

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:Lindhard_function}
    \varepsilon (\bq,\omega) = 1 - \frac{2 e^2}{\epsz q^2 V} \sum_{\bk} \frac{f_{\bk + \bq}-f_{\bk}}{\epsilon_{\bk + \bq}-\epsilon_{\bk} + \hbar \omega + \ii \eta}
\end{empheq}

\noindent In the Lindhard function (where the prefactor of $2$ comes from the spin degeneracy), we can notice its intricate dependence on both momentum and frequency.
This was already a concern in the previous chapter, however we only analyzed the local and static limits separately.
Taking the local limit $\bq \to \bzero$ in the previous expression and computing the sum (converted into an integral), one arrives naturally at

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(\omega) = 1 - \frac{\omega_{\text{p}}^2}{\omega^2}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\omega_{\text{p}}$ is the electron plasma frequency.
The previous expression is the Drude dielectric function discussed in the first chapter, found here through a microscopic approach.
This provides a glimpse of the power of quantum mechanics, and reassures the claims made in the previous chapter on how models are used and introduced in classical physics, that in fact derive from physics that it cannot describe.
The tools provided by quantum mechanics allowed obtaining the full dependence of the dielectric function on both momentum and frequency, something we were not able to do in the previous chapter (that can somewhat be achieved classically using non-local hydrodynamic models).

If the material in question is not a metal, but a semiconductor or insulator, then we can only aspire to compute the polarizability and the dielectric function numerically.
The same can be stated about the screened Coulomb potential.
In the jellium approximation, the screened Coulomb potential in reciprocal space is simply given by $W(\bq,\omega) = v_c(\bq)/\vareps(\bq,\omega)$, because there is continuous translation invariance, and as a consequence, the macroscopic dielectric function and the microscopic one coincide.
However, assuming continuous translation invariance for insulators or semiconductors is questionable.
In general, we need to account for the crystal structure, so that the screened Coulomb potential in momentum--frequency domain, obtained by Fourier transforming Eq.~\eqref{eq:W_as_of_vc}, reads

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:screened_dynamic_Coulomb_potential}
    W_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega) =  \varepsilon^{-1}_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega) v_c(\bq + \bG')\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent for which one needs the inverse dielectric matrix, satisfying $\sum_{\bG''} \varepsilon^{-1}_{\bG \bG''} \varepsilon_{\bG'' \bG'} = \delta_{\bG \bG'}$ at every point $\bq$ in the \bz~and for any frequency $\omega$.
Only after having inverted the dielectric matrix, can we estimate the macroscopic dielectric function, $\varepsilon(\bq,\omega)$, through the definition

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:macroscopic_dielectric_function_definition}
    \varepsilon(\bq,\omega) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon^{-1}_{\bzero \bzero}(\bq,\omega)} \neq \varepsilon_{\bzero \bzero}(\bq,\omega)
\end{empheq}

In the previous definition, the inequality is in general true, due to the local field effects that come from the inhomogeneity of the crystal.
This will be a key aspect that we shall leave for later aspect, in Chapter~\ref{ch:screening}.
As we will understand in the next chapter, it is of upmost importance to capture accurately the screening in semiconductors and insulators, mainly \bd~ones, in order to get realistic results for the excitonic states.
Now that we have described the ground state properties of materials, we are in position to introduce the exciton, a quasiparticle emerging from optical excitations of solids, that cannot be ignored when investigating the optical properties of gapped materials.
The theory of excitons in \bd~materials is thus the subject of the next chapter.
