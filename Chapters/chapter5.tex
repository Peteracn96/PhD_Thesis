\chapter{Optical Properties of Nanostructured hBN}
\label{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice}
\glsresetall
In previous chapters, we studied the phenomenological and theoretical aspects of exciton formation and their contribution to the optical response.
We have seen also at the end of Chapter~\ref{ch:classical_electrodynamics} how light can couple to material polar excitations to form the polariton.
In this chapter, we analyze the exciton--polariton resultant from the hybridization of the exciton with the \gls{em} field.

We revisit \gls{hbn}, whose tight-binding model has been presented in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}, a prototypical \bd~material to study excitons.
We wish to engineer the optical properties of \gls{hbn} by applying a \gls{1d} electrostatic potential, resulting in an emergent \gls{1d} superlattice.
Choosing an appropriate form for the potential, it is possible to obtain an analytical expression for the renormalized Hamiltonian for any potential strength, without resorting to perturbative methods.
Since we have an analytical expression for the low-energy Hamiltonian, we can extract the effective masses analytically, which speeds up the process of obtaining the binding energies of the excitons.
Those are obtained within the Wannier--Mott formalism discussed in the previous chapter, where the Wannier equation is solved using the the variational method.
However, due to the potential being \gls{1d}, we introduce a degree of anisotropy on the dispersion which depends on the periodicity and amplitude of the potential.
The fact that the method of the \bse~introduced before for effective models is only useful for isotropic systems, justifies the choice for the methods adopted.
The \bse~method through exact diagonalization of the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian is unfortunately not applicable here---the introduction of the electrostatic potential may spoil the translational invariance, and the code developed in this work, to be discussed in the next chapter, is only relevant for periodic \bd~materials.

The approach adopted in this chapter allows for considering any amplitude or periodicity for the potential, either commensurate or incommensurate with the underlying lattice.
Eventual problems posed by an incommensurate potential are not critical, as we work with the low-energy limit of the tight-binding Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn}.
The variational method offers also the advantage of obtaining an analytical expression for the optical conductivity, apart from a final angular integral that can only be performed analytically in the anisotropic case.
With the optical conductivity, we can finally compute the dispersion relation of the exciton--polariton.
The results that we provide in this chapter have already been reported in the literature in Ref.~\cite{Ninhos_Tserkezis_Mortensen_Peres_2024}.
Herein, we adapt the discussion to the structure of this document, giving at certain times insights that were not given in the original publication.
Essentially, there have been updates in the literature in the meantime, and other references have been found that we did not cite initially in the paper.
Moreover, a dissertation provides more freedom of writing, which permits giving detailed derivations and discussions.

We divide this chapter into five sections.
The first four consist of the theory that we apply in order to get our results, to which we reserve the last section.
In the first Section~\ref{sec:nanostructured_hBN}, we introduce the system under study, while properly contextualizing and motivating this work.
In particular, the peculiar properties of \gls{hbn} on different application fronts shall be stressed once again, as we also did in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method} when we introduced the tight-binding model for \gls{hbn}.
After motivating this work, its technical aspects follow in the next sections.
In the Section~\ref{sec:electronic_band_structure_modulated_hBN}, we derive the expression for the renormalized low-energy Hamiltonian and discuss the new emergent electronic structure.
Next, in Section~\ref{sec:anisotropic_exciton_states} we focus on solving the excitonic problem, that is, finding the excitonic binding energies and wavefunctions for the ``new'' engineered \gls{hbn}.
Afterward, in Section~\ref{sec:exciton_optical_response} we focus on the optical conductivity that we later use to compute the dispersion relation for the exciton--polariton.
Finally, in Section~\ref{sec:results} we present and discuss our results, previously reported in Ref.~\cite{Ninhos_Tserkezis_Mortensen_Peres_2024}.
Some techniques introduced in the previous chapters will be put in practice.

\section{Nanostructured hBN: a One-dimensional Superlattice}
\label{sec:nanostructured_hBN}

The material we study in this chapter, \acrlong{hbn} (\gls{hbn}), is a \gls{2d} material, one among the big family of all the \bd~materials that have been proposed or discovered since the isolation of a single layer of graphene in 2004~\cite{Novoselov_Geim_2004}.
Many materials exhibiting physical phenomena once classified as exotic by the scientific community, like superconductors~\cite{Dong_2021}, strange metals~\cite{Li_2024}, topological insulators~\cite{Zhang_Liu_Qi_Dai_Fang_Zhang_2009}, and ferroelectric materials~\cite{Wang_Meng_2023} are now quite familiar to physicists working in \bd~materials.
Using natural \bd~materials, which already have alluring properties per se, both experimental and theoretical physicists envision stacking monolayers of the same or different materials, just like pieces of Lego, as a way of producing new materials.
If these new structures, called \gls{vdw} materials, consist of monolayers of distinct compounds, then they are called \gls{vdw} heterostructures.
Otherwise, if they consist of monolayers of the same kind, they are called \gls{vdw} homostructures.
The several stacked monolayers present strong chemical bonds along their in-plane extension, and bond with neighboring layers through \gls{vdw} forces, which are much weaker than covalent or ionic bonds.
A typical naturally occurring example is graphite, that some scientists like to call multilayer graphene, which is known to conduct electricity.

The material we study, \gls{hbn}, unlike the semi-metal graphene, presents a wide bandgap as explained before in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}.
These two materials have been combined before by using two \gls{hbn} layers to encapsulate one layer of graphene~\cite{Woessner_2015} to achieve highly confined plasmonic modes in graphene with minimum losses.
It has also been observed experimentally that graphene displays ballistic transport when encapsulated with a thin film of \gls{hbn}~\cite{Banszerus_2016}.
The choice of \gls{hbn} as an encapsulating material is justified by its extremely flat surface and low defect-density~\cite{Abidi_2019}.
Its low density of defects is actually one reason why this material is also promising for applications in quantum optics, as the defects can act as single-photon emitters~\cite{Li_Pershin_2022}.
In its bulk form, \gls{hbn} is also promising for applications in nanophotonics, as we have already discussed in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}, due to its intriguing optical properties in the mid-\gls{ir}, in which \gls{hbn} can sustain phonon--polaritons~\cite{Rizzo_Zhang_2023}.

The observation of superconductivity in twisted bilayer graphene~\cite{Cao_2018} gave birth to a new field in condensed matter physics, called twistronics~\cite{Hennighausen_Kar_2021,Aggarwal_Narula_Ghosh_2023}, where bilayer systems with a relative twist angle display emergent phenomena that its isolated counterparts do not.
The exotic phenomena that we mentioned above that physicists have been so hard trying to seek, are now at reach by tuning the twist angle between stacked monolayers~\cite{Cao_2020,Zhang_2022,Du_Xiao_2024}.
By tuning the twist angle, one can produce different patterns on the atomic arrangement, called moir\'e patterns or superlattices, and in that way invent completely new materials with different electronic, optical, and magnetic properties, among others.
Nowadays, the field of twistronics is a hot playground for theoretical physicists, where they predict exotic effects stemming from strong correlations in moir\'e materials~\cite{Lopes_dos_2007,Xian_Rubio_2019,Song_Bernevig_2022}.
The effect of the moir\'e superlattice has on the electronic properties of a material can also be emulated by applying an external potential on a \bd~material of interest~\cite{Fu_Pixley_2020,Lee_Pixley_2022}.

In this work, we emulate a \gls{1d} superlattice by externally modulating a \gls{hbn} monolayer with an electrostatic potential induced by a patterned grating, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_monolayer}.
In the same figure, the grating is composed of long metallic rods with an alternated bias, that lie under a quartz substrate with permittivity $\varepsilon_2$ that acts as a substrate for the \gls{hbn} monolayer.
The period of the potential $L$ is also marked in the figure.
This display serves as a suggestion of a possible experimental setup to realize a \gls{1d} superlattice.

Admittedly interesting in the \gls{uv} range of the radiation spectrum due to its strong excitonic optical response~\cite{Ferreira_Chaves_Peres_Ribeiro_2019}, we can deem \gls{hbn} promising for polaritonics in the deep \gls{uv} down to the mid \gls{ir} by tuning the electrostatic modulation.
Quite relevant to this work is the experiment in Ref.~\cite{Danielsen_Lassaline_2025} reported after our publication~\cite{Ninhos_Tserkezis_Mortensen_Peres_2024}, where they observed exciton--polaritons in a Fourier-tailored \gls{hbn} flake in a \gls{vdw} heterostructure with a nonzero Rabi splitting, which indicates that the strong-coupling regime was reached~\cite{Danielsen_Lassaline_2025}.
However, in the experimental work the Fourier surface has more than one Fourier component, whereas in our theoretical work we consider a single spatial harmonic defined by the period $L$.
Notwithstanding the use of a more simple potential, we believe that qualitative comparisons are fair.

In Figure~\ref{fig:hBN_monolayer} one can see a schematic representation of a plane wave with frequency $\omega$ impinging on the \gls{hbn} flake that responds to this external perturbation.
We quantify that response when computing the optical conductivity detailed in Section~\ref{sec:exciton_optical_response}.

\section{Electronic Band Structure of Modulated hBN}
\label{sec:electronic_band_structure_modulated_hBN}

The idea of subjecting a \bd~material to an external \gls{1d} periodic potential in order to change its electronic properties is not exactly new.
For instance, the authors in Refs.~\cite{Nature_Louie_2008,PRL_Louie_2008} investigated a graphene monolayer subjected to a \gls{1d} potential to find that the dispersion, initially isotropic, renormalizes along one of the directions while leaving the other intact; new Dirac quasiparticles with an anisotropic low-energy dispersion emerge.
In Ref.~\cite{Louie_2010}, an identical idea for a graphene \gls{1d} superlattice was put in practice, that time with a periodically modulated magnetic field, to see that the carrier velocity renormalizes isotropically, contrary to what they expected.
In another theoretical work with graphene~\cite{Stauber_2017}, it was seen that the effect of introducing an external potential affects the plasmonic effects.
In particular the plasmonic modes along the direction of the potential variation, while the ones perpendicular to it are left unchanged.
We shall see that the introduction of a nonvanishing mass term in the Hamiltonian, when going from graphene to \gls{hbn}, leads to an anisotropic effective mass tensor, where both components in the diagonal renormalize; the enhancement of the anisotropy in the electron dispersion is more pronounced than it is in the massless case.
Only after determining how exactly this anisotropy affects the dispersion of \gls{hbn}, will we see how the excitonic properties are affected.
First and foremost, we need to determine the renormalized Hamiltonian, in the light of what was done for the gapless case of graphene in Ref.~\cite{PRL_Louie_2008}.

We start from the low-energy Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn} around the point $K'$ of the \bz~given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:low_energy_hamiltonian_hBN_K_prime}, and convert the momentum coordinates into spatial derivatives as

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{K'}  = \hbar \vF \bk\vdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+M\sigma_{z}=-\ii \hbar \vF \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\sigma_{x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\sigma_{y}\right)+M\sigma_{z}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Now, we include a periodic potential $V(x)=V(x+L)$, with the period $L$ being much larger than the atomic lattice spacing, in the Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:total_H}
    H = \mathcal{H}_{K'}  + V(x)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent with the two-by-two identity matrix implicit in the potential term.
It is not useful to have the initial Hamiltonian in reciprocal space with the external potential in real space.
The potential term can be removed by introducing the unitary transformation

\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(
    \begin{array}{cc}
       \ee^{-\ii \alpha(x)/2} & -\ee^{\ii \alpha(x)/2}\\
       \ee^{-\ii \alpha(x)/2} & \ee^{\ii \alpha(x)/2}
    \end{array}\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    \alpha(x)=\frac{2}{\hbar \vF}\int_{0}^{x} V(x') \,\dd x'\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Applying this transformation to the Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:total_H}, we obtain

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:transformed_H}
    \overline{H}=\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}[\mathcal{H}_{K'} +V(x)]\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}=\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{K'} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}+V(x)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Now, we need to massage the term in $\mathcal{H}_{K'}$, reading

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:transformed_H0}
    \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{K'} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}=\hbar \vF  \left[\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}+\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right] + M \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We point out that since we are dealing operators, the partial derivatives act as well on the function the whole operator is acting on.
In symbols, this means that, for example for the $x$ derivative, $\partial_x \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}$ should be read as $\partial_x (\mathbb{U}_{\alpha} f(\br))$, where $f(\br)$ is a generic function belonging to the Hilbert space the operator acts on.
Before we work out the terms with the spatial derivatives, let us first simplify the term in $\sigma_{z}$, since it is the simplest.
It evaluates to

\begin{equation}
    M  \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{z} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
                                                        0 & -M \ee^{\ii \alpha}\\
                                                        -M \ee^{-\ii \alpha} & 0
    \end{array}\right)\,, \qq{with} 
    \sigma_z = \left(\begin{array}{cc}
                1 & 0 \\
                0 & -1
               \end{array}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent To work out the term involving the $y$-derivative, we evaluate the matrix element of that term between two arbitrary functions resolved in real space, $f(\br)$ and $g(\br)$, where $\br=(x,y)$.
Later on, we particularize those wavefunctions to wavefunctions electronic states.
With $\sigma_y = \left(\begin{array}{cc}
                0 & -\ii \\
                \ii & 0
               \end{array}\right)$, we can write

\begin{align}
    H_{y}[f,g]  =& \hbar \vF  \int f^{\ast}(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}\frac{\partial g(\br)}{\partial y} \dr = \notag \\
    =& \frac{\hbar \vF}{2}\int f^{\ast}(\br)
        \left(\begin{array}{cc}
                0 & -\ee^{\ii \alpha(x)} \\
                \ee^{-\ii \alpha(x)}  & 0
              \end{array}\right)
              \frac{\partial g(\br)}{\partial y} \dr - \notag \\
    & \frac{\hbar \vF}{2}\int \frac{\partial f^{\ast}(\br)}{\partial y}
          \left(\begin{array}{cc}
            0 & -\ee^{\ii \alpha(x)}\\
            \ee^{-\ii \alpha(x)} & 0
        \end{array}\right)g(\br) \dr ,
\end{align}

\noindent where some clarifications are needed.
First, the integrals are over the region $[0,L] \times [0,L_y]$ in the $xy$ plane with $L_y$ the length of the material in the $y$ direction, and $\times$ denotes Cartesian product.
In the previous equation, the subscript $y$ in $H_{y}[f,g]$ stands for the term with $\sigma_{y}$ (and with $\frac{\partial}{\partial_y}$) in equation~\eqref{eq:transformed_H0}.
We also split the initial integral into two equal terms with a prefactor of $1/2$, and performed an integration by parts in $y$ on one of them.

The term in~\eqref{eq:transformed_H0} that goes with $\sigma_{x}$ is more subtle, since both the unitary transformation and the wavefunctions depend on $x$.
We compute the matrix element between two general functions for that term.
We write


\begin{align}
    H_{x}[f,g]  =& \hbar \vF \int f(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[\mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \big] \dr = \notag \\
                =& \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int f^{\ast}(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \big[\mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br)\big] \dr - \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \big[f^{\ast}(\br)\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\big] \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \dr = \notag \\
                =& \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \left[f^{\ast}(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \frac{\dd \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}}{\dd x} g(\br) +  f^{\ast}(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} \frac{\partial g(\br)}{\partial x} \right] \dr - \notag \\
                 & \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \left[f^{\ast}(\br) \frac{\dd \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}}{\dd x} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) + \frac{\partial f^{\ast}(\br)}{\partial x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \right] \dr ,
\end{align}


\noindent where an integration by parts was done from the first line to the second line, just like we did for $H_y$.
We associate the terms involving the derivative of the unitary operator and obtain

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \left[ f^{\ast}(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \frac{\dd \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}}{\dd x} g(\br) - f^{\ast}(\br) \frac{\dd \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}}{\dd x} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \right] \dr = -V(x) \mathds{1}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathds{1}$ is the two-by-two identity matrix, after doing all simplifications
The previous term cancels with the last term in equation~\eqref{eq:transformed_H}, which leaves only the terms

\begin{equation}
    \overline{H} \left[f,g\right] = \sum_{\iota = x,y,z} H_{\iota} \left[f,g\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have to redefine the term $H_x[f,g]$ as

\begin{equation}
    H_{x}\left[f,g\right]=\frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \left[ f^{\ast}(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} \frac{\partial g(\br)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial f^{\ast}(\br)}{\partial x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \right] \dr ,
\end{equation}

\noindent the term $H_y$ has already been defined above, and

\begin{equation}
    H_{z}\left[f,g\right]= M \int f^{\ast}(\br)  \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{z} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \dr .
\end{equation}

\noindent Later on, we will particularize $f$ and $g$ to physical states.
Before that, we expand $\exp{\ii\alpha(x)}$ in Fourier series as

\begin{equation}
    \ee^{\ii\alpha(x)}=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\,\ee^{\ii\ell G_{0}x}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $G_{0}=\frac{2\pi}{L}$ is defined by the periodicity of the potential, and the Fourier coefficients read

\begin{equation}
    f_{\ell}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\ii[\alpha(x)-\ell G_{0}x]} \dd x\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We assume an even potential of the form

\begin{equation}
    V(x)=V_{0}\cos(G_{0}x)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which implies

\begin{equation}
    \alpha(x)=\frac{2V_{0}}{\hbar \vF G_{0}}\sin(G_{0}x)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Computing $f_{\ell}$ for this potential, we obtain

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    f_{\ell} & =\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\ee^{\ii[\alpha(x)-\ell G_{0}x]}\dd x=\\
             & =\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\ee^{\ii\left[\frac{2V_{0}L}{\hbar \vF 2\pi} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{L} x\right) - \ell \frac{2\pi}{L} x \right]}\dd x=\\
             & =\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ee^{\ii(\beta\sin s-\ell s)}\dd s=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\pi}^{-\pi}\ee^{\ii(\ell s-\beta\sin s)}(-\dd s)=\\
             & =\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ee^{\ii(\ell s-\beta\sin s)}\dd s=J_{\ell}(\beta)\,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we defined the dimensionless parameter

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:beta_definition}
    \beta=\frac{V_{0}L}{\hbar \vF \pi}\,.
\end{equation}

Since the potential is even, in the Fourier series only the terms with even $\ell$ appear.
As $J_{-\ell}(x)=(-1)^{\ell}J_{\ell}(x)$ and $\ell$ is even, we have $J_{-\ell}(x)=J_{\ell}(x)$.
We use the following basis states

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{+}^{m} (\br) =\frac{1}{\sqrt{L L_y}} \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
    \ee^{\ii(\bk + \frac{1}{2} \bG_m)\vdot\br} \qq{and}
    \phi_{-}^{m} (\br) =\frac{1}{\sqrt{L L_y}} \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
    \ee^{\ii(\bk - \frac{1}{2} \bG_m)\vdot\br}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent denoted previously by $f$ and $g$, that have a plane-wave-like spatial part $\varphi_{\pm}^{m}(\br) = \ee^{\ii ( \bk \pm \bG_m/2) \vdot \br}$ and a spinorial part, where $\bG_m$ are the vectors of the reciprocal superlattice given by $\bG_m = m \bG = m G_0 \uvec{x}$, with $| \bk | \ll |\bG|$.
We build the new Hamiltonian using these base states as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:new_H}
    \overline{H}_{\pm, \pm} = \sum_{\iota=x,y,z} H_{\iota}[\phi_{\pm}^{m},\phi_{\pm}^{m}] = \int \big(\phi_{\pm}^{m}(\br)\big)^{\dagger} \overline{H} \phi_{\pm}^{m}(\br) \dr.
\end{equation}

\noindent For the calculation of the matrix elements for the Hamiltonian, we need to evaluate the following quantities

\begin{equation}
    \langle \varphi^{m}_{\pm} \vert \ee^{\ii\alpha(x)} \vert \varphi^{m}_{\pm} \rangle = \int \big(\varphi^{m}_{\pm} (\br) \big)^{*} \ee^{\ii\alpha(x)} \varphi_{\pm}^{m}(\br) \dr = \sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell} \int  \big(\varphi^{m}_{\pm} (\br) \big)^{*} \ee^{\ii\ell G_{0}x} \varphi_{\pm}^{m}(\br) \dr .
\end{equation}

\noindent Using the spatial parts $\varphi^{m}_{\pm}(\br)$ of the states $\phi^{m}_{\pm}(\br)$, we have

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \langle \varphi^{m}_{\pm} \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)} \vert \varphi^{m}_{\pm} \rangle= & \sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{LL_{y}}\int \ee^{-\ii(\bk\pm \frac{1}{2} \bG_{m})\vdot\br}\ee^{\pm i\ell Gx}\ee^{\ii(\bk\pm \frac{1}{2} \bG_{m})\vdot\br} \dr = \\
    = &
        \begin{cases}
            \langle \varphi^{m}_{+} \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)}\vert \varphi^{m}_{+} \rangle=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\pm i\ell G_{0}x} \dd x =f_{0} \,,\\
            \langle \varphi^{m}_{+} \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)}\vert \varphi^{m}_{-} \rangle=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\ii(\pm\ell-m)G_{0}x} \dd x =f_{\pm m} \,,\\
            \langle \varphi^{m}_{-} \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)}\vert \varphi^{m}_{+} \rangle=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\ii(\pm\ell+m)G_{0}x} \dd x =f_{\mp m} \,,\\
            \langle \varphi^{m}_{-} \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)}\vert \varphi^{m}_{-} \rangle=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\pm i\ell G_{0}x} \dd x =f_{0} \,.
        \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent We also note that the spatial derivatives are simply given by

\begin{equation}
        \begin{cases}
        \frac{\partial\phi_{\pm}^{m}}{\partial x} & = \ii \left[k_{x} \pm m \frac{G_{0}}{2}\right]\phi_{\pm}^{m}\,, \text{ and} \\
        \frac{\partial\phi_{\pm}^{m}}{\partial y} & = \ii k_{y}\phi_{\pm}^{m}\,.
        \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent For the new Hamiltonian, we need all the following matrix elements (omitting the superscript $m$ in the states for clarity)

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    H_{x}\left[\phi_{+},\phi_{+}\right] & =\hbar \vF \left(k_{x}+m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right) ,\\
    H_{y}\left[\phi_{+},\phi_{+}\right] & =0 \,,\\
    H_{z}\left[\phi_{+},\phi_{+}\right] & =0 \,,\\
    H_{x}\left[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}\right] & = H_{x}\left[\phi_{-},\phi_{+}\right]=0 \,,\\
    H_{y}\left[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}\right] & = \ii \hbar \vF  k_{y}J_{m}(\beta)=H_{y}^{*}\left[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}\right] ,\\
    H_{z}\left[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}\right] & = H_{z}\left[\phi_{-},\phi_{+}\right]=-M J_{m}(\beta) \,,\\
    H_{x}\left[\phi_{-},\phi_{-}\right] & = \hbar \vF \left(-k_{x}+m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right) \,,\\
    H_{y}\left[\phi_{-},\phi_{-}\right] & =0 \,,\\
    H_{z}\left[\phi_{-},\phi_{-}\right] & =0 \,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where to compute the matrix elements of $H_x$ we used the first Pauli matrix $\sigma_x=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
                  0 & 1 \\
                  1 & 0
                 \end{array}\right)$.
\noindent Inserting the previous expressions in equation~\eqref{eq:new_H}, we obtain the Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \overline{H} = \left(\begin{array}{cc}
                         \overline{H}_{+,+} & \overline{H}_{+,-}\\
                         \overline{H}_{-,+} & \overline{H}_{-,-}
                         \end{array}\right)=\left(
                         \begin{array}{cc}
                         \hbar \vF \left(k_{x}+m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right) & \ii \hbar \vF k_{y}J_{m}(\beta)\\
                         -\ii \hbar \vF k_{y}J_{m}(\beta) & \hbar \vF \left(-k_{x}+m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right)
                         \end{array}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent For convenience, we symmetrize and antisymmetrize the basis states as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \phi_{s}(\br) & =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{+}(\br)+\phi_{-}(\br)]\,,\\
    \phi_{a}(\br) & =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{+}(\br)-\phi_{-}(\br)]\,.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent In this new basis, the Hamiltonian reads

\begin{equation}
    \overline{H}=\frac{1}{2}
    \left(\begin{array}{cc}
          \overline{H}_{+,+}+\overline{H}_{-,-}+\overline{H}_{+,-}+\overline{H}_{-,+} & \overline{H}_{+,+}-\overline{H}_{-,-}-\overline{H}_{+,-}+\overline{H}_{-,+}\\
          \overline{H}_{+,+}-\overline{H}_{-,-}+\overline{H}_{+,-}-\overline{H}_{-,+} & \overline{H}_{+,+}+\overline{H}_{-,-}-\overline{H}_{+,-}-\overline{H}_{-,+}
          \end{array}\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent which after computing all the elements yields

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:new_Dirac_Hamiltonian}
    \begin{aligned}
        \overline{H}_m & =
        \left(\begin{array}{cc}
               \frac{1}{2}G_{0}m\hbar \vF -|J_{m}(\beta)|M & \hbar \vF [k_{x}- \ii J_{m}(\beta)k_{y}] \\
               \hbar \vF [k_{x}+ \ii J_{m}(\beta)k_{y}] & \frac{1}{2}G_{0}m\hbar \vF +|J_{m}(\beta)|M
              \end{array}\right) \\
        & = \mathds{1}\frac{m}{2}G_{0} \hbar \vF + \hbar \vF \big[\sigma_{x} k_{x} + \sigma_{y} J_{m}(\beta) k_{y}\big] - \sigma_{z} M J_{m}(\beta)\,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent which is a massive-Dirac-like Hamiltonian with renormalized parameters, and where we recovered the $m$ label as subscript in the Hamiltonian.
We see that there is a new term with the identity matrix that did not exist initially.
If we focus on the Hamiltonian matching with the original, which corresponds to the case $m=0$, the $y$-component of the Fermi velocity changes as $\vF \rightarrow J_{0}(\beta)\vF $.
Since $\vert J_{0}(\beta)\vert < 1$, an electron traveling along the $y$-direction is slowed down compared to its speed if traveling along the $x$-direction.
This leads to an anisotropic low-energy dispersion.
Also, the energy gap $E_g = 2M$, that we can identify as the \gls{dft} bandgap at the \gls{lda} level, renormalizes to $E_g \rightarrow 2M \vert J_{0}(\beta) \vert \equiv \Delta_g (\beta)$.
We will keep the notation $\Delta_g(\beta)$ for the renormalized \gls{dft} gap, that, as we commented before in the paragraph after Eq.~\eqref{eq:hBN_dispersion_relation}, is not the total bandgap.
The total bandgap includes an exchange correction, $\Delta_{\mathrm{xc}}$, coming from the third term on the \gls{rhs} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:BSE_full}, which we swept under the rug at the time when we derived the \gls{bse} for the first time.
We will see in Section~\ref{sec:results} that this contribution to the bandgap renormalizes as well..
Choosing an appropriate value for either $V_{0}$ or $L$ (actually, the product $V_{0}L$), it is possible to reduce the gap of \gls{hbn}.
With this mechanism, we can control both the gap and the excitonic states, which now become that of an anisotropic material (similarly to phosphorene).

At first glance, it is hard to realize how the previous Hamiltonian falls to the original one in the case where there is no potential.
It is actually possible to conciliate the renormalized Hamiltonian with the original one, which is done in Appendix~\ref{app:hBN_superlattice}.
In the same appendix, we also recover the original Hamiltonian when the period $L$ tends to infinity.

The group velocity components and the two effective masses follow directly from the spectrum of the previous Hamiltonian.
These are given by

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    v_{x} & =\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial \epsilon_c}{\partial k_{x}}=\frac{\hbar \vF^{2}k_{x}}{\epsilon_c(\bk,\beta)} \qq{and}\\
    v_{y} & =\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial \epsilon_c}{\partial k_{y}}=\frac{\hbar \vF^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}}{\epsilon_c(\bk,\beta)} \,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
\epsilon_c(\bk,\beta)=\sqrt{\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\left[k_{x}^{2}+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}^{2}\right] + J_{0}^{2}(\beta)M^{2}}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the renormalized low-energy dispersion of the conduction band.
The diagonal components of the effective mass tensor computed at momentum $\bk$ are given by

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        m_{x}(k_{x},k_{y}) &= \frac{\Big[J_{0}^{2}(\beta)M^{2}+\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\left(k_{x}^{2}+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}^{2}\right)\Big]^{3/2}}{J_{0}^{2}(\beta) \vF^{2} \left(M^{2}+\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}k_{y}^{2}\right)} \qq{and} \\
        m_{y}(k_{x},k_{y}) &= \frac{\Big[J_{0}^{2}(\beta)M^{2}+\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\left(k_{x}^{2}+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}^{2}\right)\Big]^{3/2}}{J_{0}^{2}(\beta) \vF^{2} \left(J_{0}^{2}(\beta) M^{2}+\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}k_{x}^{2}\right)}\,,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent that at zero momentum evaluate to

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        m_{x}&=\frac{|J_{0}(\beta)|M}{\vF^{2}} \qq{and} \\
        m_{y}&=\frac{M}{|J_{0}(\beta)|\vF^{2}}\,.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

The spinors of the new low-energy Hamiltonian are also needed to compute the optical matrix element for the conductivity, as discussed in Sections~\ref{subsec:non_interacting_conductivity} and~\ref{subsec:excitonic_conductivity}.
We change the notation slightly, replacing $M$ with $E_{g}/2$.
Besides that, we define $k_{x} + \ii J_{0}(\beta) k_{y} = k(\beta)\ee^{\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)}$, with $k(\beta)=\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}^{2}}$\,, and define

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:theta_xi_anisotropic_definitions}
    \theta_{k}(\beta)=\arctan\!\left(\frac{J_{0}(\beta)k_{y}}{k_{x}}\right) \qq{and} \xi_{k}=\arctan\!\left(\frac{2\hbar \vF k(\beta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which are the anisotropic analogues of their respective isotropic counterparts in Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta_xi_isotropic_definitions} (apart from changing the notation for momentum from $k$ and $\bk$ to $q$ and $\bq$, respectively).
In this notation, and after some straightforward algebra, the spinor for the conduction band reads

\begin{equation}
     \bC^{c\bk} = \left(
    \begin{array}{c}
        \ee^{-\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)}\frac{\sin\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}}{\cos\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}} \\ 1
    \end{array}\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent that, when multiplied by $\cos\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}$, yields the spinor in the final form that we will use

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conduction_spinor}
     \bC^{c\bk}=\left(
    \begin{array}{c}
        \ee^{-\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)}\sin\frac{\xi_{k}}{2} \\ 
        \cos\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}
    \end{array}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent Analogously, the spinor for the valence band can be written as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:valence_spinor}
    \bC^{v\bk}=\left(
    \begin{array}{c}
        \cos\frac{\xi_{k}}{2} \\ 
        -\ee^{\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)}\sin\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}
    \end{array}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent These last two equations provide the spinors of the renormalized low-energy Hamiltonian, similar to their isotropic versions found before in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mono_hBN_valence_conduction_spinors}.
They do not exactly coincide when $\beta = 0$, because the mass term in the renormalized low-energy Hamiltonian carries a negative sign, as it can be seen in Eq.~\eqref{eq:new_Dirac_Hamiltonian} when $m=0$.

\section{Anisotropic Excitonic States}
\label{sec:anisotropic_exciton_states}

In this section, we provide the theory necessary for the variational calculation of the excitonic states and energies, within a Wannier--Mott description for the excitons.
The variational method was first mentioned in Section~\ref{subsec:born_oppenheimer_approximation}, and it is here applied to solve the Wannier equation.
We wish to compare with the excitons of pristine monolayer \gls{hbn}.
Therefore, we focus on the particular case $m=0$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:new_Dirac_Hamiltonian}, for which the effective low-energy Hamiltonian reads

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:effective_low_energy_H}
    \overline{H}_0 = \hbar \vF [k_{x} \sigma_{x} +J_{0}(\beta)k_{y} \sigma_{y}] - \frac{E_g}{2} J_{0}(\beta) \sigma_{z}
\end{empheq}

The variational wavefunctions need to capture the anisotropy of the dispersion, and the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian for those wavefunctions are not easy to work out.
We separate the kinetic part from the potential part and treat each state separately in different subsections.
The kinetic energy contribution to the Wannier Hamiltonian is given by

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{K} = \frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2\mu_{x}}+\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2\mu_{y}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the effective masses $\mu_{x}$ and $\mu_{y}$ are now in general different.
We do the calculations for the Rytova--Keldysh potential

\begin{equation}
    V_{\RK}(r) = \frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}} \left[\Hz\!\left(\frac{\kappa r}{r_{0}}\right)-Y_{0}\!\left(\frac{\kappa r}{r_{0}}\right)\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent which we have already encountered in Section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit}, where we have a small modification in the argument of the special functions compared to Eq.~\eqref{eq:Rytova--Keldysh_potential_real_space}.
We introduced the parameter $\kappa = (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)/2$, which is the average between the permittivity $\varepsilon_1$ of the material on top and the permittivity $\varepsilon_2$ of the material supporting the \gls{hbn} layer.
These two permittivities are the static ones and account for electrostatic screening in the materials surrounding the \gls{hbn} monolayer~\cite{Ventura_2019}.
The parameter $r_0$ is only material dependent, and for monolayer \gls{hbn} we use $r_0 = 10$ \unit{\angstrom}~\cite{Galvani_2016,Ferreira_2019,Ventura_2019}.
Joining the kinetic and potential contributions, we have for the Wannier Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2\mu_{x}}+\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2\mu_{y}}+V_{\RK}(r)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent so that the excitonic problem can be described by the Wannier equation

\begin{equation}
    H \Psi_{\nu} = E_{b,\nu} \Psi_{\nu}\,.
\end{equation}

As trial wavefunctions for the ground state $1s$ and the first 3 excited states, $2x$, $2y$ and $2s$, we pick the expressions

\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
   \Psi_{1s}(x,y)&=C_{1s} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s} x^{2} + b_{1s} y^{2}}} \,,\\
   \Psi_{2x}(x,y)&=C_{2x} x\, \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{2x} x^{2} + b_{2x} y^{2}}} \,,\\
   \Psi_{2y}(x,y)&=C_{2y} y\, \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{2y} x^{2} + b_{2y} y^{2}}} \,, \qq{and}\\
   \Psi_{2s}(x,y)&=C_{2s} \left(1-d \sqrt{a_{2s} x^{2} + b_{2s} y^{2}}\right) \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{2s} x^{2} + b_{2s} y^{2}}}\,.\\
\end{split}
\end{equation*}

\noindent The parameter $d$ in the $2s$ wavefunction is determined by the orthogonality condition $\braket{ \Psi_{1s}}{\Psi_{2s}}=0$, and the constants $C_{\nu}$ are normalization factors.
These wavefunctions are an approximation of the true ones, but we assume that they describe the problem well enough.
They coincide with the wavefunctions used by J.~N.~S.~Gomes and co-authors in Ref.~\cite{Gomes_Trallero-Giner_Vasilevskiy_2021} for phosphorene.
Therefore, the expressions that we use to calculate the binding energies are the same ones as in Ref.~\cite{Gomes_Trallero-Giner_Vasilevskiy_2021}.
We just complement the information therein provided with abbreviated derivations.
Each wavefunction has two variational parameters that we denote by $a_\nu$ and $b_\nu$, with $\alpha = 1s, 2x, 2y, 2s$.
In this way the anisotropy of the dispersion is accounted for.
In the isotropic case, where $a_\nu = b_\nu$, we recover the wavefunctions that are the analytical solutions of the \bd~hydrogen atom~\cite{2D_Hydrogen_atom_1991}.
For each of these states, we compute the expectation value of the Hamiltonian and minimize it with respect to both parameters.
By solving the minimization condition, we find the values of $a_\nu$ and $b_\nu$.
Then, we go back to the expression for the wavefunction and for the expectation value to obtain the (approximate) eigenstate and energy, respectively.
We repeat the procedure separately for each of the four states.
Naturally, we start with the easiest case, the ground state.
In the discussions that follow, for ease of language and notation, we may tacitly refer to the binding energies as just energies, and use the symbol $E_{\nu}$ instead of $E_{b,\nu}$.

\subsection{Ground State $1s$}
\label{subsec:ground_state_1s}

As a trial wavefunction for the ground state we use

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{1s}(x,y)=C_{1s} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s}x^{2}+b_{1s}y^{2}}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
C_{1s} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(a_{1s}b_{1s}\right)^{1/4}.
\end{equation}

\noindent To compute the expression for the ground-state energy $E_{1s}$ in terms of the variational parameters, we need to evaluate

\begin{equation}
    E_{1s}\!\left(a_{1s},b_{1s}\right)=\langle\Psi_{1s}|H|\Psi_{1s}\rangle=\langle\Psi_{1s}|\mathcal{K}|\Psi_{1s}\rangle+\langle\Psi_{1s}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{1s}\rangle\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We compute first the term involving the kinetic energy

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:integral_in_x_y}
    \langle\Psi_{1s}|\mathcal{K}|\Psi_{1s}\rangle = \int \Psi_{1s}^{*}(x,y)\left[\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2\mu_{x}}+\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2\mu_{y}}\right]\Psi_{1s}(x,y) \dr \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent As a first step, we perform a change of variables proposed by Rodin et al.~in~\cite{Rodin_Carvalho_Castro_Neto_2014},

\begin{equation}
    X= s_{x} x\,,\,Y = s_{y}y\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    s_{x/y}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{x/y}}{2\bar{\mu}}} \qq{with} \bar{\mu} = \frac{\mu_{x}\mu_{y}}{\mu_{x}+\mu_{y}}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent In this way, the kinetic energy that now reads

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{K} = -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\nabla^{2}
\end{equation}

\noindent has the usual form of isotropic systems but with a different mass, and the Laplacian is with respect to the new coordinates $(X,Y)$.
The infinitesimal element of area in the old coordinates transforms into

\begin{equation}
\dd x \dd y=\frac{\dd X \dd Y}{s_{x}s_{y}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent so the integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:integral_in_x_y} reads

\begin{equation}
-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\frac{1}{s_{x}s_{y}}\int \Psi_{1s}^{*}(X,Y)\nabla^{2}\Psi_{1s}(X,Y)\, \dd X \dd Y\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The wavefunction as a function of the new variables is

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{1s}(X,Y)=C_{1s}\ee^{-\sqrt{\delta_{x}X^{2}+\delta_{y}Y^{2}}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we introduced new parameters $\delta_{x}=a_{1s}s_{x}^{-2}$ and $\delta_{y}=b_{1s}s_{y}^{-2}$.
Next, we compute the Laplacian of the wavefunction in the $(X,Y)$ coordinates and then perform another change of variables to elliptical coordinates at the end, given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic}
    X=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\delta_{x}}}\cos\theta\,, \,Y=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\delta_{y}}}\sin\theta\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent For the integral, we need the absolute value of the Jacobian, which is $\rho/\sqrt{\delta_{x}\delta_{y}}$\,.
Then, the integral reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        & -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\frac{1}{s_{x}s_{y}}\int \Psi_{1s}^{*}(X,Y)\nabla^{2}\Psi_{1s}(X,Y)\, \dd X \dd Y = \\
       =& -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\frac{C^{2}}{s_x s_y\sqrt{\delta_{x}\delta_{y}}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \rho\, \ee^{-2\rho}\left[\delta_{x}\left(\cos^{2}\theta-\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\rho}\right)+\delta_{y}\left(\sin^{2}\theta-\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\rho}\right)\right] \dd \rho \dd\theta =\\
       =& -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\frac{C^{2}}{s_x s_y\sqrt{\delta_{x}\delta_{y}}}\frac{-1}{4}\pi\left(\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}\right)=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}\!\left(\frac{a_{1s}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b_{1s}}{\mu_{y}}\right),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent after making all substitutions.
Now, we have left the term of the matrix element derived from the potential to calculate.
For that term, we change from Cartesian coordinates to polar coordinates, and define the function $\alpha(\theta)=2 \sqrt{a_{1s}+(b_{1s}-a_{1s})\sin^{2}\theta}$, to obtain

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:potential_matrix_element}
    \langle\Psi_{1s}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{1s}\rangle=\sqrt{a_{1s}b_{1s}}\frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathds{K}\!\left(\frac{\kappa r}{r_{0}}\right) r\, \ee^{-\alpha(\theta) r} \dd r \dd \theta\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we also defined $\mathds{K}(z) \equiv \Hz\left(z\right)-Y_{0}\left(z\right)$.
Fortunately, the radial integral has an analytical expression.
We calculate analytically the auxiliary integral


\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:auxiliar_radial_integral}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_{0}(\alpha,\kappa_0) & =\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{K}(\kappa_0 r)\, \ee^{-\alpha r} \dd r = \\
        & =\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\kappa_0^{2}}}\left[\log\!\left(\frac{\kappa_0+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\kappa_0^{2}}}{\alpha}\right)+\log\!\left(\frac{\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\kappa_0^{2}}}{\kappa_0}\right)\right]\,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent that depends on $\theta$ through the function $\alpha(\theta)$, and where we introduced as well $\kappa_0 = \kappa/r_{0}$\,.
The radial integral of interest in Eq.~\eqref{eq:potential_matrix_element}, that we will denote by $I$, relates to the previous one through

\begin{equation}
    I(\alpha,\kappa_0) \equiv \int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{K}(\kappa_0 r)r \ee^{-\alpha r} \dd r=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}I_{0}(\alpha,\kappa_0)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The final expression for the energy of the excitonic ground state can only be written in terms of this last integral, and it reads

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    E_{1s}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}\!\left(\frac{a_{1s}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b_{1s}}{\mu_{y}}\right)-\sqrt{a_{1s}b_{1s}}\frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}I(\alpha,\kappa_0) \,\dd \theta
\end{empheq}

Requiring an absolute minimum for this energy consists in solving $\partial E_{1s}/\partial a_{1s}=\partial E_{1s}/\partial b_{1s}=0$ for $a_{1s}$ and $b_{1s}$. 
As an example, for black phosphorus encapsulated in \gls{hbn}~\cite{Gomes_Trallero-Giner_Vasilevskiy_2021}, we have the parameters $\mu_{x}=0.99m_{e}$ and $\mu_{y}=0.12m_{e}$ where $m_e$ is the electron mass, $\kappa=4.5$ and $r_{0}=2.5$~\unit{\nano\meter}.
For this set of parameters, the binding energy evaluates to $E_{1s}=-257$ \unit{\milli\electronvolt}, and the variational parameters evaluate to $a_{1s}=2.45\text{ \unit{\nano\meter}}^{-2}$ and
$b_{1s}=0.533\text{ \unit{\nano\meter}}^{-2}$.
Computing the derivatives is straightforward, so we present here only the final expressions

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \frac{\partial E_{1s}}{\partial a_{1s}}  &= \frac{\hbar^2}{4 \mu_x} - \frac{e}{8 \pi \epsz r_0} \sqrt{\frac{b_{1s}}{a_{1s}}} \int_0^{2 \pi} I(\alpha,\kappa_0) + 4 \frac{a_{1s} \cos^2 \theta}{\alpha} \partial_\alpha I \,\dd \theta\,, \\
        \frac{\partial E_{1s}}{\partial b_{1s}}  &= \frac{\hbar^2}{4 \mu_y} - \frac{e}{8 \pi \epsz r_0} \sqrt{\frac{a_{1s}}{b_{1s}}} \int_0^{2 \pi} I(\alpha,\kappa_0) + 4 \frac{b_{1s} \sin^2 \theta}{\alpha} \partial_\alpha I \,\dd \theta\,.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent The excited states follow, starting with the states $2x$ and $2y$.

\subsection{Excited States $2x$ and $2y$}
\label{subsec:excited_states_2x_2y}

We discuss first the calculations for the $2x$ state.
We recall the expression for the trial wavefunction

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:2x_trial_wavefunction}
    \Psi_{2x}(x,y)=C_{2x}x \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{2x}x^{2}+b_{2x}y^{2}}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the normalisation constant can be found using the same change of variables as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic} for the integral $\int |\Psi_{2x}\left(\br\right)|^2 \dr $, and it is given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:2x_normalisation}
    |C_{2x}|^2 = \frac{8 a_{2x} \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \,.
\end{equation}

To obtain the kinetic contribution to the energy, we followed the same strategy as in the $1s$ case.
We change from $(x,y)$ to $(X,Y)$ coordinates and compute the Laplacian in these new coordinates, followed by a change of variables similar to~\eqref{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic}, performing the radial integral and finally the angular integral.
After all substitutions, we obtain

\begin{equation}
    \langle\Psi_{2x}|\mathcal{K}|\Psi_{2x}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}\left(\frac{3 a_{2x}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b_{2x}}{\mu_{y}}\right) .
\end{equation}

\noindent The potential term reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \langle\Psi_{2x}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{2x}\rangle &= \frac{8 a_{2x} \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0)\, \dd\theta = \\
                                            &= \frac{8 a_{2x} \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \frac{-e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^3}{\partial \alpha^3} I_0 (\alpha, \kappa_0)\, \dd\theta \,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we made use of the auxiliary function $I_0(\alpha,\kappa_0)$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:auxiliar_radial_integral}, having thus for the binding energy

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    E_{2x} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}\left(\frac{3 a_{2x}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b_{2x}}{\mu_{y}}\right) - \frac{8 a_{2x} \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0) \, \dd\theta
\end{empheq}

The derivatives with respect to $a_{2x}$ and $b_{2x}$, respectively, read

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        &\frac{\partial E_{2x}}{\partial a_{2x}} = \frac{3 \hbar^{2}}{8\mu_{x}} - \\
        &\frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}} \left[ \frac{4 \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{\pi}  \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0) }{\partial \alpha^2}  \dd\theta + \frac{8 \sqrt{a_{2x}^3 b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial }{\partial a_{2x}} \left(\frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \right) \dd\theta \right], \notag \\
        &\frac{\partial E_{2x}}{\partial b_{2x}} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8\mu_{y}} -\\
        &\frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}} \left[ \frac{4 \sqrt{a_{2x}^3/ b_{2x}} }{3 \pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0) }{\partial \alpha^2}  \dd\theta +  \frac{8 \sqrt{a_{2x}^3 b_{2x}}}{3 \pi}  \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial }{\partial b_{2x}} \left(\frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0) }{\partial \alpha^2} \right) \dd\theta \right]. \notag
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent For simplicity, we omitted the explicit forms of the derivatives $\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{2x}} \left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0) \right)$ and $\frac{\partial }{\partial b_{2x}} \left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0) \right)$.

Since the calculations for the state $2y$ are analogous, we show only the key results.
For the binding energy, we have

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    E_{2y}= \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}\left(\frac{a_{2y}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{3 b_{2y}}{\mu_{y}}\right) - \frac{8 b_{2y} \sqrt{a_{2y} b_{2y}}}{3 \pi} \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0) \,\dd\theta
\end{empheq}

\noindent and for the derivatives of the binding energy we have

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        &\frac{\partial E_{2y}}{\partial a_{2y}} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8\mu_{x}} - \\
        &\frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}}  \left[ \frac{4 \sqrt{b_{2y}^3/a_{2y}}}{3 \pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \dd\theta + \frac{8 \sqrt{a_{2y} b_{2y}^3}}{3 \pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin^2 \theta \frac{\partial }{\partial b_{2y}} \left(\frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \right)\dd\theta \right] ,  \notag\\
        &\frac{\partial E_{2y}}{\partial b_{2y}} = \frac{3 \hbar^{2}}{8\mu_{y}} - \\
        &\frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}}\left[ \frac{4 \sqrt{a_{2y} b_{2y}}}{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \dd\theta + \frac{8 \sqrt{a_{2y} b_{2y}^3}}{3 \pi}  \int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin^2 \theta \frac{\partial }{\partial a_{2y}} \left(\frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \right)\dd\theta \right] .\notag
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\subsection{Excited State $2s$}
\label{subsec:excited_state_2s}

We reserve this entire section to the calculations for the state $2s$, as for this state there are more intermediate steps.
First, the normalisation condition leads to

\begin{equation}
    |C_{2s}|^2 = \frac{4 \sqrt{a_{2s} b_{2s}}}{\pi (2 - 4d + 3 d^2)}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the value of $d$ is obtained by the orthogonality condition $\langle \Psi_{1s} | \Psi_{2s} \rangle = 0$.
To ease the notation, from now on we will write $a$ and $b$ instead of $a_{2s}$ and $b_{2s}$.
Since the system is anisotropic, $d$ is not a simple function of the parameters $a_{1s}$ and $b_{1s}$.
To see this, let us write

\begin{align}
    \langle \Psi_{1s} | \Psi_{2s} \rangle &= C_{1s} C_{2s} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(1-d\sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}\right) \ee^{-\sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s}x^{2}+b_{1s}y^{2}}} \dd x \dd y = 0 \rightarrow \notag \\
    &\rightarrow d = \frac{\mathcal{I}_1}{\mathcal{I}_2} \,,
\end{align}

\noindent where we defined

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{I}_1 &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}  \ee^{-\sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s}x^{2}+b_{1s}y^{2}}} \dd x\dd y \qq{and} \\
        \mathcal{I}_2 &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}} \ee^{-\sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s}x^{2}+b_{1s}y^{2}}} \dd x \dd y \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent For both integrals, we perform a change of variables like in~\eqref{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic}.
The first integral reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{I}_1 &= \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho\, \ee^{-\rho} \ee^{-\rho \sqrt{\frac{a_{1s}}{a}\cos^2 \theta + \frac{b_{1s}}{b}\sin^2 \theta}} \frac{\dd \rho \dd\theta}{\sqrt{a b}} = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho \, \ee^{- t(\theta) \rho} \frac{\dd \rho \dd \theta}{\sqrt{a b}} =\\
        &=  \int_{0}^{2 \pi} t^{-2}(\theta) \frac{\dd\theta}{\sqrt{a b}} \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent if we also define $t(\theta) \equiv 1 + \sqrt{\frac{a_{1s}}{a}\cos^2 \theta + \frac{b_{1s}}{b}\sin^2 \theta}$\,.
For the second integral, we have

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{I}_2 &= \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho^2 \ee^{-\rho} \ee^{-\sqrt{\frac{a_{1s}}{a}\cos^2 \theta + \frac{b_{1s}}{b}\sin^2 \theta} \rho } \frac{\dd \rho \dd \theta}{\sqrt{a b}} = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho^2 \ee^{-t(\theta) \rho} \frac{\dd \rho \dd \theta}{\sqrt{a b}} =\\
        &=  \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \frac{2}{t^{3}(\theta)} \frac{\dd \theta}{\sqrt{a b}} \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent For convenience, we define the auxiliary functions

\begin{equation}
    f_1 (a,b) = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} t^{-2}(\theta)\, \dd \theta \qq{and} f_2 (a,b) = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} t^{-3}(\theta)\, \dd \theta ,
\end{equation}

\noindent so that the orthogonality condition reads

\begin{equation}
    d (a,b) = \frac{f_1 (a,b)}{2 f_2 (a,b)} \,.
\end{equation}

Let us now compute the energy of the $2s$ state.
The kinetic term is obtained similarly to the previous cases, and equals

\begin{equation}
    \langle\Psi_{2s}| \mathcal{K}|\Psi_{2s}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}\!\left(\frac{a}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b}{\mu_{y}}\right) \frac{2 + d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2}  = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{4} F(a,b) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we defined

\begin{equation}
    F(a,b) = \left(\frac{a}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b}{\mu_{y}}\right) \frac{2 + d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The potential term is computed through the integral

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \langle\Psi_{2s}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{2s}\rangle &= C_{2s}^2 \int \left(1 - d \sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}} \right)^2 \ee^{-2 \sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}} V_{\RK}(r) \dr= \\
                                            &= 2 \frac{\sqrt{a b}}{ (2 - 4d + 3 d^2)}  \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_0} \int_0^{2 \pi} \left[1 + \alpha (\theta) \frac{d}{2} \partial_\alpha \right]^2 I(\alpha, \kappa_0) \, \dd \theta \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent We define now the function

\begin{equation}
    G(a, b) = \frac{1}{ (2 - 4d + 3 d^2)}  \int_0^{2 \pi} \int_0^\infty \mathds{K}\big(\kappa_0 \alpha(\theta) \rho \big) (1- d \rho)^2 \rho\, \ee^{-2 \rho} \dd \rho \dd \theta \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where for the integral we performed a change of variables as in~\eqref{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic}, so that we can write the potential term in the alternative way

\begin{equation}
    \langle\Psi_{2s}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{2s}\rangle = 2  \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz  r_0} G(a, b) \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent In this way, the expression for the binding energy of the $2s$ state can be more compactly written as

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    E_{2s} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{4} F(a,b) - 2  \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz  r_0} G(a, b)
\end{empheq}

\noindent which is the expression that we need to minimize.
For that, we need the derivatives of the function $d (a,b)$, and they read

\begin{equation}
    \partial_{a} d = \frac{\partial_{a} f_1 f_2 - f_1 \partial_{a} f_2}{2 f_2^2} \qq{and}
    \partial_{b} d = \frac{\partial_{b} f_1 f_2 - f_1 \partial_{b} f_2}{2 f_2^2} \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent and also the derivatives of $f_i$ with $i=1,2$, which are given by

\begin{equation}
    \partial_{a} f_i = \frac{a_{1s} (1+i)}{2a^2} \int_0^{2 \pi} \frac{\cos^2 \theta\, t^{-(2+i)} (\theta)}{t (\theta)-1}  \dd \theta \qq{and}
    \partial_{b} f_i = \frac{b_{1s} (1+i)}{2b^2} \int_0^{2 \pi} \frac{\sin^2 \theta\, t^{-(2+i)} (\theta)}{t (\theta)-1}  \dd \theta \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The derivatives of the function $F$ are straightforward to calculate

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \partial_{a} F &= \frac{2 + d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \frac{1}{\mu_x} + \left(\frac{a}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b}{\mu_{y}}\right) \frac{8 -8d - 4d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \partial_{a} d, \\
        \partial_{b} F &=  \frac{2 + d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \frac{1}{\mu_y} + \left(\frac{a}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b}{\mu_{y}}\right) \frac{8 -8d - 4d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \partial_{b} d\,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent Regarding the derivatives of $G$, we define two additional auxiliary integrals,

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_1 (\theta) &= \int_0^\infty \mathds{K} \big(\kappa_0 \alpha (\theta) \rho\big) (1 - d  \rho) \rho^2 \ee^{-2 \rho} \dd \rho \qq{and} \\
        I_2 (\theta) &= \int_0^\infty \mathds{K}' \big(\kappa_0 \alpha (\theta) \rho\big) (1 - d  \rho)^2 \rho^2 \ee^{-2 \rho} \dd \rho \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathds{K}' \big(\kappa_0 \alpha (\theta) \rho\big)$ denotes the derivative of $\mathds{K} (z)$ computed at the point $z=\kappa_0 \alpha (\theta) \rho$.
In this way, the derivatives of $G$ read

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \partial_{a} G =& - \frac{1}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \int_0^{2 \pi} \frac{\kappa}{2 r_0 a^2} \frac{\cos^2 \theta}{\sqrt{a^{-1} \cos^2 \theta + b^{-1} \sin^2 \theta}} I_2(\theta)\,  \dd \theta + \\
                        & \frac{\partial_{a} d}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \left[ (4 - 6d)G -2 \int_0^{2 \pi}I_1(\theta) \, \dd \theta \right] \qq{and} \\
        \partial_{b} G =& - \frac{1}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \int_0^{2 \pi} \frac{\kappa}{2 r_0 b^2} \frac{\sin^2 \theta}{\sqrt{a^{-1} \cos^2 \theta + b^{-1} \sin^2 \theta}} I_2(\theta)\,  \dd \theta + \\
                        & \frac{\partial_{b} d}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \left[ (4 - 6d)G -2 \int_0^{2 \pi}I_1(\theta) \, \dd \theta \right] \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Finally, the derivatives of the energy of the $2s$ state can be compactly written as

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        &\frac{\partial E_{2s}}{\partial a} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} \partial_{a} F \big(a,b, d(a,b)\big) - 2 \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_0} \partial_{a} G \big(a,b, d(a,b)\big)\,, \\
        &\frac{\partial E_{2s}}{\partial b} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} \partial_{b} F \big(a,b, d(a,b)\big) - 2 \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_0} \partial_{b} G \big(a,b, d(a,b)\big) \,.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

We stress that to compute the excited state $2s$, the ground state $1s$ has to be computed first, since the orthogonalization parameter $d$ depends on the variational parameters of the ground state.
In the next section, we see how we can compute the excitonic optical conductivity using the exciton energies and wavefunctions.

\section{Excitonic Optical Response}
\label{sec:exciton_optical_response}

If we subject a material to a drive from a monochromatic field with frequency $\omega$, the expression for the excitonic optical conductivity in the linear regime, and in the dipole approximation, is given by the following sum over the excitonic states $\nu$~\cite{Pedersen_2015}

\begin{equation}
    \sigma (\omega) = \frac{e^2}{8 \pi^3 \ii \hbar} \sum_\nu \left[ E_\nu \frac{|\Omega_\nu|^2 }{E_\nu - (\hbar \omega+\ii\Gamma)} + E_\nu \frac{|\Omega_\nu|^2 }{E_\nu + (\hbar \omega+\ii\Gamma)} \right]
    \label{eq:optical_conductivity}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the quantity $\Omega_\nu$ depends on the exciton wavefunction $\Psi_\nu(\bk)$, and recalling its expression~\eqref{eq:Berry_curvature_definition}, it is computed by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Berry_curvature_definition_new}
    \Omega_\nu =  \sum_{\bk} \Psi_{\nu} (\bk)\, \uvec{e} \vdot \langle v, \bk | \br | c, \bk \rangle
\end{equation}

\noindent with no sum over bands, since we consider the most simple two-band model for \gls{hbn}, $\uvec{e}$ is the polarization vector, $\Psi_{\nu} (\bk)$ is the momentum-space wavefunction of the exciton in the state $\nu$, and the braket is the optical matrix element we have encountered before in Section~\ref{subsec:non_interacting_conductivity}.
However, we still need to explain how we compute it in practice.
Let us then call $\Omega_\nu$ the interacting optical matrix element, or excitonic optical matrix element.

In this work, we consider light polarized along the $x$-direction and along the $y$-direction separately, so that the polarization vector will write $\uvec{e} = \uvec{x}$ or $\uvec{e} = \uvec{y}$ accordingly.
After computing the optical matrix element, we shall compute its dot product with $\uvec{e}$.
We will then have the two distinct conductivities $\sigma_{xx}$ and $\sigma_{yy}$ in the main diagonal of the conductivity tensor.
But before that, we need to list the exciton wavefunctions in momentum space to compute the excitonic optical matrix element.

\subsection{Exciton wavefunctions in momentum space}
\label{subsec:exciton_wavefunctions_momentum_space}

Now, we derive the wavefunction of the excitonic ground state in momentum space, and the excited states will follow.
For that, we just need to Fourier transform $\Psi_{1s}(\br)$.
In the isotropic case, the integral is calculated by fixing the orientation of the wavenumber $\bk$, and then write $\bk\vdot\br= k r \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\bk$ and $\br$, alongside with a change of variables from cartesian to polar coordinates.
Now we need to do different, because we are evaluating the anisotropic case.
We perform instead the change of variables

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic_x_y}
    \begin{cases}
        x=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{a_{1s}}}\cos\theta\,,\\
        y=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{b_{1s}}}\sin\theta\,,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent which is in fact a change from cartesian to elliptic coordinates.
\noindent Then, the Fourier transform evaluates to

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Psi_{1s}(\bk) & =\int \ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br}\,\Psi_{1s}(\br) \dr = \\
        & = C_{1s}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \ee^{-\ii\rho\left(\frac{k_{x}}{\sqrt{a_{1s}}}\cos\theta+\frac{k_{y}}{\sqrt{b_{1s}}}\sin\theta\right)}\frac{\rho \ee^{-\rho}}{\sqrt{a_{1s}b_{1s}}} \dd \rho \dd \theta=\\
        & = C_{1s}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_{1s}b_{1s}}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\ii\frac{k_{x}}{\sqrt{a_{1s}}}\cos\theta+\ii\frac{k_{y}}{\sqrt{b_{1s}}}\sin\theta\right)^{2}} \dd\theta=\\
        & =\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\left(a_{1s}b_{1s}\right)^{1/4}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{k_{x}^{2}}{a_{1s}}+\frac{k_{y}^{2}}{b_{1s}}\right)^{3/2}}\, ,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent which is an analytical expression after all.
Fortunately, that will be the case for all states.
We note that when $a_{1s}=b_{1s}$\,, the previous expression depends only on $k=|\bk|$, and we recover the isotropic case.
For all the excited states, we follow a similar reasoning.
For the excited states $2x$ and $2y$, respectively, we have

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:2x_2y_wavefunctions_momentum}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Psi_{2x} (\bk) &= - C_{2x} \frac{3 \ii \pi k_x/a_{2x}}{\left(1 + \frac{k_x^2}{a_{2x}} + \frac{k_y^2}{b_{2x}}\right)^{5/2}} \qq{and} \\
        \Psi_{2y} (\bk) &= - C_{2y} \frac{3 \ii \pi k_y/b_{2y}}{\left(1 + \frac{k_x^2}{a_{2y}} + \frac{k_y^2}{b_{2y}}\right)^{5/2}} \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The excited state $2s$, even though more involved, has also an analytical expression for its Fourier transform, which reads

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{2s} (\bk) = \sqrt{\frac{16 \pi}{(2 - 4d + 3d^2)}} (a_{2s} b_{2s})^{-1/4} \frac{1-2d + (1 + d) \left(\frac{k_x^2}{a_{2s}} + \frac{k_y^2}{b_{2s}}\right)}{\left(1 + \frac{k_x^2}{a_{2s}} + \frac{k_y^2}{b_{2s}}\right)^{5/2}} \,.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Optical matrix element: the anisotropic case}
\label{subsec:optical_matrix_element_anisotropic_case}

The optical matrix element can be computed by using the identity

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:optical_matrix_element}
    \langle v,\bk|\br|c,\bk\rangle=\frac{\langle v,\bk|[H_0,\br]|c,\bk\rangle}{\epsilon_{v \bk}-\epsilon_{c \bk}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where the Hamiltonian is the noninteracting one, the ket and the bra correspond to Bloch states of an electron in the conduction and valence bands, respectively, and $\epsilon_{v \bk}$ and $\epsilon_{c \bk}$ are the energies of an electron in the corresponding Bloch states.
By coincidence, the subscript $0$ we use in the Hamiltonian $\overline{H}_0$, which stands for the particular case of $\overline{H}_m$ when $m=0$, coincides with the same symbol used in Section~\ref{sec:optical_effects} to denote the noninteracting Hamiltonian $H_0$.
It happens that $\overline{H}_0$ is our noninteracting Hamiltonian at the moment, so there is no problem in repeating the symbol, we just advert for the different meanings it may have.
In practice, the numerator can be evaluated in terms of the commutator $[\overline{H}_0,\br]$, if in the low-energy Hamiltonian we perceive the momentum components as operators, and take the commutator with $\br$ as an operator by using the canonical commutation relations $[x_i,p_j] = \ii \hbar \delta_{ij}$.
After getting the commutator, we compute the matrix element replacing the bra and the ket with their respective spinors of the low-energy Hamiltonian, denoted by $\bC^{v \bk}$ and $\bC^{c \bk}$.
However, it may not seem obvious that the optical matrix element can be computed in this way, without refering explicitly to the Bloch wavefunctions.
We are not aware of any justification for this in the literature, even though it has been used before and with good final results~\cite{Ventura_2019,Mauricio_2022,Henriques_Epstein_Peres_2022,Henriques_PRB2022}.
Therefore, we give an instructive explanation, even though it may feel ad hoc.

To evaluate the optical matrix element, one considers first  the term $H_0 \br$ in the commutator.
Then, we can insert a resolution of the identity $\sum_{n,\bk'} |n,\bk'\rangle \langle n,\bk'|$ between $H_0$ and $\br$, assuming it is complete.
The matrix elements of $H_0$ between the Bloch states $\bra{v,\bk}$ and $\ket{n,\bk'}$ can be written as real-space integrals.
If we represent the Bloch wavefunctions in the tight-binding approximation per Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_state_LCAO} as a linear combination of the atomic orbitals, then the matrix element transforms into a linear combination of integrals, which are the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian between the atomic orbitals, eventually located at different positions in the lattice---these are nothing more than the transfer integrals, which define the parameters of the tight-binding model.
Since in the wavefunction we have a sum over unit cells $\bR$ with the imaginary exponential $\ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \bR}$, then, at the end, we would just have to calculate $\left(\bC^{v\bk}\right)^{\dagger}\! H(\bk) \bC^{n,\bk'}$, where $H(\bk)$ is the Bloch Hamiltonian and the arrays $\bC^{n,\bk}$ are its eigenvectors, that we derived earlier and are given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:valence_spinor} and~\eqref{eq:conduction_spinor} for the valence and conduction bands, respectively.
For the position operator, we have integrals of the form $\int \phi_{\beta}(\br - \bR -\bt_j)\, \br\, \phi_{\beta'}(\br - \bR' -\bt_{j'}) \dr$.
In the tight-binding approximation, if the orbitals are point-like, the integrals amount to matrix elements $\br_{j \beta,j' \beta'} \delta_{\bR \bR'}$ of a position operator with a matrix representation that is diagonal in the $\bR$ variable, that we can denote by $\bar{\bar{\br}}$.
The kronecker delta $\delta_{\bR \bR'}$ simplifies the calculations.
Then, we have to use the fact that $\ket{n,\bk'}$ is an eigenstate of $H_0$, so in the sum in the resolution of the identity, only the term $\ket{n,\bk'} = \ket{v,\bk}$ survives, simplifying even more the calculation.
Then at the end, if we choose the lattice gauge without loss of generality, we would have at the end an expression of the form

\begin{equation*}
\begin{gathered}
    \sum_{\bR} \ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \bR} \sum_{i\alpha,i'\alpha'} (C^{v \bk}_{i\alpha})^* H_{i\alpha,i'\alpha'}(\bR) C^{v \bk}_{i'\alpha'} \sum_{j'\beta',j\beta} (C^{v \bk}_{j'\beta'})^* \bar{\bar{\br}}_{j'\beta',j\beta} C^{c \bk}_{j\beta} \\
    = (\bC^{v\bk})^\dagger H(\bk) \bC^{v\bk} (\bC^{v\bk})^{\dagger} \bar{\bar{\br}}\, \bC^{c \bk} = \\
    = (\bC^{v\bk})^\dagger H(\bk) \bar{\bar{\br}}\, \bC^{c \bk} \,,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation*}


\noindent where $H(\bk)$ is simply the Bloch Hamiltonian that we have defined before in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_Hamiltonian_definition}.
Upon doing something similar for the other term in the commutator $\br H$ and taking the difference with the term above, we arrive at the intermediate formula

\begin{equation}
    \langle v,\bk|[H_0,\br]|c,\bk\rangle = (\bC^{v\bk})^\dagger [H(\bk),\bar{\bar{\br}}] \bC^{c \bk}\,.
\end{equation}

Now, we expand the Bloch Hamiltonian $H(\bk)$ around the point where the exciton is localized, the points $\bK$ or $\bK'$ in the case of \gls{hbn}, so that we can replace $H(\bk) \to \overline{H}_0$, where the low-energy Hamiltonian is now the renormalized one, and  we allow for the momentum variable $\bk$ to become the momentum operator, and $\bar{\bar{\br}}$ the position operator.
Then, we can replace the commutator $[H_0,\br]$ with the commutator

\begin{equation}
    [\overline{H}_0,\br] = -\ii \hbar  \vF  \left[ \sigma_{x}\uvec{x}+J_{0}(\beta)\sigma_{y}\uvec{y} \right] \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which was obtained by using the canonical commutation relations.
Then, the initial matrix element between the Bloch states of the commutator between the Hamiltonian and the position operator evaluates to

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:commutator_matrix_element}
    \langle v,\bk|[H,\br]|c,\bk\rangle  \approx -\ii \hbar  \vF  (\bC^{v\bk})^\dagger \left[ \sigma_{x}\uvec{x}+J_{0}(\beta)\sigma_{y}\uvec{y} \right] \bC^{c\bk}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the arrays $\bC^{v,\bk}$ and $\bC^{c,\bk}$ are the spinors of the low-energy Hamiltonian that we have defined before in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:valence_spinor} and~\eqref{eq:conduction_spinor} for the valence and conduction bands, respectively.
Inserting them in~\eqref{eq:commutator_matrix_element} and going back to the optical matrix element~\eqref{eq:optical_matrix_element}, we find

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:optical_matrix_element_final}
    \langle v,\bk|\br|c,\bk\rangle \approx \frac{\ii\hbar  \vF}{\epsilon_{v \bk}-\epsilon_{c \bk}}\left\{\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{\xi_{q}}{2}\right)\left[\uvec{x}-J_{0}(\beta)\uvec{y}\right]-\ee^{-2 \ii \theta_{q}(\beta)}\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\xi_{q}}{2}\right)\left[\uvec{x}+J_{0}(\beta)\uvec{y}\right]\right\}\,.
\end{equation}

Finally, we can compute $\Omega_\nu$ for each excitonic state.

\subsubsection{Ground state $1s$}
\label{subsubsec:ground_state_1s}

We recall that the variational wavefunction of the $1s$ state in momentum space, easing the notation and drop the subscripts accompanying the variational parameters, is given by

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{1s}(\bk) =\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\left(a b\right)^{1/4}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{k_{x}^{2}}{a}+\frac{k_{y}^{2}}{b}\right)^{3/2}} \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent To compute the interacting optical matrix element~\eqref{eq:Berry_curvature_definition_new}, we convert the sum over momentum into an integral, for which the following functions will be useful

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \gamma(\theta) &= \sqrt{\cos^{2}\theta+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sin^{2}\theta} \,,\\
        \sigma(\theta) &=\sqrt{\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{a}+\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{b}} \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent With these functions, we can write the quantities

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \epsilon_{c \bk}-\epsilon_{v \bk} &= \sqrt{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)+4\hbar^{2} \vF ^{2}[k_{x}^2 + J^2_{0}(\beta)k_{y}^2]} = \sqrt{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta) + 4\hbar^{2} \vF ^{2}k^{2} \gamma^{2}(\theta)}\,, \\
        \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\xi_{q}}{2}\right) &= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2 \hbar  \vF k \gamma(\theta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)^{2}}}\,, \qq{and} \\
        \sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\xi_{q}}{2}\right) &= \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2 \hbar  \vF k \gamma(\theta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)^{2}}}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent that appear in the optical matrix element, and for the wavefunction

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{1s}(\bk) =  \frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\left(a b \right)^{1/4}}\frac{1}{\left[1+k^2 \sigma^2(\theta) \right]^{3/2}}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent In the excitonic optical matrix element, we have two terms; one that comes from the cosine squared in the optical matrix element in Eq.~\eqref{eq:optical_matrix_element_final}, and another that comes from the sine squared in the same equation.
So we write $\Omega  = \Omega^{\cos}_{1s} + \Omega^{\sin}_{1s}$, and treat both terms separately.
We consider first light polarized along the $x$-axis.
The term that comes from the cosine reads

\begin{equation}
    \Omega^{\cos}_{1s} = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \dd\theta \int_{0}^{\infty} \dd k \frac{ 2\sqrt{2\pi} \,\ii \hbar \vF \,k  \left\{\frac{1}{2} + \left[2 \sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2\hbar \vF k \gamma(\theta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)^{2}}\right]^{-1}\right\}}{\left(a b\right)^{1/4} \left[1+k^{2}\sigma^{2}(\theta)\right]^{3/2} \sqrt{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)+4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}k^{2}\gamma^{2}(\theta)}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have already performed the usual change of variables to polar coordinates.
In the isotropic case, the integrand function does not depend on $\theta$, so from the angular integral it only comes a factor of $2 \pi$, and the radial integral has an analytical form.
However, the angular integral is not trivial anymore, but the radial integral is still analytical, so we perform it first.
We obtain
%\todo{Say somewhere that it is the angular integral that defines the selection rules, and cite Henriques paper, and the 3 papers by Mauricio, tutorial and those with Pedersen}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Omega^{\cos}_{1s} =& \ii \hbar \vF\frac{-2\sqrt{2\pi}}{(a b)^{1/4}}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \Biggl\{ \frac{\hbar \vF \gamma(\theta)-E_{g}|J_{0}(\beta)|\sigma(\theta)}{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sigma^{3}(\theta) - 4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\sigma(\theta)\gamma^{2}(\theta)} -\\
        & - \frac{E_{g}\hbar \vF|J_{0}(\beta)|\gamma(\theta) \arcsec \left(\frac{E_{g}|J_{0}(\beta)|\sigma(\theta)}{2\hbar \vF\gamma(\theta)}\right)}{\left[E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sigma^{2}(\theta)-4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\gamma^{2}(\theta)\right]^{3/2}} \Biggl\} \dd\theta \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\noindent leaving only the angular integral that can only be evaluated numerically.
The term that goes with the sine reads

\begin{equation}
    \Omega^{\sin}_{1s} = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \dd\theta \int_{0}^{\infty} \dd k \frac{ 2\sqrt{2\pi}\, \ii \hbar \vF\, k\, \ee^{-2\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)} \left\{\frac{1}{2} - \left[2 \sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2\hbar \vF k \gamma(\theta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)^{2}}\right]^{-1}\right\}}{\left(a b\right)^{1/4} \left[1+k^{2}\sigma^{2}(\theta)\right]^{3/2} \sqrt{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)+4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}k^{2}\gamma^{2}(\theta)}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent and performing the radial integral we obtain

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Omega^{\sin}_{1s} =& \ii \hbar \vF \frac{-2\sqrt{2\pi}}{(a b )^{1/4}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Bigg\{\frac{-\hbar \vF\gamma(\theta)}{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sigma^{3}(\theta)-4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\sigma(\theta)\gamma^{2}(\theta)} + \\
        &+\frac{E_{g}\hbar \vF|J_{0}(\beta)|\gamma(\theta)\arcsec\left(\frac{E_{g}|J_{0}(\beta)|\sigma(\theta)}{2\hbar \vF\gamma(\theta)}\right)}{\left[E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sigma^{2}(\theta)-4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\gamma^{2}(\theta)\right]^{3/2}}\Bigg\}\ee^{-2\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)} \dd\theta \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\noindent to be evaluated numerically as well.
The excitonic optical matrix element, when taking the polarization along the $x$-axis, is then simply $\Omega^x_{1s} = \Omega^{\cos}_{1s} + \Omega^{\sin}_{1s}$, where we put an $x$ in superscript to stress that the calculation was for light polarized along the $x$-direction.
For light polarized along the $y$-direction, we can identify from the optical matrix element~\eqref{eq:optical_matrix_element} an extra factor of $J_0(\beta)$, and also a relative sign between the cosine and sine terms, so that $\Omega^y_{1s} = J_0(\beta) (\Omega^{\cos}_{1s} - \Omega^{\sin}_{1s})$.

\subsubsection{Excited states $2x$, $2y$ and $2s$}
\label{subsubsec:excited_states_2x_2y}

Regarding the excited states $2x$ and $2y$, we need to argue why they do not contribute to the optical conductivity, since it is not straightforward to see why is that (even in the anisotropic case).
We consider the wavefunction of the $2x$ state given in equation~\eqref{eq:2x_2y_wavefunctions_momentum}, and note that it has a factor of $\cos \theta$ if we change to polar coordinates.
The angular integral in the interacting optical matrix element that comes from the cosine in the optical matrix element is of the form

\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta\, \dd \theta\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the factor $\cos \theta$ comes from the wavefunction, and the function $f$ encodes all the other factors.
We can perform a change of variables $\vartheta = \pi/2 - \theta$ and write

\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta \, \dd \theta = -\int_{\pi/2}^{- 3 \pi/2}  f(\sin^2 \vartheta) \sin \vartheta\, \dd \vartheta =\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}  f(\sin^2 \vartheta) \sin \vartheta\, \dd \vartheta = 0\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where in the second equality we swapped the limits of the integral and did a shift of $\pi/2$, which is allowed since the integrand is periodic.
The last equality holds because we are integrating an odd function over an interval centered at the origin.
The term that comes from the sine of the optical matrix element is more delicate.
It reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
         &\int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta\, \ee^{-2\ii\arctan\left(J_{0}(\beta)\arctan\theta\right)} \dd \theta = \\
        =&\int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta \cos\!\big(2\arctan\left(J_{0}(\beta)\arctan\theta\right)\big) \dd \theta - \\
         &\ii \int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta \sin\!\big(\arctan\left(J_{0}(\beta)\arctan\theta\right)\big) \dd \theta\,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent If in the term on the second line of the equation of this equation we perform the same change of variables, we end up with an integral of an odd function again, so it does not contribute.
For the second term, we simply make a shift of $-\pi$ in the integration limits, since the integrand function is odd from the start, so that term vanishes as well.
For the excited state $2y$, all the previous arguments apply as well, so it does not contribute to the conductivity just like the state $2x$.
We are left only with the calculation of the optical conductivity deriving from the state $2s$.
As in the case of the $1s$ state, the radial integral is analytical, and the angular integral has to be performed numerically.
Even though the expression of the radial integral is analytical, it is very complicated, hence we refrain from writing it here.

At the end, we have only the contributions from the $1s$ and $2s$ state to the optical conductivity, that for $x$ polarization reads

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:optical_conductivities}
    \sigma_{xx} (\omega) &= \frac{e^2}{8 \pi^3 \ii \hbar} \sum_{\nu=1s,2s} E_\nu \frac{|\Omega^x_\nu|^2 }{E_\nu - (\hbar \omega + \ii\Gamma)} + (\omega \to - \omega)^*
\end{empheq}

\noindent where $(\omega \to - \omega)^*$ is used to refer to the nonresonant term in the sum, which we discard since its contribution is negligible.
For $y$ polarization the expression is analogous.
We have seen now how to compute the optical conductivity for the system under study.
In the next section we discuss the results obtained.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organize this section in three parts.
In the first one, we discuss the renormalized band structure and the consequence for the excitons, namely the binding energies.
With the information from the excitonic energies and states, we can compute the excitonic optical conductivity as we have just discussed in the previous section, which is the subject of the second part of this section.
In the third part, we finish with the results for the dispersion of the exciton--polaritons and the material absorption based on the theory provided in Section~\ref{sec:polaritonics_fundamentals}.

\subsection{Bandgap and excitonic binding energies}
\label{subsec:band_gap_and_binding_energies}

As we have seen in Section~\ref{sec:electronic_band_structure_modulated_hBN}, the effective masses renormalize as $m_x = m^* |J_{0}(\beta)|$ and $m_y = m^*/ |J_{0}(\beta)| $, where $m^* = E_g/2\vF^2$ is the isotropic effective mass of electrons in pristine \gls{hbn}.
This means that as $J_{0}(\beta) \to 0$, the dispersion becomes more and more anisotropic.
To study the effects of this anisotropy in the electronic and excitonic properties of the system, we compute the bandgap and the binding energies of the excitonic states $1s$, $2x$, $2y$ and $2s$ for varying $\beta$, from the case $\beta=0$ without potential to $\beta=2.4$ sufficiently close to the first zero of the Bessel function $J_0(\beta)$.
We stress that the bandgap includes the electron--electron exchange correction $\Delta_{\mathrm{ex}}(\beta)$, which also renormalizes with $\beta$, and whose calculation is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:exchange_energy} of the Appendix~\ref{app:hBN_superlattice}.
The results are displayed in Fig.~\ref{fig:Eb_of_beta}.
The value of the total bandgap, also called the fundamental gap, for $\beta=0$ is the one of pristine monolayer \gls{hbn} that, with our estimation, is of $6.2$ \unit{\electronvolt}, which is not very distant from the values reported in the literature from \gls{dft} calculations using hybrid functionals~\cite{PhysRevB.106.045118}, or taking into account many-body effects using the $GW$+\gls{bse} approach.
The work by F.~Ferreira et al.~in Ref.~\cite{Ferreira_2019} has, in Table 1 therein, a comprehensive list of values for the bandgap of \gls{hbn} obtained from different works that use the $GW$ and $GW$+\gls{bse} methods.
Another report listing different values for the bandgap of \gls{hbn} in the literature is Ref.~\cite{PhysRevB.106.045118}, where Table I from the main text displays analogous results to those of Ref.~\cite{Ferreira_2019}, also from \abinit~calculations at both \gls{dft} and $GW$ levels.

The total bandgap $\Delta = \Delta_g(\beta) + \Delta_{\mathrm{ex}}(\beta)$ as a function of $\beta$ computed with our approach is in Figure~\ref{fig:Eb_of_beta}\pnl{a}.
As one increases the potential strength $V_0$ (or the period $L$), the bandgap of \gls{hbn} decreases from its initial value to zero, with the bandgap closing at the first zero of $J_0(\beta)$.
This information is relevant to calculate the sub-gap optical conductivity, because the excitonic levels $E_{\nu}$ determine the resonant frequencies.
Knowing the bandgap $\Delta$ and the binding energy $E_{b,\nu}$, we know the exciton energies $E_\nu = \Delta - |E_{b,\nu}|$\footnote{Most commonly in the literature, the binding energy is presented as a negative value, to stress that it is a bound state. We work with the absolute value of the binding energy, making the analysis indifferent to the convention.}.
The exciton binding energies, obtained in the light of the variational principle, are represented in Figure~\ref{fig:Eb_of_beta}\pnl{b}.
The $s$ states are in red, and the $p$ states $2x$ and $2y$ are in black.
The $p$ states being originally degenerate in the isotropic case $\beta=0$, something expectable from using the Wannier--Mott description, are not degenerate anymore in the anisotropic case $\beta \neq 0$.
Also, even though the binding energies generically decrease with increasing $\beta$, the $2y$ state presents a non-monotic behavior, but it is always the first excited state.
On the contrary, the binding energy of the $2x$ state always decreases, and at some point around $\beta \approx 1.6$, the binding energies of the states $2x$ and $2s$ match.
After that point, the $2s$ state is now the second excited state, whereas the $2x$ is the highest one (among these four states).
Not surprisingly, the $1s$ state stays always as the ground state.
We could use variational wavefunctions for even higher energy states, however, the optical response is typically dominated by the lowest energy states.
Moreover, as they go higher in energy, successive states become closer in energy and harder to distinguish.
In addition to that, as we get closer to the end of bandgap the optical response is dominated by \gls{ipa} conductivity, which we do not analyze in this chapter.

To obtain the binding energies, we applied the variational method as explained in Section~\ref{sec:anisotropic_exciton_states}, in which we have to find the variational parameters that minimize the binding energy.
So, for each value of $\beta$, we have to find the roots of the equations of the form $\partial E_{\nu} / \partial a_{\nu} = \partial E_{\nu} / \partial b_{\nu} = 0$, where the forms of the derivatives for each one of the states $1s$, $2x$ and $2y$, and $2s$ are given at the end of the Subsections~\ref{subsec:ground_state_1s},~\ref{subsec:excited_states_2x_2y} and~\ref{subsec:excited_state_2s}, respectively.
This was done using the method \lstinline[language=Mathematica]|FindRoot| of the software Mathematica~\cite{Mathematica}.

\subsection{Sub-gap optical conductivity and absorption}
\label{subsec:optical_conductivity_and_absorption}

In this section, we show the results obtained for the optical conductivity computed through Eq.~\eqref{eq:optical_conductivity}.
Figure~\ref{fig:sigma_grid} displays the results for the excitonic optical conductivity for different realizations of $\beta$, and for light polarized along the $x$ and $y$ axes separately.
Since the conductivity is complex, we plot separately the real and imaginary parts in the top and bottom panels, respectively.
The panels on the left are for $\uvec{e} = \uvec{x}$, while the panels on the right are for $\uvec{e} = \uvec{y}$.
We have justified before why the $2x$ and $2y$ states do not contribute to the optical conductivity in Section~\ref{subsubsec:excited_states_2x_2y}, the reason why in panels~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{a} and \ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{b} there are only two peaks for each realization of $\beta$; the higher peak corresponds to the $1s$ state, while the smaller peak corresponds to the $2s$ state.

The results for the isotropic case without potential, $\beta=0$, represented by the black curves in the plots of Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}, are in agreement with the literature where the same methods for the excitonic states, energies and conductivity  were used~\cite{Ventura_2019,Ferreira_2019}.
In turn, they are in good qualitative agreement with the results from the $GW$+\gls{bse} approach as discussed in Ref.~\cite{Ferreira_2019}.
Note that the results for the case $\beta=0$ appear on both sides of Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}, where the real and imaginary parts of the conductivity are the same regardless of the polarization.
They seem to have different heights for $\uvec{e}=\uvec{x}$ and $\uvec{e}=\uvec{y}$, but the scales of the vertical axes are different.
The scales of the vertical axes have to be different, otherwise we would not be able to see the conductivity in the case of $\uvec{y}$ polarization, since it gets attenuated when $\beta$ increases.
On the contrary, the opposite happens for the $\uvec{x}$ polarization; as $\beta$ increases, the oscillator strength increases as well, and the peaks in the conductivity are enhanced.
Besides that, the resonant frequencies, defined by the excitonic levels as $\hbar \omega = E_{\nu}$, tend to redshift with increasing $\beta$.

A particularity of the most extreme case of $\beta=2.3$, is the existence of only one zero in the imaginary part of the conductivity, as one can see in Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{c}.
This will affect the behavior of the polariton dispersion, since we discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:polaritonics_dispersion_relation} whether polaritons are supported depending on the sign of $\Im \{ \sigma(\omega) \}$.

Knowing the optical conductivity, besides being able to calculate the dispersion relation for polaritonic modes, we can also compute the optical absorption for normal incidence by using the expression~\eqref{eq:absorption}, and the definitions of reflected and transmitted radiation fractions in Eqs.~\eqref{subeq:reflected_radiation} and~\eqref{subeq:transmitted_radiation}, respectively.
When $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are given in terms of the permittivities of media $1$ and $2$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:absorption} reads

\begin{equation} \label{eq:optical_absorption_repeated}
        \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{opt}} = 1 - \qty|\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_1} - \sqrt{\varepsilon_2}  - \frac{\sigma(\omega)}{c \epsz}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \frac{\sigma(\omega)}{c \epsz}}|^2 - \frac{\Re\{\sqrt{\varepsilon_2}\}}{\Re\{\sqrt{\varepsilon_1}\}} \qty|\frac{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_1}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \frac{\sigma(\omega)}{c \epsz}}|^2 \,.
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.35cm}

\noindent The optical absorption as a function of the frequency is shown in Figures~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{a} and~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{b}, for light polarized along the $x$- and $y$-directions, respectively.
Each panel has the absorption spectrum for the same values of $\beta$ as in Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}.
For $\uvec{y}$ polarization, in the panel~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{b} we can see that initially the $1s$ peak slightly increases, but after that the material becomes less responsive, with an attenuated absorption as $\beta$ increases.
The behavior of the absorption for $\uvec{x}$ polarization in Fig.~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{b} has more interesting features.
For instance, from pristine \gls{hbn} where the $1s$ peak is naturally more intense than the $2s$ peak, by modulating externally and fine tuning the potential, it is possible to have the $2s$ higher than the $1s$ peak in the same material.
This can be seen in panel \pnl{a}, in the blue and gray curves more specifically.
Even though this behavior is unusual, it has already been observed experimentally~\cite{Ju_2017} and predicted theoretically~\cite{Henriques_PRB2022}.
However, in the same plot, we can see that the intensity of the $2s$ peak does not increase monotonically with $\beta$.

To better understand the results in Figure~\ref{fig:absorption}, we use again the expression~\eqref{eq:optical_absorption_repeated}, but now computed at the resonant frequencies.
We stress that we have the optical conductivity for $\beta$ varying continuously from zero up to the first zero of $J_0(\beta)$, we just selected a couple of values for $\beta$ to plot $\sigma(\omega)$.
Then, it is possible to compute the maximum of the absorption at the $1s$ and $2s$ peaks separately as a function of $\beta$, and the result can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:absorption_peaks} below.
Therein, we can observe on panel \pnl{a} how each peak behaves with varying $\beta$ for both polarizations.
For $\uvec{x}$ polarization, the $1s$ absorption peak (continuous black line) decreases monotonically, while the $2s$ peak (continuous red line) increases until it surpasses the $1s$ peak, but it decreases at the end.
For $\uvec{y}$ polarization, it is the $1s$ peak (dashed black line) that has a nonmonotonic behavior, while the $2s$ peak (dashed red line) always decreases, but unlike the case $\uvec{e} = \uvec{x}$, the $1s$ peak stays always higher than the $2s$ peak.

That we can have a $1s$ peak higher than a $2s$ peak can be explained by close inspection of the equations~\eqref{subeq:reflected_radiation},~\eqref{subeq:transmitted_radiation} and~\eqref{eq:optical_absorption_repeated}.
Writing the conductivity $\sigma = \sigma' + \ii \sigma''$ in terms of its real and imaginary parts, assuming that the permittivities are real, and working out $\mathcal{R} = \qty|r|^2$ alongside $\mathcal{T} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_2 / \varepsilon_1} \qty|t|^2$ (casting the factor $\epsz c$ into $\sigma$ for simplicity) we get

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reflection_and_transmission}
    \mathcal{R} = \frac{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2}- \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2 + (\sigma'')^2}{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2  + (\sigma'')^2} \,, \qq{and} \mathcal{T} = \frac{4 \sqrt{\varepsilon_1 \varepsilon_2}}{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2 + (\sigma'')^2}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent If we analyze the previous expressions at the frequency of the $1s$ excitonic resonance where $\sigma'' = 0$ holds and let $\sigma' \to \infty$, corresponding to the most extreme case of $\beta=2.3$, we can easily see that $\mathcal{R} \to 1$ and $\mathcal{T} \to 0$.
Hence, the system reflects all the incident light, and $ \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{opt}} \to 0$.
When the oscillator strength grows immensely making $\sigma'$ grow to infinity, the \bd~material behaves like a perfect mirror, similarly to the surface of a very good conducting metal.
But if we now consider the resonance at the $2s$ excitonic state energy, we see that both $\sigma'$ and $\sigma''$ are finite (the imaginary part has only one zero in the most extreme case $\beta=2.3$, see Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{c}), which means that $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are finite, and consequently, so is the absorption at the $2s$ resonance.
Initially, without potential, the $1s$ resonance is still higher than the $2s$ resonance, both in the conductivity and the absorption.
Nonetheless, this does not contradict our reasoning of the perfect mirror when $\beta=2.3$.
The reason is that the absorption peak is not monotonic with $\sigma'$.
To see this, we work out explicitly the expression for $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{opt}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:optical_absorption_repeated} and simplify to yield

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{opt}} = \frac{4 \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} \sigma'}{ \left(\sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \sigma'\right)^2 + (\sigma'')^2} \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Looking always at the same resonant frequency, we have always $\sigma''=0$ (unless the $2s$ resonance is close enough to the $1s$ resonance so that $\sigma'' \neq 0$), and then the absorption peak is given by

\begin{equation}
    \max_{\omega}{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{opt}}} = \frac{4 \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} \sigma'}{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the real part of the conductivity is computed at a resonant frequency, corresponding either to the excitonic energy $E_{1s}$ or $E_{2s}$.
Plotting the previous expression as a function of $\sigma'$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:absorption_peaks}\pnl{b}, we observe that indeed it is not monotonous, which explains why we can have in general a $2s$ peak higher than a $1s$ peak, even if the oscillator strength of the $1s$ resonance is higher than that of the $2s$ resonance.

To finish the analysis of the interaction of light with our system, and within the theme of strong-coupling, in the next section we will look at the dispersion relation of the emergent exciton--polaritons our system can support.

\subsection{Exciton--polaritons}
\label{subsec:exciton-polaritons}

In this section, we analyze the dispersive behavior of the polaritonic modes our system can support.
Polaritons have been introduced before in Chapter~\ref{ch:classical_electrodynamics}, within a classical description of the theory of light--matter interaction.
In that chapter, we derived the equations that provide the dispersion relation of polariton for each type of \gls{em} field polarization.
The dispersion relation of the polariton differs between the two possible polarizations, $s$ or \gls{te}, and $p$ or \gls{tm} (see Fig.~\ref{fig:TE_and_TM_modes}).
In our calculations for the system at hand, we observed that the \gls{te} modes, if existent, present either free-light-like dispersion or weak field confinement, something we already expected from the discussion in Subsection~\ref{subsec:polaritonics_dispersion_relation}, and also from the literature~\cite{Bludov_primer,GoncalvesPeres:2016}.
The \gls{tm} modes are decidedly more interesting, as we will see from our results.
Just for the purpose of refreshing the reader's memory, we present once more their dispersion relation

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:TM_modes_disp_rel}
    \frac{\varepsilon_1}{\kappa_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{\kappa_2} + \ii  \frac{\sigma_{xx} (\omega)}{\epsz \omega} = 0
\end{empheq}

\noindent where $\kappa_j = \sqrt{q^2 - \varepsilon_j \omega^2/c^2}$, $j=1,2$, $q$ is the wavenumber of the polariton, and $\sigma_{xx}$ is the conductivity we obtained in the previous section (see Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}).
The dispersion relation $\omega(q)$ was obtained as the numerical solutions of the equation just above, which exist whenever $\Im \{\sigma_{xx}(\omega)\} > 0$.
We obtained different dispersions for different realizations of $\beta$, which are supported in different frequency ranges determined by the excitonic levels $E_{1s}$ and $E_{2s}$.
We selected $\beta=0$, the case without potential, and two other values of $\beta=0.5$ and $\beta=2.3$, this last corresponding to the case where the oscillator strength is the highest in Figs.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid} and~\ref{fig:absorption}.
The results are displayed in the three panels of Fig.~\ref{fig:polaritons_disp_rel}, presenting the frequency as a function of the real and imaginary parts of $q$.
In panel \pnl{a}, we can see how different the dispersion of the polariton is compared to that of free light in vacuum and in quartz, in continuous and dashed black lines, respectively.
In the cases $\beta=0$ of panel \pnl{a} and $\beta=0.5$ of panel \pnl{b}, these black lines cannot be distinguished because the wavenumber of the polariton is much larger than that of free light.
The increase in $\Re\{q\}$ is more gradual for lower frequencies, but presents a pronounced kink at higher frequencies alongside $\Im\{q\}$, suggesting these modes can achieve high \gls{em} field confinements.

The dispersion relation of the polariton in the $\beta=2.3$ case of panel \pnl{c} is quantitatively distinct from the previous two, as one can see when comparing the scale of the $q$-axis with the scales of panels \pnl{a} and \pnl{b}.
The third case presents wavenumbers of order of the \unit{\micro \meter}, whereas in the first two cases $q$ is of the order of the \unit{\nano \meter}.
Nonetheless, in panel \pnl{c} it is still higher than that of free light, whose dispersion can now be clearly seen in the straight black lines that were not visible in panels \pnl{a} and \pnl{b}.
It is also qualitatively distinct from the previous two, due to the fact that in the region of frequencies where the mode is supported, there is a frequency corresponding to $E_{2s}$ where the imaginary part of the conductivity is not zero due to its proximity to $E_{1s}$ (see gray curve in Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{c}).
The consequence is a back-bending of the dispersion curve at the frequency where $\Re\{\sigma_{xx}\}$ has a bump resulting from the nonzero oscillator strength of the $2s$ excitation.
This phenomenon has been predicted before by C.~Wolff and co-authors in Ref.~\cite{Wolff:2018}, where the theory therein developed applies in general to any periodic optical system with loss and dispersion, and to any kind of materials strongly coupled to resonances.
What we observe here is the particular case of the strong coupling of light with excitons in a \bd~material, and when taking a complex wavevector but real frequency.
Event though the reverse situation of complex frequency and real wavevector is outside of the scope of this work, it has also been considered in Ref.~\cite{Wolff:2018}.

For the reader to get a feeling for the spatial distribution of the \gls{em} field of a surface polariton, in Fig.~\ref{fig:TM_mode_map} we display a map of the electric and magnetic fields of a \gls{tm} polarised mode, represented by the black arrows and the colorful background, respectively.
We can see that both fields decay fast away from the \gls{hbn} monolayer along the $z$-direction, and how they oscillate along the $x$-direction, the direction in which thepolariton propagates.
The region $z>0$ corresponds to vacuum, while the region $z<0$ corresponds to the  substrate, where the field confinement is higher than in air (stronger colors and bigger arrows).
This suggests that it is possible to engineer the field confinement by appropriately choosing the materials for the environment of the \bd~material.

At the time of the publication of the work that this chapter is based on, Ref.~\cite{Ninhos_Tserkezis_Mortensen_Peres_2024}, the setup proposed initially in Figure~\ref{fig:hBN_monolayer} was a mere suggestion of how one could realise a periodic electrostatic modulation, albeit with a step potential, even though in the derivation of the low-energy Hamiltonian in Section~\ref{sec:electronic_band_structure_modulated_hBN} we considered a cosine-like potential to arrive at an analytical expression for the Hamiltonian.
In the advent of current technologies and developments on the isolation of \bd~monolayers, there is actually a recent experiment~\cite{Danielsen_Lassaline_2025} where the authors could realise a similar experiment to the one here proposed also with a \gls{hbn} layer, and with a periodic potential consisting of a finite number of Fourier components.
In this way, with the likelihood of the evolution of experimental techniques, we presume that in a not so distant future one could realise a single-harmonic periodic modulation on a \gls{hbn} flake to put our theory up to test.
This could deem our study relevant for possible applications in nanophotonics or optoelectronics in different ranges of the \gls{em} spectrum, since the parameter $\beta$ is quite tunable, due to its dependence on the amplitude $V_0$ and the period $L$ of the potential.
