\chapter{Optical Properties of Nanostructured hBN}
\label{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice}
\glsresetall
In previous chapters, we studied the phenomenology and theoretical aspects of the exciton formation and their contribution to the optical response.
We have seen also at the end of Chapter~\ref{ch:classical_electrodynamics}, how light can couple to material polar excitations to form the polariton.
In this chapter we analyze the exciton--polariton resultant from the hybridization of the exciton with the \gls{em} field.

Herein, we revisit \gls{hbn}, whose tight-binding model has been presented in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}, a prototypical \bd~material to study excitons.
We wish to engineer the optical properties of \gls{hbn}, by applying a \gls{1d} electrostatic potential, resulting in an emergent \gls{1d} superlattice.
Choosing an appropriate form for the potential, it is possible to obtain an analytical expression for the renormalized Hamiltonian for any potential strength, without resorting to perturbative methods.
Since we have an analytical expression for the low-energy Hamiltonian, we can extract the effective masses analytically, which speeds up the process of obtaining the binding energies of the excitons.
For that, we solve the Wannier equation using the variational method.
However, due to the potential being \gls{1d}, we introduce a degree of anisotropy on the dispersion which depends on the periodicity and amplitude of the potential.
The fact that the method of the \bse~introduced before for effective models is only useful for isotropic systems justifies the choice for the methods adopted.
The \bse~method through exact diagonalization of the Bethe--Salpeter Hamiltonian is unfortunately not applicable here---the introduction of the electrostatic potential may spoil the translational invariance, and the code developed in this work is relevant only for periodic \bd~materials.
Furthermore, the theory behind the code is based on the typical system of solid state physics, an infinite crystal with discrete translational symmetry.
The approach adopted in this chapter allows for considering any periodicity for the potential, either commensurate or incommensurate with the underlying lattice, meaning that the resultant material can be periodic or not.
Eventual problems posed by an incommensurate potential are not critical, as we work with the low-energy limit of the tight-binding Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn}.
The variational method offers also the advantage of obtaining an analytical expression for the optical conductivity, apart from a final angular integral that can only be performed analytically in the anisotropic case.
With the optical conductivity, we can finally compute the dispersion relation of the exciton--polariton.
The results that we provide in this chapter have already been reported in the literature in Ref.~\cite{Ninhos_Tserkezis_Mortensen_Peres_2024}.
Herein, we adapt the discussion to the structure of this document, giving at certain times insights that were not given in the original publication.
Essentially, there have been updates in the literature in the meantime, and other references have been found that we did not cite initially in the paper.
Moreover, a dissertation provides more freedom of writing, which permits giving detailed derivations and discussions.

We divide this chapter into five sections.
The first four consist of the theory that we apply in order to get our results, to which we reserve the last section.
In the first Section~\ref{sec:nanostructured_hBN}, we introduce the system under study while properly contextualizing and motivating this work.
In particular, the peculiar properties of \gls{hbn} on different application fronts shall be stressed once again, as we also did in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method} when we introduced the tight-binding model for \gls{hbn}.
After motivating this work, its technical aspects follow in the next sections.
In the Section~\ref{sec:electronic_band_structure_modulated_hBN}, we derive the expression for the renormalized low-energy Hamiltonian and discuss the new emergent electronic structure.
Next, in the Section~\ref{sec:anisotropic_exciton_states} we focus on solving the excitonic problem, that is, finding the excitonic binding energies and wavefunctions for the ``new'' engineered \gls{hbn}.
Afterward, in the Section~\ref{sec:exciton_optical_response} we focus on the optical conductivity that we later use to compute the dispersion relation for the exciton--polariton.
Finally, in the Section~\ref{sec:results_discussion_hBN_superlattice} we present and discuss our results, previously reported in Ref.~\cite{Ninhos_Tserkezis_Mortensen_Peres_2024}.
Throughout the chapter, we stress the difficulties introduced by potential and consequent dispersion anisotropy along our reasoning.
Some techniques introduced in the previous chapters will be put in practice.

\section{Nanostructured hBN: a One-dimensional Superlattice}
\label{sec:nanostructured_hBN}

The material we study in this chapter, \acrlong{hbn} (\gls{hbn}), is a \gls{2d} material, one among the big family of \bd~materials that have been proposed or discovered since the isolation of a single graphene layer in 2004~\cite{Novoselov_Geim_2004}.
Many materials exhibiting physical phenomena once classified as exotic by the scientific community, like superconductors~\cite{Dong_2021}, strange metals~\cite{Li_2024}, topological insulators~\cite{Zhang_Liu_Qi_Dai_Fang_Zhang_2009} and ferroelectric materials~\cite{Wang_Meng_2023} are now quite familiar to the physicists working in the field of \bd~materials.
Using natural \bd~materials, which already have alluring properties per se, both experimental and theoretical physicists envision stacking monolayers of the same or different materials, just like pieces of Lego, as a way of producing new materials.
If these new structures, called \gls{vdw} materials, consist of monolayers of distinct compounds, then they are called \gls{vdw} heterostructures.
Otherwise, if they consist of monolayers of the same kind, they are called \gls{vdw} homostructures.
The several stacked monolayers present strong chemical bonds along their in-plane extension, and bond with the other layers through weaker \gls{vdw} forces.
A typical naturally occurring example is graphite, that some scientists like to call multilayer graphene, which is known to conduct electricity.

The material we study, \gls{hbn}, unlike graphene which is a semi-metal, presents a wide bandgap as explained before in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}.
These two materials have been combined before by using two \gls{hbn} layers to encapsulate one layer of graphene~\cite{Woessner_2015} to achieve highly confined plasmonic modes in graphene while minimizing losses.
It has also been seen experimentally that graphene displays ballistic transport when encapsulated with a thin film of \gls{hbn}~\cite{Banszerus_2016}.
The choice of \gls{hbn} as an encapsulating material is justified by its extremely flat surface and low defect-density~\cite{Abidi_2019}.
Its low density of defects is actually one reason why this material is also promising for applications in quantum optics, as the defects can act as single-photon emitters~\cite{Li_Pershin_2022}.
It is also promising for applications in nanophotonics, as we have already discussed in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}, due to its intriguing optical properties in the mid-\gls{ir}, where phonon--polaritons have also been observed~\cite{Rizzo_Zhang_2023}.

The observation of superconductivity in twisted bilayer graphene~\cite{Cao_2018} gave birth to a new field in condensed matter physics, called twistronics~\cite{Hennighausen_Kar_2021,Aggarwal_Narula_Ghosh_2023}, where bilayer systems with a relative twist angle display emergent phenomena that its isolated counterparts do not.
The exotic phenomena that we mentioned above that physicists have been so hard trying to seek, are now at reach by tuning the twist angle between stacked monolayers~\cite{Cao_2020,Zhang_2022,Du_Xiao_2024}.
By tuning the twist angle, one can produce different patterns on the atomic arrangement, called moir\'e patterns or superlattices, and in that way invent completely new materials with different electronic, optical, and magnetic properties, among others.
Nowadays, the field of twistronics is a hot playground for theoretical physicists, where they predict exotic effects stemming from strong correlations in moir\'e materials~\cite{Lopes_dos_2007,Xian_Rubio_2019,Song_Bernevig_2022}.
The effect of the moir\'e superlattice has on the electronic properties of a material can also be emulated by applying an external potential on a \bd~material of interest~\cite{Fu_Pixley_2020,Lee_Pixley_2022}.

In this work, we emulate a \gls{1d} superlattice by externally modulating a \gls{hbn} monolayer with an electrostatic potential induced by a patterned grating, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_monolayer}.
In the same figure, the grating is composed of long metallic rods with an alternated bias, that lye under a quartz substrate with permittivity $\varepsilon_2$ that acts as a substrate for the \gls{hbn} monolayer.
The period of the potential $L$ is also marked in the figure.
This display serves as a suggestion of a possible experimental setup and has essentially illustrative purposes.

Admittedly interesting in the \gls{uv} range of the radiation spectrum due to its strong excitonic optical response~\cite{Ferreira_Chaves_Peres_Ribeiro_2019}, we can deem \gls{hbn} promising for polaritonics in the deep \gls{uv} down to the mid \gls{ir} by electrostatic modulation.
Quite relevant to this work is the experiment in Ref.~\cite{Danielsen_Lassaline_2025} reported after our publication~\cite{Ninhos_Tserkezis_Mortensen_Peres_2024}, where they observed exciton--polaritons in a Fourier-tailored \gls{hbn} flake in a \gls{vdw} heterostructure, where a nonzero Rabi splitting indicates that the strong-coupling regime was achieved~\cite{Danielsen_Lassaline_2025}.
However, in the experimental work the Fourier surface has more than one Fourier component, whereas in our theoretical work we consider a single spatial harmonic defined by the period $L$.
Notwithstanding the use of a more simple potential, we believe that qualitative comparisons are fair.

Still, in Figure~\ref{fig:hBN_monolayer} one can see a schematic representation of a plane wave with frequency $\omega$ impinging on the \gls{hbn} flake that responds to this external perturbation.
We quantify that response when computing the optical conductivity explained in Section~\ref{sec:exciton_optical_response}.

\section{Electronic Band Structure of Modulated hBN}
\label{sec:electronic_band_structure_modulated_hBN}

The idea of subjecting a \bd~material to an external \gls{1d} periodic potential in order to change its electronic properties is not exactly new.
The authors in Refs.~\cite{Nature_Louie_2008,PRL_Louie_2008} investigated a graphene monolayer subjected to a \gls{1d} potential to find that the dispersion, initially isotropic, renormalizes along one of the directions while leaving the other intact; new Dirac quasiparticles with an anisotropic low-energy dispersion emerge.
In Ref.~\cite{Louie_2010}, an identical idea for a graphene \gls{1d} superlattice was put in practice, that time with a periodically modulated magnetic field, to see that the carrier velocity renormalizes isotropically, contrary to what they expected.
In another theoretical work~\cite{Stauber_2017}, the effect of introducing an external potential affects the plasmonic effects in graphene, in particular the plasmonic modes along the direction of the potential variation, while the ones perpendicular to it are left unchanged
We shall see that the introduction of a nonvanishing mass term in the Hamiltonian (and changing the hopping parameter accordingly) when going from graphene to \gls{hbn}, leads to an anisotropic effective mass tensor, where both components in the diagonal renormalize; the enhancement of the anisotropy in the electron dispersion is more pronounced than the massless case.
Only after determining how exactly this anisotropy affects the dispersion of \gls{hbn}, will we see how the excitonic properties are affected.
First and foremost, we need to determine the renormalized Hamiltonian, in the light of what was done for the gapless case of graphene in Ref.~\cite{PRL_Louie_2008}.

We start from the low-energy Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn} around the point $K'$ of the \bz~given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:low_energy_hamiltonian_hBN_K_prime}, and convert the momentum coordinates into spatial derivatives as

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{K'}  = \hbar \vF \bk\vdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+M\sigma_{z}=-\ii \hbar \vF \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\sigma_{x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\sigma_{y}\right)+M\sigma_{z}\,.
\end{equation}

Now, we include a periodic potential $V(x)=V\left(x+L\right)$, with $L \gg a$ and $a$ the atomic lattice spacing, in the Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:total_H}
    H = \mathcal{H}_{K'}  + V(x)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent with the two-by-two identity matrix implicit in the potential term.
It is awkward to have the initial Hamiltonian in reciprocal space, while the external potential is in real space.
The potential term can be removed by introducing the unitary transformation

\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(
    \begin{array}{cc}
       \ee^{-\ii \alpha(x)/2} & -\ee^{\ii \alpha(x)/2}\\
       \ee^{-\ii \alpha(x)/2} & \ee^{\ii \alpha(x)/2}
    \end{array}\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    \alpha(x)=\frac{2}{\hbar \vF}\int_{0}^{x} V(x') \,\dd x'.
\end{equation}

\noindent Applying this transformation to the Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:total_H}, we obtain

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:transformed_H}
    \overline{H}=\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}[\mathcal{H}_{K} +V(x)]\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}=\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{K} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}+V(x)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Now, we need to massage the term in $\mathcal{H}_{K}$, reading

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:transformed_H0}
    \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{K} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}=\hbar \vF  \left[\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}+\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right] + M \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The term in $\sigma_{z}$ is the simplest to simplify, and it evaluates to

\begin{equation}
    M  \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{z} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
                                                        0 & -M \ee^{\ii \alpha}\\
                                                        -M \ee^{-\ii \alpha} & 0
    \end{array}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent To work out the term involving the $y$-derivative, we evaluate the matrix element of that term between two arbitrary wavefunctions resolved in real space, $\varphi_i(\br)$ and $\varphi_j(\br)$, where $\br=(x,y)$.
Later we particularize those wavefunctions to wavefunctions electronic states.
We write

\begin{align}
    H_{y}[\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}]  =& \hbar \vF  \int \varphi_{i}^{\ast}(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}(\br)}{\partial y}\ \dr = \notag \\
    & \frac{\hbar \vF}{2}\int \varphi_{i}^{\ast}(\br)
        \left(\begin{array}{cc}
                0 & -\ee^{\ii \alpha(x)} \\
                \ee^{-\ii \alpha(x)}  & 0
              \end{array}\right)
              \frac{\partial\varphi_{j}(\br)}{\partial y} \dr - \notag \\
    & \frac{\hbar \vF}{2}\int \frac{\partial\varphi_{i}^{\ast}(\br)}{\partial y}
          \left(\begin{array}{cc}
            0 & -\ee^{\ii \alpha(x)}\\
            \ee^{-\ii \alpha(x)} & 0
        \end{array}\right)\varphi_{i}(\br) \dr ,
\end{align}

\noindent where some clarifications are needed.
First, the integrals are over the region $[0,L] \times [0,L_y]$ in the $xy$ plane, where $\times$ denotes Cartesian product.
In the previous equation, the subscript $y$ in $H_{y}[\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}]$ stands for the term with $\sigma_{y}$ (and also for $\frac{\partial}{\partial_y}$) in equation~\eqref{eq:transformed_H0}.
We also split the initial integral into two equal terms with a prefactor of $1/2$, and performed an integration by parts in $y$ on one of them.

The term in~\eqref{eq:transformed_H0} that goes with $\sigma_{x}$ is more subtle, since both the unitary transformation and the wavefunctions depend on $x$.
We compute the matrix element between two general functions $f(\br)$ and $g(\br)$.
We write


\begin{align}
    H_{x}[f,g]  =& \hbar \vF \int f(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[\mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \big] \dr = \notag \\
                =& \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int f(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \big[\mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br)\big] \dr - \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \big[f(\br)\mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\big] \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \dr = \notag \\
                =& \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \left[f(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \frac{\dd \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}}{\dd x} g(\br) +  f(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} \frac{\partial g(\br)}{\partial x} \right] \dr - \notag \\
                 & \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \left[f(\br) \frac{\dd \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}}{\dd x} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) - \frac{\partial f(\br)}{\partial x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \right] \dr \,,
\end{align}


\noindent where an integration by parts has been made in the first line, just like we did for $H_y$.
We associate the terms involving the derivative of the unitary operator and obtain

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \left[ f(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \frac{\dd \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}}{\dd x} g(\br) - f(\br) \frac{\dd \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}}{\dd x} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \right] \dr = -V(x) \mathds{1}.
\end{equation}

\noindent The previous term cancels with the last term in equation~\eqref{eq:transformed_H}, which leaves only the terms

\begin{equation}
    \overline{H} \left[f,g\right] = \sum_{\iota = x,y,z} H_{\iota} \left[f,g\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where we defined

\begin{equation}
    H_{x}\left[f,g\right]=\frac{\hbar \vF}{2} \int \left[ f(\br) \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} \frac{\partial g(\br)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial f(\br)}{\partial x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{x} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \right] \dr \, ,
\end{equation}

\noindent the term $H_y$ has already been defined above, and

\begin{equation}
    H_{z}\left[f,g\right]= M \int f(\br)  \mathbb{U}_{\alpha}^{\dagger} \sigma_{z} \mathbb{U}_{\alpha} g(\br) \dr\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Later on, we will particularize $f$ and $g$ to physical states.
Before that, we expand $\exp{\ii\alpha(x)}$ in Fourier series

\begin{equation}
    \ee^{\ii\alpha(x)}=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\,\ee^{\ii\ell G_{0}x}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $G_{0}=\frac{2\pi}{L}$ is defined by the periodicity of the potential, and the Fourier coefficients read

\begin{equation}
    f_{\ell}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\ii[\alpha(x)-\ell G_{0}x]} \dd x.
\end{equation}

\noindent We assume an even potential of the form

\begin{equation}
    V(x)=V_{0}\cos\left(G_{0}x\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent which implies

\begin{equation}
    \alpha(x)=\frac{2V_{0}}{\hbar \vF G_{0}}\sin\left(G_{0}x\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent Computing $f_{\ell}$ for this potential, we obtain

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    f_{\ell} & =\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\ee^{\ii[\alpha(x)-\ell G_{0}x]}\dd x=\\
             & =\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\ee^{\ii\left[\frac{2V_{0}L}{\hbar \vF 2\pi} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{L} x\right) - \ell \frac{2\pi}{L} x \right]}\dd x=\\
             & =\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ee^{\ii(\beta\sin s-\ell s)}\dd s=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\pi}^{-\pi}\ee^{\ii(\ell s-\beta\sin s)}(-\dd s)=\\
             & =\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ee^{\ii(\ell s-\beta\sin s)}\dd s=J_{\ell}(\beta)\,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we defined the dimensionless parameter

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:beta_definition}
    \beta=\frac{V_{0}L}{\hbar \vF \pi}\,.
\end{equation}

Since the potential is even, in the Fourier series only the terms with even $\ell$ appear.
As $J_{-\ell}(x)=(-1)^{\ell}J_{\ell}(x)$ and $\ell$ is even, we have $J_{-\ell}(x)=J_{\ell}(x)$.
We use the following basis states

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{1}^{m} (\br) =\frac{1}{\sqrt{L L_y}} \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
    \ee^{\ii(\bk + \frac{1}{2} \bG_m)\vdot\br} \qq{and}
    \phi_{2}^{m} (\br) =\frac{1}{\sqrt{L L_y}} \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
    \ee^{\ii(\bk - \frac{1}{2} \bG_m)\vdot\br},
\end{equation}

\noindent denoted previously by $f$ and $g$, where $L_y$ is the size of the superlattice along the $y$-direction, they have a plane-wave like spatial part of the form $\varphi_{i}^{m}(\br) = \exp{\ii ( \bk \pm \bG_m/2) \vdot \br}$ with $i=1,2\,$, a spinorial part, and $\bG_m$ are the vectors of the reciprocal superlattice given by $\bG_m = m \bG = m G_0 \uvec{x}$, with $| \bk | \ll |\bG|$.
We build the new Hamiltonian using these base states as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:new_H}
    \overline{H}_{ij} = \sum_{\iota=x,y,z} H_{\iota}[\phi_{i}^{m},\phi_{j}^{m}] = \int \big(\phi_{i}^{m}(\br)\big)^{\dagger} \overline{H} \phi_{j}^{m}(\br) \dr.
\end{equation}

\noindent For the calculation of the matrix elements for the Hamiltonian, we need to evaluate the following quantity

\begin{equation}
    \langle \varphi^{m}_i \vert \ee^{\ii\alpha(x)} \vert \varphi^{m}_j \rangle = \int \big(\varphi^{m}_{i} (\br) \big)^{*} \ee^{\ii\alpha(x)} \varphi_{j}^{m}(\br) \dr = \sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell} \int  \big(\varphi^{m}_{i} (\br) \big)^{*} \ee^{\ii\ell G_{0}x} \varphi_{j}^{m}(\br) \dr,
\end{equation}

\noindent Using the spatial parts $\varphi_{i}(\br)$ of the states $\phi_{i}(\br)$, we have $(i,j=1,2)$

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \langle \varphi_i^{m} \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)} \vert \varphi_j^{m} \rangle= & \sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{LL_{y}}\int \ee^{-\ii(\bk\pm \frac{1}{2} \bG_{m})\vdot\br}\ee^{\pm i\ell Gx}\ee^{\ii(\bk\pm \frac{1}{2} \bG_{m})\vdot\br} \dr = \\
    = &
        \begin{cases}
            \langle \varphi^{m}_1 \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)}\vert \varphi^{m}_1 \rangle=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\pm i\ell G_{0}x} \dd x =f_{0}\\
            \langle \varphi^{m}_1 \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)}\vert \varphi^{m}_2 \rangle=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\ii(\pm\ell-m)G_{0}x} \dd x =f_{\pm m}\\
            \langle \varphi^{m}_2 \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)}\vert \varphi^{m}_1 \rangle=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\ii(\pm\ell+m)G_{0}x} \dd x =f_{\mp m}\\
            \langle \varphi^{m}_2 \vert \ee^{\pm\ii \alpha (x)}\vert \varphi^{m}_2 \rangle=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} \ee^{\pm i\ell G_{0}x} \dd x =f_{0}
        \end{cases}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent We also note that the spatial derivatives are simply given by

\begin{equation}
        \begin{cases}
        \frac{\partial\phi_{j}^{m}}{\partial x} & = \ii \left[k_{x}+(-1)^{j+1}m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right]\phi_{j}^{m} \\
        \frac{\partial\phi_{j}^{m}}{\partial y} & = \ii k_{y}\phi_{j}^{m},
        \end{cases},
    \qq{with} j=1,2.
\end{equation}

\noindent For the new Hamiltonian, we need all the following matrix elements (omitting the superscript $m$ in the states for clarity)

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    H_{x}\left[\phi_{1},\phi_{1}\right] & =\hbar \vF \left(k_{x}+m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right)\\
    H_{y}\left[\phi_{1},\phi_{1}\right] & =0\\
    H_{z}\left[\phi_{1},\phi_{1}\right] & =0\\
    H_{x}\left[\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\right] & =H_{x}\left[\phi_{2},\phi_{1}\right]=0\\
    H_{y}\left[\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\right] & =\ii \hbar \vF  k_{y}J_{m}(\beta)=H_{y}^{*}\left[\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\right]\\
    H_{z}\left[\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\right] & =H_{z}\left[\phi_{2},\phi_{1}\right]=-MJ_{m}(\beta)\\
    H_{x}\left[\phi_{2},\phi_{2}\right] & =\hbar \vF \left(-k_{x}+m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right)\\
    H_{y}\left[\phi_{2},\phi_{2}\right] & =0\\
    H_{z}\left[\phi_{2},\phi_{2}\right] & =0
\end{aligned} .
\end{equation}

\noindent Inserting the previous expressions in equation~\eqref{eq:new_H}, we obtain the Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \overline{H} = \left(\begin{array}{cc}
                         \overline{H}_{11} & \overline{H}_{12}\\
                         \overline{H}_{21} & \overline{H}_{22}
                         \end{array}\right)=\left(
                         \begin{array}{cc}
                         \hbar \vF \left(k_{x}+m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right) & \ii \hbar \vF k_{y}J_{m}(\beta)\\
                         -\ii \hbar \vF k_{y}J_{m}(\beta) & \hbar \vF \left(-k_{x}+m\frac{G_{0}}{2}\right)
                         \end{array}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent It is convenient to symmetrize and antisymmetrize the basis states as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \phi_{s}(\br) & =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{1}(\br)+\phi_{2}(\br)],\\
    \phi_{a}(\br) & =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_{1}(\br)-\phi_{2}(\br)].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent In this new basis, the Hamiltonian reads

\begin{equation}
    \overline{H}=\frac{1}{2}
    \left(\begin{array}{cc}
          \overline{H}_{11}+\overline{H}_{22}+\overline{H}_{12}+\overline{H}_{21} & \overline{H}_{11}-\overline{H}_{22}-\overline{H}_{12}+\overline{H}_{21}\\
          \overline{H}_{11}-\overline{H}_{22}+\overline{H}_{12}-\overline{H}_{21} & \overline{H}_{11}+\overline{H}_{22}-\overline{H}_{12}-\overline{H}_{21}
          \end{array}\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent which after computing all the elements gives

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:new_Dirac_Hamiltonian}
    \begin{aligned}
        \overline{H}_m & =
        \left(\begin{array}{cc}
               \frac{1}{2}G_{0}m\hbar \vF -|J_{m}(\beta)|M & \hbar \vF (k_{x}- \ii J_{m}(\beta)k_{y}) =m\\
               \hbar \vF (k_{x}+ \ii J_{m}(\beta)k_{y}) & \frac{1}{2}G_{0}m\hbar \vF +|J_{m}(\beta)|M
              \end{array}\right) \\
        & = \mathds{1}\frac{m}{2}G_{0} \hbar \vF + \hbar \vF \big[\sigma_{x} k_{x} + \sigma_{y} J_{m}(\beta) k_{y}\big] - \sigma_{z} M J_{m}(\beta),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent which is a massive Dirac-like Hamiltonian with renormalized parameters, and where we recovered the $m$ label as subscript in the Hamiltonian.
We see that there is a new term with the identity matrix that did not exist initially.
If we focus on the Hamiltonian matching with the original, which corresponds to the case $m=0$, the $y$-component of the Fermi velocity changes as $\vF \rightarrow J_{0}(\beta)\vF $.
Since $\vert J_{0}(\beta)\vert < 1$, an electron traveling along the $y$-direction is slowed down compared to its speed if traveling along the $x$-direction.
This leads to an anisotropic low-energy dispersion.
Also, the energy gap $E_g = 2M$, that we can identify as the \gls{dft} bandgap at the \gls{lda} level, renormalizes to $E_g \rightarrow 2M \vert J_{0}(\beta) \vert \equiv \Delta_g (\beta)$.
We will keep the notation $\Delta_g(\beta)$ for the renormalized \gls{dft} gap, that, as we will see later on, is not the total bandgap.
Therefore, it is possible to reduce the gap of \gls{hbn} choosing an appropriate value for either $V_{0}$ or $L$ (actually, the product $V_{0}L$).
With this mechanism, we can control both the gap and the excitonic states, which now become that of an anisotropic material (similarly to phosphorene).

At first glance, it is hard to realize how the previous Hamiltonian falls to the original one in the case where there is no potential.
It is actually possible to conciliate the renormalized Hamiltonian with the original one, which is done in Appendix~\ref{app:hBN_superlattice}.
In the same appendix, we also recover the original Hamiltonian when the period $L$ tends to infinity.

The group velocity components and the two effective masses follow directly from the spectrum of the previous Hamiltonian.
These are given by

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    v_{x} & =\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial E_c}{\partial k_{x}}=\frac{\hbar \vF^{2}k_{x}}{E_c(\bk,\beta)} \qq{and}\\
    v_{y} & =\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial E_c}{\partial k_{y}}=\frac{\hbar \vF^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}}{E_c(\bk,\beta)} \,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
E_c(\bk,\beta)=\sqrt{\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\left[k_{x}^{2}+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}^{2}\right] + J_{0}^{2}(\beta)M^{2}}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the renormalized low-energy dispersion of the conduction band.
The diagonal components of the effective mass tensor computed at momentum $\bk$ are given by

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        m_{x}(k_{x},k_{y}) &= \frac{\Big[J_{0}^{2}(\beta)M^{2}+\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\left(k_{x}^{2}+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}^{2}\right)\Big]^{3/2}}{J_{0}^{2}(\beta) \vF^{2} \left(M^{2}+\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}k_{y}^{2}\right)} \qq{and} \\
        m_{y}(k_{x},k_{y}) &= \frac{\Big[J_{0}^{2}(\beta)M^{2}+\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\left(k_{x}^{2}+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}^{2}\right)\Big]^{3/2}}{J_{0}^{2}(\beta) \vF^{2} \left(J_{0}^{2}(\beta) M^{2}+\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}k_{x}^{2}\right)}\,,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent that at zero momentum evaluate to

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        m_{x}&=\frac{|J_{0}(\beta)|M}{\vF^{2}} \qq{and} \\
        m_{y}&=\frac{M}{|J_{0}(\beta)|\vF^{2}}\,.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

The spinors of the new low-energy Hamiltonian are also needed to compute the optical matrix element for the conductivity, as discussed in Sections~\ref{subsec:non_interacting_conductivity} and~\ref{subsec:excitonic_conductivity}.
We change the notation slightly, replacing $M$ with $E_{g}/2$.
Besides that, we define $k_{x} + \ii J_{0}(\beta) k_{y} = k(\beta)\ee^{\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)}$, with $k(\beta)=\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)k_{y}^{2}}$, and define

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:theta_xi_anisotropic_definitions}
    \theta_{k}(\beta)=\arctan\left(\frac{J_{0}(\beta)k_{y}}{k_{x}}\right) \qq{and} \xi_{k}=\arctan\left(\frac{2\hbar \vF k(\beta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which are the anisotropic analogues of their respective isotropic counterparts in Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta_xi_isotropic_definitions} (apart from changing the notation for momentum from $k$ and $\bk$ to $q$ and $\bq$, respectively).
In this notation, and after some straightforward algebra, the spinor for the conduction band reads

\begin{equation}
     \bC^{c\bk} = \left(
    \begin{array}{c}
        \ee^{-\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)}\frac{\sin\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}}{\cos\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}} \\ 1
    \end{array}\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent that, when multiplied by $\cos\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}$, yields the spinor in the final form that we will use

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conduction_spinor}
     \bC^{c\bk}=\left(
    \begin{array}{c}
        \ee^{-\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)}\sin\frac{\xi_{k}}{2} \\ 
        \cos\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}
    \end{array}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent Analogously, the spinor for the valence band can be written as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:valence_spinor}
    \bC^{v\bk}=\left(
    \begin{array}{c}
        \cos\frac{\xi_{k}}{2} \\ 
        -\ee^{\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)}\sin\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}
    \end{array}\right).
\end{equation}

\noindent These last two equations provide the spinors of the renormalized low-energy Hamiltonian, similar to their isotropic versions found before in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mono_hBN_valence_conduction_spinors}.
They do not exactly coincide when $\beta = 0$, because the mass term in the renormalized low-energy Hamiltonian carries a negative sign, as it can be seen in Eq.~\eqref{eq:new_Dirac_Hamiltonian} when $m=0$.

\section{Anisotropic Excitonic States}
\label{sec:anisotropic_exciton_states}

In this section, we provide the theory necessary for the variational calculation of the excitonic states and energies, within a Wannier--Mott description for the excitons.
The variational method was first mentioned in Section~\ref{subsec:born_oppenheimer_approximation}, and it is here applied to solve the Wannier equation.
We wish to compare with the excitons of pristine monolayer \gls{hbn}.
Therefore, we focus on the particular case $m=0$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:new_Dirac_Hamiltonian}, for which the effective low-energy Hamiltonian reads

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:effective_low_energy_H}
    \overline{H}_0 = \hbar \vF [k_{x} \sigma_{x} +J_{0}(\beta)k_{y} \sigma_{y}] - \frac{E_g}{2} J_{0}(\beta) \sigma_{z}
\end{empheq}

The variational wavefunctions need to capture the anisotropy of the dispersion, and the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian for those wavefunctions are not easy to work out.
We separate the kinetic part from the potential part and treat each state separately in different subsections.
The kinetic energy contribution to the Wannier Hamiltonian is given by

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{K} = \frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2\mu_{x}}+\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2\mu_{y}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the effective masses $\mu_{x}$ and $\mu_{y}$ are now in general different.
We do the calculations for the Rytova--Keldysh potential, that we have already encountered in Section~\ref{subsubsec:dielectric_function_static_limit}, that we recall

\begin{equation}
    V_{\RK}(r) = \frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}} \left[\mathbf{H}_{0}\left(\frac{\kappa r}{r_{0}}\right)-Y_{0}\left(\frac{\kappa r}{r_{0}}\right)\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have a small modification in the argument of the special functions compared to Eq.~\eqref{eq:Rytova--Keldysh_potential_real_space}.
We introduced the parameter $\kappa = (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)/2$, which is the average between the permittivity $\varepsilon_1$ of the material on top and the permittivity $\varepsilon_2$ of the material supporting the \gls{hbn} layer.
These two permittivities are the static ones and account for electrostatic screening in the materials surrounding the \gls{hbn} monolayer~\cite{Ventura_2019}.
Joining the two contributions, we have for the Wannier Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2\mu_{x}}+\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2\mu_{y}}+V_{\RK}(r)\,.
\end{equation}

As trial wavefunctions for the ground state $1s$ and the first 3 excited states $2x$, $2y$ and $2s$, we pick the expressions

\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
   \Psi_{1s}(x,y)&=C_{1s} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s} x^{2} + b_{1s} y^{2}}} \,,\\
   \Psi_{2x}(x,y)&=C_{2x} x\, \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{2x} x^{2} + b_{2x} y^{2}}} \,,\\
   \Psi_{2y}(x,y)&=C_{2y} y\, \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{2y} x^{2} + b_{2y} y^{2}}} \,,\\
   \Psi_{2s}(x,y)&=C_{2s} \left(1-d \sqrt{a_{2s} x^{2} + b_{2s} y^{2}}\right) \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{2s} x^{2} + b_{2s} y^{2}}}\,.\\
\end{split}
\end{equation*}

\noindent The parameter $d$ in the $2s$ wavefunction is determined by the orthogonality condition $\braket{ \Psi_{1s}}{\Psi_{2s}}=0$, and the constants $C_{\alpha}$ are normalization factors.
These wavefunctions are an approximation of the true ones, but we assume that they describe the problem well enough.
They coincide with the wavefunctions used by Jos\'e Nuno S.~Gomes and co-authors in Ref.~\cite{Gomes_Trallero-Giner_Vasilevskiy_2021} for phosphorene.
Therefore, the expressions that we use to calculate the binding energies are the same ones as in Ref.~\cite{Gomes_Trallero-Giner_Vasilevskiy_2021}, we just complement the information therein provided.
Each wavefunction has two variational parameters that we denote by $a_\alpha$ and $b_\alpha$, with $\alpha = 1s, 2x, 2y, 2s$.
In this way the anisotropy of the dispersion is accounted for.
In the isotropic case where $a_\alpha = b_\alpha$, then we recover the wavefunctions that are the analytical solutions of the \bd~hydrogen atom~\cite{2D_Hydrogen_atom_1991}.
For each of these states, we compute the expectation value of the Hamiltonian and minimize it with respect to both parameters.
By solving the minimization condition, we find the values for $a_\alpha$ and $b_\alpha$.
Then, we go back to the expression for the wavefunction and for the expectation value to obtain the (approximate) eigenstate and energy, respectively.
We repeat the procedure separately for each of the four states.
Naturally, we start with the easiest case, the ground state.

\subsection{Ground State $1s$}
\label{subsec:ground_state_1s}

As a trial wavefunction for the ground state we use

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{1s}(x,y)=C_{1s} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s}x^{2}+b_{1s}y^{2}}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
C_{1s} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(a_{1s}b_{1s}\right)^{1/4}.
\end{equation}

\noindent To compute the expression for the ground-state energy $E_{1s}$ in terms of the variational parameters, we need to evaluate

\begin{equation}
    E_{1s}\left(a_{1s},b_{1s}\right)=\langle\Psi_{1s}|H|\Psi_{1s}\rangle=\langle\Psi_{1s}|\mathcal{K}|\Psi_{1s}\rangle+\langle\Psi_{1s}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{1s}\rangle\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent We compute first the term involving the kinetic energy

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:integral_in_x_y}
    \langle\Psi_{1s}|\mathcal{K}|\Psi_{1s}\rangle = \int \Psi_{1s}^{*}(x,y)\left[\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2\mu_{x}}+\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2\mu_{y}}\right]\Psi_{1s}(x,y) \dr \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent As a first step, we perform a change of variables proposed by Rodin et al.~in~\cite{Rodin_Carvalho_Castro_Neto_2014}

\begin{equation}
    X= s_{x} x\,,\,Y = s_{y}y\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where

\begin{equation}
    s_{x/y}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{x/y}}{2\bar{\mu}}}\,, \qq{with} \bar{\mu} = \frac{\mu_{x}\mu_{y}}{\mu_{x}+\mu_{y}}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent In this way, the kinetic energy that now reads

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{K} = -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\nabla^{2}
\end{equation}

\noindent has the usual form of isotropic systems but with a different mass, and the Laplacian is with respect to the new variables $X,Y$.
The infinitesimal element of area in the old coordinates transforms into

\begin{equation}
\dd x \dd y=\frac{\dd X \dd Y}{s_{x}s_{y}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent so the integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:integral_in_x_y} reads now

\begin{equation}
-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\frac{1}{s_{x}s_{y}}\int \Psi_{1s}^{*}(X,Y)\nabla^{2}\Psi_{1s}(X,Y)\, \dd X \dd Y\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The wavefunction as a function of the new variables is

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{1s}(X,Y)=C_{1s}\ee^{-\sqrt{\delta_{x}X^{2}+\delta_{y}Y^{2}}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we introduced new parameters $\delta_{x}=a_{1s}s_{x}^{-2}$ and $\delta_{y}=b_{1s}s_{y}^{-2}$.
Next, we compute the Laplacian of the wavefunction in the $(X,Y)$ coordinates and then perform another change of variables to elliptical coordinates at the end given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic}
    X=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\delta_{x}}}\cos\theta\,, \,Y=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\delta_{y}}}\sin\theta\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent For the integral, we need the absolute value of the Jacobian, which is $\rho/\sqrt{\delta_{x}\delta_{y}}$\,.
Then, the integral reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        & -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\frac{1}{s_{x}s_{y}}\int \Psi_{1s}^{*}(X,Y)\nabla^{2}\Psi_{1s}(X,Y)\, \dd X \dd Y = \\
       =& -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\frac{C^{2}}{s_x s_y\sqrt{\delta_{x}\delta_{y}}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \rho\, \ee^{-2\rho}\left[\delta_{x}\left(\cos^{2}\theta-\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\rho}\right)+\delta_{y}\left(\sin^{2}\theta-\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\rho}\right)\right] \dd \rho \dd\theta =\\
       =& -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\bar{\mu}}\frac{C^{2}}{s_x s_y\sqrt{\delta_{x}\delta_{y}}}\frac{-1}{4}\pi\left(\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}\right)=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{a_{1s}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b_{1s}}{\mu_{y}}\right)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent after making all substitutions.
Now, we have left the term of the matrix element derived from the potential to calculate.
For that term, we change from Cartesian $(x,y)$ coordinates to polar coordinates, and defining the function $\alpha(\theta)=2 \sqrt{a_{1s}+(b_{1s}-a_{1s})\sin^{2}\theta}$, to obtain

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:potential_matrix_element}
    \langle\Psi_{1s}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{1s}\rangle=\sqrt{a_{1s}b_{1s}}\frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathds{K} \left(\frac{\kappa r}{r_{0}}\right) r\, \ee^{-\alpha(\theta) r} \dd r \dd \theta\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we also defined $\mathds{K}(z) \equiv \mathbf{H}_{0}\left(z\right)-Y_{0}\left(z\right)$.
Fortunately, the radial integral has an analytical expression.
We calculate analytically the auxiliary integral


\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:auxiliar_radial_integral}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_{0}(\alpha,\kappa_0) & =\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{K}(\kappa_0 r)\, \ee^{-\alpha r} \dd r = \\
        & =\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\kappa_0^{2}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\kappa_0+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\kappa_0^{2}}}{\alpha}\right)+\log\left(\frac{\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\kappa_0^{2}}}{\kappa_0}\right)\right]\,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent that depends on $\theta$ through the function $\alpha(\theta)$, and where we introduced as well $\kappa_0 = \kappa/r_{0}$\,.
The radial integral of interest in Eq.~\eqref{eq:potential_matrix_element}, that we will denote by $I$, relates to the previous one through

\begin{equation}
    I(\alpha,\kappa_0) \equiv \int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{K}(\kappa_0 r)r \ee^{-\alpha r} \dd r=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}I_{0}(\alpha,\kappa_0)\,.
\end{equation}


\noindent The final expression for the energy of the excitonic ground state can only be written in terms of this last integral, and it reads

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    E_{1s}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{a_{1s}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b_{1s}}{\mu_{y}}\right)-\sqrt{a_{1s}b_{1s}}\frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}I(\alpha,\kappa_0) \,\dd \theta
\end{empheq}

Requiring an absolute minimum for this energy consists in solving $\partial E_{1s}/\partial a_{1s}=\partial E_{1s}/\partial b_{1s}=0$ for $a_{1s}$ and $b_{1s}$. 
As an example, for black phosphorus encapsulated in \gls{hbn}~\cite{Gomes_Trallero-Giner_Vasilevskiy_2021}, we have the parameters $\mu_{x}=0.99m_{e}$ and $\mu_{y}=0.12m_{e}$ where $m_e$ is the electron mass, $\kappa=4.5$ and $r_{0}=2.5$~\unit{\nano\meter}.
For this set of parameters, the binding energy evaluates to $E_{1s}=-257$ \unit{\milli\electronvolt}, and the variational parameters evaluate to $a_{1s}=2.45\text{ \unit{\nano\meter}}^{-2}$ and
$b_{1s}=0.533\text{ \unit{\nano\meter}}^{-2}$.
Computing the derivatives is straightforward, so we present here only the final expressions

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \frac{\partial E_{1s}}{\partial a_{1s}}  &= \frac{\hbar^2}{4 \mu_x} - \frac{e}{8 \pi \epsz r_0} \sqrt{\frac{b_{1s}}{a_{1s}}} \int_0^{2 \pi} I(\alpha,\kappa_0) + 4 \frac{a_{1s} \cos^2 \theta}{\alpha} \partial_\alpha I \,\dd \theta\,, \\
        \frac{\partial E_{1s}}{\partial b_{1s}}  &= \frac{\hbar^2}{4 \mu_y} - \frac{e}{8 \pi \epsz r_0} \sqrt{\frac{a_{1s}}{b_{1s}}} \int_0^{2 \pi} I(\alpha,\kappa_0) + 4 \frac{b_{1s} \sin^2 \theta}{\alpha} \partial_\alpha I \,\dd \theta\,.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent The excited states follow, starting with the states $2x$ and $2y$.

\subsection{Excited States $2x$ and $2y$}
\label{subsec:excited_states_2x_2y}

We discuss first the calculations for the $2x$ state.
We recall the expression for the trial wavefunction

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:2x_trial_wavefunction}
    \Psi_{2x}\left(x,y\right)=C_{2x}x \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{2x}x^{2}+b_{2x}y^{2}}}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the normalisation constant can be found using the same change of variables as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic} for the integral $\int |\Psi_{2x}\left(\br\right)|^2 \dr $, and it is given by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:2x_normalisation}
    |C_{2x}|^2 = \frac{8 a_{2x} \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \,.
\end{equation}

To obtain the kinetic contribution to the energy, we followed the same strategy as in the $1s$ case.
We change from $(x,y)$ to $(X,Y)$ coordinates and compute the Laplacian in these new coordinates, followed by a change of variables similar to~\eqref{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic}, performing the radial integral and finally the angular integral.
After all substitutions, we obtain

\begin{equation}
    \langle\Psi_{2x}|\mathcal{K}|\Psi_{2x}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}\left(\frac{3 a_{2x}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b_{2x}}{\mu_{y}}\right) .
\end{equation}

\noindent The potential term reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \langle\Psi_{2x}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{2x}\rangle &= \frac{8 a_{2x} \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0)\, \dd\theta = \\
                                            &= \frac{8 a_{2x} \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \frac{-e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^3}{\partial \alpha^3} I_0 (\alpha, \kappa_0)\, \dd\theta \,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we made use of the auxiliary function $I_0(\alpha,\kappa_0)$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:auxiliar_radial_integral}, having thus for the binding energy

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    E_{2x} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}\left(\frac{3 a_{2x}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b_{2x}}{\mu_{y}}\right) - \frac{8 a_{2x} \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0) \, \dd\theta
\end{empheq}

The derivatives with respect to $a_{2x}$ and $b_{2x}$, respectively, read

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        &\frac{\partial E_{2x}}{\partial a_{2x}} = \frac{3 \hbar^{2}}{8\mu_{x}} - \\
        &\frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}} \left[ \frac{4 \sqrt{a_{2x} b_{2x}}}{\pi}  \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0) }{\partial \alpha^2}  \dd\theta + \frac{8 \sqrt{a_{2x}^3 b_{2x}}}{3 \pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial }{\partial a_{2x}} \left(\frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \right) \dd\theta \right], \notag \\
        &\frac{\partial E_{2x}}{\partial b_{2x}} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8\mu_{y}} -\\
        &\frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}} \left[ \frac{4 \sqrt{a_{2x}^3/ b_{2x}} }{3 \pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0) }{\partial \alpha^2}  \dd\theta +  \frac{8 \sqrt{a_{2x}^3 b_{2x}}}{3 \pi}  \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial }{\partial b_{2x}} \left(\frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0) }{\partial \alpha^2} \right) \dd\theta \right]. \notag
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\noindent For simplicity, we omitted the explicit forms of the derivatives $\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{2x}} \left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0) \right)$ and $\frac{\partial }{\partial b_{2x}} \left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0) \right)$.

Since the calculations for the state $2y$ are analogous, we show only the key results.
For the binding energy, we have

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    E_{2y}= \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8}\left(\frac{a_{2y}}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{3 b_{2y}}{\mu_{y}}\right) - \frac{8 b_{2y} \sqrt{a_{2y} b_{2y}}}{3 \pi} \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_{0}} \frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \alpha^2} I (\alpha, \kappa_0) \,\dd\theta
\end{empheq}

\noindent and for the derivatives of the binding energy we have

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        &\frac{\partial E_{2y}}{\partial a_{2y}} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{8\mu_{x}} - \\
        &\frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}}  \left[ \frac{4 \sqrt{b_{2y}^3/a_{2y}}}{3 \pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \dd\theta + \frac{8 \sqrt{a_{2y} b_{2y}^3}}{3 \pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin^2 \theta \frac{\partial }{\partial b_{2y}} \left(\frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \right)\dd\theta \right] ,  \\
        &\frac{\partial E_{2y}}{\partial b_{2y}} = \frac{3 \hbar^{2}}{8\mu_{y}} - \\
        &\frac{e}{8 \epsz r_{0}}\left[ \frac{4 \sqrt{a_{2y} b_{2y}}}{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin^2 \theta \frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \dd\theta + \frac{8 \sqrt{a_{2y} b_{2y}^3}}{3 \pi}  \int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin^2 \theta \frac{\partial }{\partial a_{2y}} \left(\frac{\partial^2 I (\alpha, \kappa_0)}{\partial \alpha^2}  \right)\dd\theta \right] .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

The excited state $2s$ is the only one left.

\subsection{Excited State $2s$}
\label{subsec:excited_state_2s}

We reserve this entire section to the calculations for the state $2s$, as there are more key steps.
First, the normalisation condition leads to

\begin{equation}
    |C_{2s}|^2 = \frac{4 \sqrt{a_{2s} b_{2s}}}{\pi (2 - 4d + 3 d^2)}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the value of $d$ is obtained by the orthogonality condition $\langle \Psi_{1s} | \Psi_{2s} \rangle = 0$.
To ease the notation, from now on we will tacitly write $a$ and $b$ instead of $a_{2s}$ and $b_{2s}$.
Since the system is anisotropic, $d$ is not a simple function of the parameters $a_{1s}$ and $b_{1s}$.
To see this, let us write

\begin{align}
    \langle \Psi_{1s} | \Psi_{2s} \rangle &= C_{1s} C_{2s} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(1-d\sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}\right) \ee^{-\sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s}x^{2}+b_{1s}y^{2}}} \dd x \dd y = 0 \rightarrow \notag \\
    &\rightarrow d = \frac{\mathcal{I}_1}{\mathcal{I}_2} \,,
\end{align}

\noindent where we defined

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{I}_1 &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}  \ee^{-\sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s}x^{2}+b_{1s}y^{2}}} \dd x\dd y \qq{and} \\
        \mathcal{I}_2 &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}} \ee^{-\sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}} \ee^{-\sqrt{a_{1s}x^{2}+b_{1s}y^{2}}} \dd x \dd y \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent For both integrals, we perform a change of variables like in~\eqref{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic}.
The first integral reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{I}_1 &= \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho\, \ee^{-\rho} \ee^{-\rho \sqrt{\frac{a_{1s}}{a}\cos^2 \theta + \frac{b_{1s}}{b}\sin^2 \theta}} \frac{\dd \rho \dd\theta}{\sqrt{a b}} = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho \, \ee^{- t(\theta) \rho} \frac{\dd \rho \dd \theta}{\sqrt{a b}} =\\
        &=  \int_{0}^{2 \pi} t^{-2}(\theta) \frac{\dd\theta}{\sqrt{a b}} \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent if we also define $t(\theta) \equiv 1 + \sqrt{\frac{a_{1s}}{a}\cos^2 \theta + \frac{b_{1s}}{b}\sin^2 \theta}$\,.
For the second integral, we have

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{I}_2 &= \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho^2 \ee^{-\rho} \ee^{-\sqrt{\frac{a_{1s}}{a}\cos^2 \theta + \frac{b_{1s}}{b}\sin^2 \theta} \rho } \frac{\dd \rho \dd \theta}{\sqrt{a b}} = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho^2 \ee^{-t(\theta) \rho} \frac{\dd \rho \dd \theta}{\sqrt{a b}} =\\
        &=  \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \frac{2}{t^{3}(\theta)} \frac{\dd \theta}{\sqrt{a b}} \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent For convenience, we define the auxiliary functions

\begin{equation}
    f_1 (a,b) = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} t^{-2}(\theta)\, \dd \theta \qq{and} f_2 (a,b) = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} t^{-3}(\theta)\, \dd \theta ,
\end{equation}

\noindent so that the orthogonality condition reads

\begin{equation}
    d (a,b) = \frac{f_1 (a,b)}{2 f_2 (a,b)} \,.
\end{equation}

Let us now compute the energy of the $2s$ state.
The kinetic term is obtained similarly to the previous cases, and equals

\begin{equation}
    \langle\Psi_{2s}| \mathcal{K}|\Psi_{2s}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{a}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b}{\mu_{y}}\right) \frac{2 + d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2}  = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{4} F(a,b) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we defined

\begin{equation}
    F(a,b) = \left(\frac{a}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b}{\mu_{y}}\right) \frac{2 + d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The potential term is computed through the integral

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \langle\Psi_{2s}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{2s}\rangle &= C_{2s}^2 \int \left(1 - d \sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}} \right)^2 \ee^{-2 \sqrt{ax^{2}+by^{2}}} V_{\RK}(r) \dr= \\
                                            &= 2 \frac{\sqrt{a b}}{ (2 - 4d + 3 d^2)}  \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_0} \int_0^{2 \pi} \left(1 + \alpha (\theta) \frac{d}{2} \partial_\alpha \right)^2 I(\alpha, \kappa_0) \, \dd \theta \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent We define now the function

\begin{equation}
    G(a, b) = \frac{1}{ (2 - 4d + 3 d^2)}  \int_0^{2 \pi} \int_0^\infty \mathds{K}(\kappa_0 \alpha(\theta) \rho ) (1- d \rho)^2 \rho\, \ee^{-2 \rho} \dd \rho \dd \theta \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where for the integral we performed a change of variables as in~\eqref{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic}, so that we can write the potential term in the alternative way

\begin{equation}
    \langle\Psi_{2s}|V_{\RK}|\Psi_{2s}\rangle = 2  \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz  r_0} G(a, b) \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent In this way, the expression for the binding energy of the $2s$ state can be more compactly written as

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    E_{2s} = \frac{\hbar^{2}}{4} F(a,b) - 2  \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz  r_0} G(a, b)
\end{empheq}

\noindent which the expression that we need to minimize.
For that, we need the derivatives of the function $d (a,b)$, and they read

\begin{equation}
    \partial_{a} d = \frac{\partial_{a} f_1 f_2 - f_1 \partial_{a} f_2}{2 f_2^2} \qq{and}
    \partial_{b} d = \frac{\partial_{b} f_1 f_2 - f_1 \partial_{b} f_2}{2 f_2^2} \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent and in turn the derivatives of $f_i$ with $i=1,2$

\begin{equation}
    \partial_{a} f_i = \frac{a_{1s} (1+i)}{2a^2} \int_0^{2 \pi} \frac{\cos^2 \theta\, t^{-(2+i)} (\theta)}{t (\theta)-1}  \dd \theta \qq{and}
    \partial_{b} f_i = \frac{b_{1s} (1+i)}{2b^2} \int_0^{2 \pi} \frac{\sin^2 \theta\, t^{-(2+i)} (\theta)}{t (\theta)-1}  \dd \theta \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The derivatives of the function $F$ are straightforward to calculate:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \partial_{a} F &= \frac{2 + d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \frac{1}{\mu_x} + \left(\frac{a}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b}{\mu_{y}}\right) \frac{8 -8d - 4d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \partial_{a} d, \\
        \partial_{b} F &=  \frac{2 + d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \frac{1}{\mu_y} + \left(\frac{a}{\mu_{x}}+\frac{b}{\mu_{y}}\right) \frac{8 -8d - 4d^2}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \partial_{b} d\,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent Regarding the derivatives of $G$, we define two additional auxiliary integrals,

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_1 (\theta) &= \int_0^\infty \mathds{K} (\kappa_0 \alpha (\theta) \rho) (1 - d  \rho) \rho^2 \ee^{-2 \rho} \dd \rho \qq{and} \\
        I_2 (\theta) &= \int_0^\infty \mathds{K}' (\kappa_0 \alpha (\theta) \rho) (1 - d  \rho)^2 \rho^2 \ee^{-2 \rho} \dd \rho \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathds{K}' (\kappa_0 \alpha (\theta) \rho)$ denotes the derivative of $\mathds{K} (z)$ computed at the point $z=\kappa_0 \alpha (\theta) \rho$.
In this way, the derivatives of $G$ read

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \partial_{a} G =& - \frac{1}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \int_0^{2 \pi} \frac{\kappa}{2 r_0 a^2} \frac{\cos^2 \theta}{\sqrt{a^{-1} \cos^2 \theta + b^{-1} \sin^2 \theta}} I_2(\theta)\,  \dd \theta + \\
                        & \frac{\partial_{a} d}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \left[ (4 - 6d)G -2 \int_0^{2 \pi}I_1(\theta) \, \dd \theta \right] \qq{and} \\
        \partial_{b} G =& - \frac{1}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \int_0^{2 \pi} \frac{\kappa}{2 r_0 b^2} \frac{\sin^2 \theta}{\sqrt{a^{-1} \cos^2 \theta + b^{-1} \sin^2 \theta}} I_2(\theta)\,  \dd \theta + \\
                        & \frac{\partial_{b} d}{2 - 4d + 3d^2} \left[ (4 - 6d)G -2 \int_0^{2 \pi}I_1(\theta) \, \dd \theta \right] \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Finally, the derivatives of the energy of the $2s$ state can be compactly written as

\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        &\frac{\partial E_{2s}}{\partial a} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} \partial_{a} F(a,b, d(a,b)) - 2 \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_0} \partial_{a} G(a,b, d(a,b))\,, \\
        &\frac{\partial E_{2s}}{\partial b} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} \partial_{b} F(a,b, d(a,b)) - 2 \frac{e}{4 \pi \epsz r_0} \partial_{b} G(a,b, d(a,b)) \,.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

We stress that to compute the excited state $2s$, the ground state $1s$ has to be computed first, since the orthogonalization parameter $d$ depends on the variational parameters of the ground state.
In the next section we see how we can compute the excitonic optical conductivity using the exciton energies and wavefunctions.

\section{Excitonic Optical Response and Absorption Spectrum}
\label{sec:exciton_optical_response}

If we subject a material to a drive from a monochromatic field with frequency $\omega$, the expression for the excitonic optical conductivity in the linear regime and in the dipole approximation, is given by the following sum over the excitonic states $\nu = 1s,2x,2y,2s$~\cite{Pedersen_2015}

\begin{equation}
    \sigma (\omega) = \frac{e^2}{8 \pi^3 \ii \hbar} \sum_\nu \left[ E_\nu \frac{|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_\nu|^2 }{E_\nu - (\hbar \omega+\ii\Gamma)} + E_\nu \frac{|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_\nu|^2 }{E_\nu + (\hbar \omega+\ii\Gamma)} \right]
    \label{eq:optical_conductivity}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the quantity $\Omega_\nu$ is the Berry curvature of the excitonic state $\nu$, and recalling its expression~\eqref{eq:Berry_curvature_definition}, it is computed by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Berry_curvature_definition_new}
    \boldsymbol{\Omega}_\nu =  \sum_{\bk} \Psi_{\nu} (\bk) \langle v, \bk | \br\, | c, \bk \rangle\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent with no sum over bands as we consider the most simple two-band model for \gls{hbn}, and $\Psi_{\nu} (\bk)$ is the momentum-space wavefunction of the exciton in the state $\nu$, and the braket is the optical matrix element we have encountered before in Section~\ref{subsec:non_interacting_conductivity}, but we still need to explain how we will compute it in practice.
Herein, we consider light polarized along the $x$-direction and along the $y$-direction separately, so that the polarization vector will write $\uvec{e} = \uvec{x}$ or $\uvec{e} = \uvec{y}$ accordingly.
After computing the optical matrix element, we shall compute its dot product with $\uvec{e}$.
We will then have the two distinct conductivities $\sigma_{xx}$ and $\sigma_{yy}$ in the main diagonal of the conductivity tensor.
But before that, we need to list the exciton wavefunctions in momentum space to compute the optical matrix element.

\subsection{Exciton wavefunctions in momentum space}
\label{subsec:exciton_wavefunctions_momentum_space}

Now, we derive the wavefunction of the excitonic ground state in momentum space, and the excited states will follow.
For that, we just need to Fourier transform $\Psi_{1s}(\br)$.
In the isotropic case, the integral is calculated by fixing the orientation of the wavenumber vector $\bk$, and then write $\bk\vdot\br= k r \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\bk$ and $\br$, alongside with a change of variables from cartesian to polar coordinates.
Now we need to do different, because we are evaluating the anisotropic case here.
We perform instead the change of variables

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:change_of_variables_elliptic_x_y}
    \begin{cases}
        x=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{a_{1s}}}\cos\theta\,,\\
        y=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{b_{1s}}}\sin\theta\,.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent Then, the Fourier transform evaluates to (we refer to Appendix~\ref{app:fourier_transform} in case the reader needs to recall the definition adopted in this text)

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Psi_{1s}(\bk) & =\int \ee^{-\ii \bk \vdot \br}\,\Psi_{1s}(\br) \dr = \\
        & = C_{1s}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \ee^{-\ii\rho\left(\frac{k_{x}}{\sqrt{a_{1s}}}\cos\theta+\frac{k_{y}}{\sqrt{b_{1s}}}\sin\theta\right)}\frac{\rho e^{-\rho}}{\sqrt{a_{1s}b_{1s}}} \dd \rho \dd \theta=\\
        & = C_{1s}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_{1s}b_{1s}}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\ii\frac{k_{x}}{\sqrt{a_{1s}}}\cos\theta+\ii\frac{k_{y}}{\sqrt{b_{1s}}}\sin\theta\right)^{2}} \dd\theta=\\
        & =\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\left(a_{1s}b_{1s}\right)^{1/4}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{k_{x}^{2}}{a_{1s}}+\frac{k_{y}^{2}}{b_{1s}}\right)^{3/2}}\, ,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent which has an analytical expression after all.
Fortunately, that will be the case for all states we consider.
We note that when $a_{1s}=b_{1s}$\,, the previous expression depends only on $k=|\bk|$, and we recover the isotropic case.
For all the excited states, we follow a similar reasoning.
For the excited states $2x$ and $2y$, respectively, we have

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:2x_2y_wavefunctions_momentum}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Psi_{2x} (\bk) &= - C_{2x} \frac{3 \ii \pi k_x/a_{2x}}{\left(1 + \frac{k_x^2}{a_{2x}} + \frac{k_y^2}{b_{2x}}\right)^{5/2}} \qq{and} \\
        \Psi_{2y} (\bk) &= - C_{2y} \frac{3 \ii \pi k_y/b_{2y}}{\left(1 + \frac{k_x^2}{a_{2y}} + \frac{k_y^2}{b_{2y}}\right)^{5/2}} \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The excited state $2s$, even though more involved, has also an analytical expression for its Fourier transform, which reads

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{2s} (\bk) = \sqrt{\frac{16 \pi}{(2 - 4d + 3d^2)}} (a_{2s} b_{2s})^{-1/4} \frac{1-2d + (1 + d) \left(\frac{k_x^2}{a_{2s}} + \frac{k_y^2}{b_{2s}}\right)}{\left(1 + \frac{k_x^2}{a_{2s}} + \frac{k_y^2}{b_{2s}}\right)^{5/2}} \,.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Berry curvature and the optical matrix element: the anisotropic case}
\label{subsec:optical_matrix_element_anisotropic_case}

The optical matrix element can be computed by using the identity

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:optical_matrix_element}
    \langle v,\bk|\br|c,\bk\rangle=\frac{\langle v,\bk|[H,\br]|c,\bk\rangle}{\epsilon_{v \bk}-\epsilon_{c \bk}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where the Hamiltonian is the noninteracting one, the ket and the bra correspond to Bloch states of an electron in the conduction and valence bands, respectively, and $\epsilon_{v \bk}$ and $\epsilon_{c \bk}$ are the energies of an electron in the corresponding Bloch states.
By shear coincidence, the subscript $0$ we use in the Hamiltonian $\overline{H}_0$, which stands for the particular case of $\overline{H}_m$ when $m=0$, coincides with the same symbol used in the Section~\ref{sec:optical_effects} to denote the noninteracting Hamiltonian $H_0$.
It happens that $\overline{H}_0$ is our noninteracting Hamiltonian at the moment, so there is no problem in repeating the symbol, we just advert for the different meanings it may have.
In practice, the numerator can be evaluated in terms of the commutator $[\overline{H}_0,\br]$, if in the low-energy Hamiltonian we perceive the momentum components as operators, and take the commutator with $\br$ as an operator as well by using the canonical commutation relations $[x_i,p_j] = \ii \hbar \delta_{ij}$.
After getting the commutator, we compute the matrix element replacing the bra and the ket with their respective spinors of the low-energy Hamiltonian, denoted by $\bC^{v \bk}$ and $\bC^{c \bk}$.
However, it may not seem obvious that the optical matrix element can be computed in this way, without using the Bloch wavefunctions.
We are not aware of any justification for this in the literature, even though it has been used before~\cite{Ventura_2019,Mauricio_2022} and with good final results.
First, one considers from the commutator the term $H \br$, for instance.
Then, we can insert a resolution of the identity $\sum_{n,\bk'} |n,\bk'\rangle \langle n,\bk'|$ between $H$ and $\br$, assuming it is complete.
The matrix elements of $H$ between the Bloch states $\bra{v,\bk}$ and $\ket{n,\bk'}$ are real-space integrals.
If we represent the Bloch wavefunctions in the tight-binding approximation per Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_state_LCAO} as a linear combination of the atomic orbitals, then the matrix element transforms into a linear combination of integrals which are the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian between the atomic orbitals, eventually located at different positions in the lattice---this is nothing more than the transfer integrals, which define the parameters of the tight-binding model.
Since in the wavefunction we have a sum over unit cells $\bR$ with the imaginary exponential $\ee^{\ii \bk \vdot \bR}$, then at the end we would  just have to evaluate $\left(\bC^{v\bk}\right)^{\dagger} H(\bk) \bC^{n,\bk'}$, where $H(\bk)$ is the Bloch Hamiltonian and the arrays $\bC^{n,\bk}$ are its eigenvectors, that we derived earlier and are given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:valence_spinor} and~\eqref{eq:conduction_spinor} for the valence and conduction bands, respectively.
For the position operator, we have integrals of the form $\int \phi_{\beta}(\br - \bR -\bt_j)\, \br\, \phi_{\beta'}(\br - \bR' -\bt_{j'}) \dr$.
In the tight-binding approximation, if the orbitals are point-like, the integral amounts to $\br_{j \beta,j' \beta'} \delta_{\bR \bR'}$, which simplifies the calculation.
Then, we have to use the fact that $\ket{n,\bk'}$ is an eigenstate of $H$, so in the sum of the resolution of the identity, only the term $\ket{n,\bk'} = \ket{v,\bk}$ survives, simplifying even more the calculation.
Then at the end, if we choose the lattice gauge without loss of generality, we would have a term of the form

\begin{equation*}
\begin{gathered}
    \sum_{\bR} \exp{\ii \bk \vdot \bR} \sum_{i\alpha,i'\alpha'} (C^{v \bk}_{i\alpha})^* H_{i\alpha,i'\alpha'}(\bR) C^{v \bk}_{i'\alpha'} \sum_{j'\beta',j\beta} (C^{v \bk}_{j'\beta'})^* \bar{\bar{\br}}_{j'\beta',j\beta} C^{c \bk}_{j\beta} \\
    = (\bC^{v\bk})^\dagger H(\bk) \bC^{v\bk} (\bC^{v\bk})^{\dagger} \bar{\bar{\br}}\, \bC^{c \bk} = \\
    = (\bC^{v\bk})^\dagger H(\bk) \bar{\bar{\br}}\, \bC^{c \bk} \,,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation*}


\noindent where $H(\bk)$ is simply the Bloch Hamiltonian that we have seen before in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_Hamiltonian_definition}, and $\bar{\bar{\br}}$ stands for a matrix representation of the position operator in a basis of localized orbitals.
Upon doing something similar for the other term in the commutator $\br H$, we arrive at the intermediate formula

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    \langle v,\bk|[H,\br]|c,\bk\rangle = (\bC^{v\bk})^\dagger [H(\bk),\bar{\bar{\br}}] \bC^{c \bk}
\end{empheq}

Now, we expand the Bloch Hamiltonian $H(\bk)$ around the $\bk$ point where the exciton is localized, so that we can replace $H(\bk) \to \overline{H}_0$, where the low-energy Hamiltonian is now the renormalized one, and  we allow for the momentum variable $\bk$ to become the momentum operator, and $\bar{\bar{\br}}$ the position operator.
Then, we can replace the commutator $[H,\br]$ with the commutator

\begin{equation}
    [\overline{H}_0,\br] = -\ii \hbar  \vF  \left[ \sigma_{x}\uvec{x}+J_{0}(\beta)\sigma_{y}\uvec{y} \right] \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which was obtained by using the canonical commutation relations.
Then, the initial matrix element between the Bloch states of the commutator between the Hamiltonian and the position operator evaluates to

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:commutator_matrix_element}
    \langle v,\bk|[H,\br]|c,\bk\rangle  \approx -\ii \hbar  \vF  (\bC^{v\bk})^\dagger \left[ \sigma_{x}\uvec{x}+J_{0}(\beta)\sigma_{y}\uvec{y} \right] \bC^{c\bk}\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the arrays $\bC^{v,\bk}$ and $\bC^{c,\bk}$ are the spinors of the low-energy Hamiltonian that we have defined before in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:valence_spinor} and~\eqref{eq:conduction_spinor} for the valence and conduction bands, respectively.
Inserting them in~\eqref{eq:commutator_matrix_element} and going back to the optical matrix element~\eqref{eq:optical_matrix_element}, we find

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:optical_matrix_element_final}
    \langle v,\bk|\br|c,\bk\rangle \approx \frac{\ii\hbar  \vF}{\epsilon_{v \bk}-\epsilon_{c \bk}}\left\{\cos^{2}\frac{\xi_{q}}{2}\left[\uvec{x}-J_{0}(\beta)\uvec{y}\right]-\ee^{-2 \ii \theta_{q}(\beta)}\sin^{2}\frac{\xi_{q}}{2}\left[\uvec{x}+J_{0}(\beta)\uvec{y}\right]\right\}\,.
\end{equation}

Finally, we can compute the Berry curvature for each excitonic state.
We split the discussion into the ground state and the excited states.

\subsubsection{Ground state $1s$}
\label{subsubsec:ground_state_1s}

We recall that the variational wavefunction of the $1s$ state in momentum space, easing the notation and drop the subscripts accompanying the variational parameters, is given by

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{1s}(\bk) =\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\left(a b\right)^{1/4}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{k_{x}^{2}}{a}+\frac{k_{y}^{2}}{b}\right)^{3/2}} \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent To compute the Berry curvature~\eqref{eq:Berry_curvature_definition_new}, we convert the sum over momentum into an integral, for which the following functions will be useful

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \gamma(\theta) &= \sqrt{\cos^{2}\theta+J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sin^{2}\theta} \,,\\
        \sigma(\theta) &=\sqrt{\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{a}+\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{b}} \,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent With these functions, we can write the quantities

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \epsilon_{c \bk}-\epsilon_{v \bk} &= \sqrt{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)+4\hbar^{2} \vF ^{2}[k_{x}^2 + J^2_{0}(\beta)k_{y}^2]} = \sqrt{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta) + 4\hbar^{2} \vF ^{2}k^{2} \gamma^{2}(\theta)}\,, \\
        \cos^{2}\frac{\xi_{q}}{2} &= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2 \hbar  \vF k \gamma(\theta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)^{2}}}\,, \qq{and} \\
        \sin^{2}\frac{\xi_{q}}{2} &= \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2 \hbar  \vF k \gamma(\theta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)^{2}}}\,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent that appear in the optical matrix element, and for the wavefunction

\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{1s}(\bk) =  \frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\left(a b \right)^{1/4}}\frac{1}{\left[1+k^2 \sigma^2(\theta) \right]^{3/2}}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent In the Berry curvature, we have two terms; one that comes from the cosine squared in the optical matrix element in Eq.~\eqref{eq:optical_matrix_element_final}, and another that comes from the sine squared in the same equation.
So we write $\Omega  = \Omega^{\cos}_{1s} + \Omega^{\sin}_{1s}$, and treat both terms separately.
We consider first light polarized along the $x$ axis.
The term that comes from the cosine reads

\begin{equation}
    \Omega^{\cos}_{1s} = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \dd\theta \int_{0}^{\infty} \dd k \frac{ 2\sqrt{2\pi} \ii \hbar \vF k  \left\{\frac{1}{2} + \left[2 \sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2\hbar \vF k \gamma(\theta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)^{2}}\right]^{-1}\right\}}{\left(a_{1}b_{1}\right)^{1/4} \left[1+k^{2}\sigma^{2}(\theta)\right]^{3/2} \sqrt{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)+4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}k^{2}\gamma^{2}(\theta)}}
\end{equation}

\noindent where we have already performed the usual change of variables to polar coordinates.
In the isotropic case, the integrand function does not depend on $\theta$, so from the angular integral it only comes a factor of $2 \pi$, and the radial integral has an analytical form.%\todo{Say somewhere that it is the angular integral that defines the selection rules, and cite Henriques paper, and the 3 papers by Mauricio, tutorial and those with Pedersen}
However, the angular integral is not trivial anymore, but the radial integral is still analytical, so we perform it first.
We obtain

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Omega^{\cos}_{1s} =& \ii \hbar \vF\frac{-2\sqrt{2\pi}}{(a b )^{-1/4}}\int_{0}^{2\pi} \Biggl\{ \frac{\hbar \vF \gamma(\theta)-E_{g}|J_{0}(\beta)|\sigma(\theta)}{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sigma^{3}(\theta) - 4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\sigma(\theta)\gamma^{2}(\theta)} -\\
        & - \frac{E_{g}\hbar \vF|J_{0}(\beta)|\gamma(\theta) \arcsec \left(\frac{E_{g}|J_{0}(\beta)|\sigma(\theta)}{2\hbar \vF\gamma(\theta)}\right)}{\left[E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sigma^{2}(\theta)-4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\gamma^{2}(\theta)\right]^{3/2}} \Biggl\} \dd\theta \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\noindent leaving only the angular integral that can only be evaluated numerically.
The term of the Berry curvature that goes with the sine reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Omega^{\sin}_{1s} &= \int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\left(a_{1}b_{1}\right)^{1/4}}\frac{k}{\left(1+k^{2}\sigma^{2}(\theta)\right)^{3/2}}\frac{\ii \hbar \vF}{\sqrt{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)+4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}k^{2}\gamma^{2}(\theta)}}\left[\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{2\hbar \vF q \gamma(\theta)}{J_{0}(\beta)E_{g}}\right)^{2}}}\right] \ee^{-2\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)} \dd q \dd\theta \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent and performing the radial integral we obtain

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Omega^{\sin}_{1s} =&\frac{- 2\sqrt{2\pi} \ii \hbar \vF}{(a b )^{-1/4}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Bigg[\frac{-\hbar \vF\gamma(\theta)}{E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sigma^{3}(\theta)-4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\sigma(\theta)\gamma^{2}(\theta)} + \\
        &\frac{E_{g}\hbar \vF|J_{0}(\beta)|\gamma(\theta)\arcsec\left(\frac{E_{g}|J_{0}(\beta)|\sigma(\theta)}{2\hbar \vF\gamma(\theta)}\right)}{\left(E_{g}^{2}J_{0}^{2}(\beta)\sigma^{2}(\theta)-4\hbar^{2}\vF^{2}\gamma^{2}(\theta)\right)^{3/2}}\Bigg]\ee^{-2\ii\theta_{k}(\beta)} \dd\theta \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent to be evaluated numerically as well.
The Berry curvature is then simply $\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{1s} \vdot \uvec{x} = \Omega^x_{1s} = \Omega^{\cos}_{1s} + \Omega^{\sin}_{1s}$, where we put an $x$ in superscript to stress that the calculation was for light polarized along the $x$-direction.
For light polarized along the $y$-direction, we can identify from the optical matrix element~\eqref{eq:optical_matrix_element} an extra factor of $J_0(\beta)$, and also a relative sign between the cosine and sine terms, so that the Berry curvature is now $\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{1s} \vdot \uvec{y} = \Omega^y_{1s} = J_0(\beta) (\Omega^{\cos}_{1s} - \Omega^{\sin}_{1s})$.

\subsubsection{Excited states $2x$, $2y$ and $2s$}
\label{subsubsec:excited_states_2x_2y}

Regarding the excited states $2x$ and $2y$, we need to argue why they do not contribute to the optical conductivity, since it is not straightforward to see why they do not contribute even in the anisotropic case.
We consider the wavefunction of the $2x$ state given in equation~\eqref{eq:2x_2y_wavefunctions_momentum}, and note that it has a factor of $\cos \theta$ if we change to polar coordinates.
The angular integral of the Berry curvature that comes from the cosine in the optical matrix element is of the form

\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta \dd \theta\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where the factor $\cos \theta$ comes from the wavefunction, and the function $f$ encodes all the other factors.
We can perform a change of variables $\vartheta = \pi/2 - \theta$ and write

\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta d \theta = -\int_{\pi/2}^{- 3 \pi/2}  f(\sin^2 \vartheta) \sin \vartheta \dd \vartheta =\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}  f(\sin^2 \vartheta) \sin \vartheta \dd \vartheta = 0\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where in the second equality we swapped the limits of the integral and did a shift of $\pi/2$, which is allowed since the integrand is periodic.
The last equality holds because we are integrating an odd function over an interval centered at the origin.
The term that comes from the sine of the optical matrix element is more delicate.
It is of the form

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta \ee^{-2\ii\arctan\left(J_{0}(\beta)\arctan\theta\right)} \dd \theta =  &\int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta \cos(2\arctan\left(J_{0}(\beta)\arctan\theta\right)) \dd \theta - \\
        & \ii \int_0^{2 \pi} f(\cos^2 \theta) \cos \theta \sin(\arctan\left(J_{0}(\beta)\arctan\theta\right)) \dd \theta\,.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent If in the first term in the \gls{rhs} of this equation we perform the same change of variables, we end up with an integral of an odd function again, so it does not contribute.
For the second term, we simply make a shift of $-\pi$ in the integration limits, since the integrand function is odd from the start, so that term vanishes as well.
For the excited state $2y$, all the previous arguments apply as well, so it does not contribute to the conductivity as well.
We are left only with the calculation of the optical conductivity deriving from the state $2s$.
As in the case of the $1s$ state, the radial integral is analytical, and the angular integral has to be performed numerically.
The expression of the radial integral, even though analytical, is complicated, so we will not write it down here.

At the end, we have only the contributions from the $1s$ and $2s$ state to the optical conductivity, hence we write for $x$ polarization

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:optical_conductivities}
    \sigma_{xx} (\omega) &= \frac{e^2}{8 \pi^3 \ii \hbar} \sum_{\nu=1s,2s} E_\nu \frac{|\Omega^x_\nu|^2 }{E_\nu - (\hbar \omega + \ii\Gamma)} + (\omega \to - \omega)^*
\end{empheq}

\noindent and for $y$ polarization the expression is analogous.
We have seen now how to compute the optical conductivity for the system under study.
In the next section we discuss the results obtained.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}


\subsection{Bandgap and excitonic binding energies}
\label{subsec:band_gap_and_binding_energies}

As we have seen in Section~\ref{sec:electronic_band_structure_modulated_hBN}, the effective masses renormalize as $m_x = m^* |J_{0}(\beta)|$ and $m_y = m^*/ |J_{0}(\beta)| $, where $m^* = E_g/2\vF^2$ is the isotropic effective mass of electrons in pristine \gls{hbn}.
This means that as $J_{0}(\beta) \to 0$, the dispersion becomes more and more anisotropic.
To study the effects of this anisotropy in the electronic properties of the system at hand, we compute the bandgap and the binding energies of the excitonic states $1s$, $2x$, $2y$ and $2s$ for varying $\beta$, from the case $\beta=0$ without potential, to $\beta=2.4$ sufficiently close to the zero of the Bessel function $J_0(\beta)$.
We stress that the bandgap includes the electron--electron exchange correction, that also renormalizes with $\beta$ (see Section~\ref{sec:exchange_energy} of the appendix~\ref{app:hBN_superlattice}).
The results are displayed in Fig.~\ref{fig:Eb_of_beta}.
It can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:Eb_of_beta}\pnl{a} that by manipulating the value of $\beta$, we can tune the bandgap.
When $\beta$ reaches the zero of $J_0(\beta)$, the gap closes.
Regarding the binding energies, Fig.~\ref{fig:Eb_of_beta}\pnl{b} shows that the anisotropy lifts the degeneracy between the states $2x$ and $2y$.
Also, the binding energies of the states $1s$, $2x$ and $2s$ decrease as we increase $\beta$, but that of the $2y$ state is nonmonotonic.
Nonetheless, the state $2y$ is always the first excited state.
Initially, the second excited state is $2x$, but for $\beta \approx 1.5$, the $2s$ takes its place.
Naturally, the state $1s$ stays always the ground state.

\subsection{Sub-gap optical conductivity and absorption}
\label{subsec:optical_conductivity_and_absorption}

A recent experimental work~\cite{Elias_2019} showed that single-layer \gls{hbn} displays optical activity in the \gls{uv} range.
The measured photoluminescence exhibits two sharp peaks at a frequency around $6.1$\,eV.
These peaks are excitonic in nature, and their theoretical prediction in~\cite{Ventura_2019} agrees very well with the experimental measurement.
Here we apply the same theory as in~\cite{Ventura_2019} to compute the optical absorption of monolayer \gls{hbn} subjected to the external periodic potential.
But first, we computed the optical conductivity using Eq.~\eqref{eq:optical_conductivity} for selected values of $\beta$, to obtain the results displayed in Figs.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{a}--\pnl{d}.
Since the optical conductivity is complex, we plot separately the real part in Figs.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{a} and~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{b}, and the imaginary part in Figs.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{c} and~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{d}.
In the calculation we focus on the excitonic states $1s$ and $2s$, while the states $2x$ and $2y$ do not contribute to the conductivity as previously explained in Section~\ref{subsubsec:excited_states_2x_2y}.
We also considered both cases of light polarized along the $x$ and $y$ axes.
We observe a tendency of a redshift in the peaks as we increase $\beta$, and consequently the anisotropy of the system.
We note that the energy level $E_{\nu}$ of the excitonic state $\nu$ is given by $E_{\nu} = E_{b,\nu} + \Delta$, where $E_{b,\nu}$ is the respective binding energy and $\Delta$ the bandgap, quantities computed in the previous section.
For light polarized along the $x$-axis, we observe in Figs.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{a} and~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{c} that the peaks of the conductivity coming from the $1s$ and $2s$ states are enhanced as we increase $\beta$.
On the other hand, the peaks in the  conductivity for light polarized along the $y$-direction are attenuated.

One can notice also in Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{b} that for the case $\beta=2.3$, the excitonic states $1s$ and $2s$ become so close, that the imaginary part of the conductivity $\Im \{ \sigma(\omega) \}$ has only one zero.
We expect that this influences the character of the polaritons, since the sign of $\Im \{ \sigma(\omega) \}$ dictates which kind of polaritons, \gls{tm} or \gls{te} the 2D material supports, as we have seen in Section~\ref{subsec:polaritonics_dispersion_relation}.
We can now compute the optical absorption at normal incidence using Fresnel's coefficients for normal incidence through Eq.~\eqref{eq:absorption} from chapter~\ref{ch:classical_electrodynamics}.

Figs.~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{a} and~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{b} display the optical absorption for the same values of $\beta$ as in Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}, for light polarized along the $x$ and $y$-directions, respectively.
Interestingly, the behavior of the absorption is not as simple as that of the conductivity.
For instance, let us look at Fig.~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{a}.
The peak due to the $1s$ state seems to attenuate from right to left, as we increase $\beta$.
In contrast, the peak due to the $2s$ state seems to intensify.

To study more carefully the behavior of the optical absorption with the anisotropy, and consequently, the oscillator strength, in Fig.~\ref{fig:absorption_peaks}\pnl{a} we plot the value of the absorption peaks due to the $1s$ (red lines) and $2s$ (black lines) states separately, for polarization along the $x$ (continuous lines) and $y$-directions (dashed lines), as a function of $\beta$, which is allowed to vary continuously.
Indeed, we see that for polarization along the $x$ axis the $1s$ peak decreases monotonically as we approach the zero of $J_0(\beta)$, while the $2s$ peak tends to increase, until it reaches a maximum right before the zero of the Bessel function.
Interestingly, we can have a $2s$ peak more intense than a $1s$ peak, a feature that can be observed in Fig.~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{a} (notice the gray and blue curves) and~\ref{fig:absorption}\pnl{c}.
This phenomenon has already been observed experimentally~\cite{Ju_2017} and explained theoretically~\cite{Henriques_PRB2022}.
For light polarized along the $y$-direction, one can observe that the absorption for light polarized along the $y$-direction is not as intense as for light polarized along the $x$-direction.
Furthermore, from Fig.~\ref{fig:absorption_peaks}\pnl{a}, we can note that it is now the $2s$ peak that decreases monotonically, while the $1s$ peak is maximum for $\beta \approx 0.9$.

That we can have a $1s$ peak higher than a $2s$ peak can be explained by inspecting Eqs.~\eqref{subeq:Fresnel_reflection_coefficient} and~\eqref{subeq:Fresnel_transmission_coefficient} more closely.
Writing the conductivity in terms of its real and imaginary parts, $\sigma = \sigma' + \ii \sigma''$, and working out $\mathcal{R} = \qty|r|^2$ and $\mathcal{T} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_2 / \varepsilon_1} \qty|t|^2$ (casting the factor $\varepsilon_0 c$ into $\sigma$ for simplicity) we get

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reflection_and_transmission}
    \mathcal{R} = \frac{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2}- \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2 + (\sigma'')^2}{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2  + (\sigma'')^2} \,, \qq{and} \mathcal{T} = \frac{4 \varepsilon_1}{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2 + (\sigma'')^2}
\end{equation}

For each realization, two peaks are observed. The one occurring at lower energy corresponds to the $1s$ state, while the other corresponds to the $2s$ state.  We used for air, the material on top, $\varepsilon_1=1$, and for the quartz substrate $\varepsilon_2=2.16$.}
\label{fig:absorption}
\end{figure}

\noindent If we analyze the previous expressions at the frequency of the $1s$ excitonic resonance where $\sigma'' = 0$ holds and let $\sigma' \to \infty$, which could correspond to the most extreme case of $\beta=2.3$, we can easily see that $\mathcal{R} \to 1$ and $\mathcal{T} \to 0$.
Hence, the system reflects all the incident light.
When the oscillator strength grows immensely making $\sigma'$ grow to infinity, the \bd~material behaves like a perfect mirror, similarly to the surface of a very good conducting metal.
But if we now consider the resonance at the $2s$ excitonic state energy, we see that both $\sigma'$ and $\sigma''$ are finite (the imaginary part has only one zero in the most extreme case $\beta=2.3$, see Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_grid}\pnl{c}), which means that $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are finite, and consequently, so is the absorption.
Initially, without potential, the $1s$ resonance is still higher than the $2s$ resonance, both in the conductivity and the absorption, but that does not contradict our reasoning that leads to the perfect mirror. To see that, we work out explicitly the expression for $\mathcal{A}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:absorption} to yield

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A} = 4 \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} \frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_2}- \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma'}{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2 + (\sigma'')^2} \,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Looking at the resonant frequencies, we have always $\sigma''=0$ (unless the $2s$ resonance is close enough to the $1s$ resonance so that $\sigma'' \neq 0$) and then the absorption peak is given by

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    \max_{\omega}{\mathcal{A}} = 4 \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} \frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_2}- \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma'}{(\sqrt{\varepsilon_2} + \sqrt{\varepsilon_1} + \sigma')^2}
\end{empheq}

\noindent where the real part of the conductivity is computed at resonant frequencies corresponding to the excitonic energies $E_{1s}$ and $E_{2s}$.
If we plot the previous expression as a function of $\sigma'$ as in Fig.~\ref{fig:absorption_peaks}\pnl{b}, we observe in fact that it is not monotonous, which explains why we can have in general a $2s$ peak higher than a $1s$ peak.

To finish the analysis of the interaction of light with our monolayer \gls{hbn} under a periodic potential, within the theme of strong-coupling, we shall now look at the dispersion relation of the emergent exciton--polaritons our system can support.

\subsection{Exciton--polaritons}
\label{subsec:exciton-polaritons}

Polaritons are light--matter hybrids, arising from the strong-coupling of elementary excitations of a 2D material with light.
Polaritonic modes are characterized by an evanescent decay away from the material and a strong enhancement of the \gls{em} field in its vicinity.
There are many kinds of polaritons, like phonon--polaritons, plasmon--polaritons (see Section~\ref{subsec:polaritonics_dispersion_relation}, Fig.~\ref{fig:SPP_dispersion_relation}), magnon--polaritons and exciton--polaritons, depending on the material and also the frequency range~\cite{basov_nanoph10,Basov_Garcia_de_Abajo_2016}.
Here we are concerned only with exciton--polaritons.
The dispersion relation of the polariton depends on the polarization of the \gls{em} field, as we have seen previously.
For \gls{te} modes, the electric field is in the plane of the \bd~material, and perpendicular to the wavevector of the polariton, while the magnetic field lies on the $xz$-plane.
We recall that the dispersion relation for \gls{te} modes is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:TE_modes_disp_rel}
    \kappa_1 + \kappa_2 - \ii \omega \mu_0 \sigma_{yy}(\omega) = 0,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\varepsilon_j$, $j=1,2$, is the relative electric permittivity of the two media cladding the \gls{hbn} layer in the frequency region herein considered, $\kappa_j = \sqrt{k^2 - \varepsilon_j \omega^2/c^2}$, $\omega$ is the frequency of the polariton, $q$ is the in-plane wavenumber, and $\mu_0$ is the vacuum magnetic permeability.
For \gls{tm}  modes, it is the magnetic field that lies on the plane of the 2D material, while the electric field is in the $xz$-plane. Their dispersion relation is

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:TM_modes_disp_rel}
    \frac{\varepsilon_1}{\kappa_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{\kappa_2} + \ii  \frac{\sigma_{xx} (\omega)}{\varepsilon_0 \omega} = 0
\end{empheq}

As a rule of thumb, \gls{te} modes are supported when the imaginary part of the conductivity is negative, whereas \gls{tm}  modes are supported when the imaginary part of the conductivity is positive.
The solutions of the previous two equations, that can only be obtained numerically if $\varepsilon_1 \neq \varepsilon_2$, gives the dispersion relation $\omega(q)$, where the wavenumber has now an imaginary part, since the conductivity is complex\footnote{We could also consider complex frequency, as explained in footnote~\ref{fn:complex_frequency} of Chapter~\ref{ch:classical_electrodynamics}.}.
It is important to highlight that in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:TE_modes_disp_rel} and~\eqref{eq:TM_modes_disp_rel} two different conductivities appear.
In isotropic systems, such as the case of monolayer \gls{hbn}, the conductivities $\sigma_{xx}$ and $\sigma_{yy}$ coincide.
However, they are in general different, as we have seen in the previous section.

Regarding the \gls{te} modes, we observe that they are either very poorly confined and present a similar dispersion to free light as expected~\cite{Bludov_primer,GoncalvesPeres:2016}, or they do not exist at all---the equation~\eqref{eq:TE_modes_disp_rel} does not present any solution.
Whence, we omit the analysis for these modes, as they are not relevant.
We focus solely on the \gls{tm}  modes.
To obtain the dispersion relation $\omega(q)$ for \gls{tm}  polarized polaritons, we solve numerically Eq.~\eqref{eq:TM_modes_disp_rel} in the regions where $\Im \{\sigma_{xx}(\omega)\} > 0$, for different realizations of $\beta$.
In general, the imaginary part of the conductivity admits more than one zero, and in our case, we have observed in Section~\ref{subsec:optical_conductivity_and_absorption} that it has either two or one.
The main region of frequencies of interest is that between the first and second zeros, in the case $\Im \{\sigma_{xx}(\omega)\}$ does have two zeros.
After the third zero, we are very near the bottom of the conduction band, where the sub-gap conductivity we computed has a nonvanishing contribution from the noninteracting conductivity above the gap~\cite{Ivchenko_2021}, but herein we are only concerned with the coupling of the light field with the interacting excitation that results in the exciton.

The dispersion relation of \gls{tm}  modes is presented in Figs.~\ref{fig:polaritons_disp_rel}\pnl{a}--\pnl{c}, for three different values of $\beta$.
The case $\beta=0$ (panel~\pnl{a}) represents \gls{hbn} with no potential on top, and we also choose an intermediate case $\beta = 0.5$ (panel~\pnl{b}), and an extreme case $\beta=2.3$ (panel~\pnl{c}).
The case without potential and the intermediate case share similar features, namely the dispersive behavior very distinct from free light, with the real part of the wavenumber $\Re\{q\}$ reaching a high maximum value at a specific frequency, and the high degree of confinement given the high values that $\Im\{q\}$ can reach, similar to what we saw for the plasmon--polariton in Fig.~\ref{fig:SPP_dispersion_relation}.
However, the $\beta = 2.3$ case in Fig.~\ref{fig:polaritons_disp_rel}\pnl{c} is qualitatively different.
This is due to the facts that in this case the imaginary part of the conductivity has only one zero and the conductivity peaks at higher values.
As a consequence, the dispersion behaves linearly for low frequencies and then behaves like the previous two cases, with $\Re\{q\}$ achieving a maximum around $36\,\unit{\micro \meter}^{-1}$.
Then, the dispersion bends until $\Re\{q\}$ reaches a minimum of $14\,\unit{\micro \meter}^{-1}$, and afterwards it increases back again.
This has been predicted by Wolff et al.~in Ref~\cite{Wolff:2018}.
Therein, the authors saw that in the case of real frequency and complex wavenumber, if the oscillator strength is high enough, then the curve $\omega$ of $\Re\{q\}$ bends back at the resonant frequency, in quite general terms.
Here we just happen to verify it in a particular system.
The degree of confinement is also lower in this last case, as can be seen by the different wavenumber scales (\unit{\nano\meter}$^{-1}$ in Figs.~\ref{fig:polaritons_disp_rel}\pnl{a} and~\ref{fig:polaritons_disp_rel}\pnl{b}, $\unit{\micro\meter}^{-1}$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:polaritons_disp_rel}\pnl{c}).
