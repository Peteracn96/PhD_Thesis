\section{Dielectric Function Revisited: Theory}
\label{sec:dielectric_function_revisited}

%Here we recall the expression of the microscopic dielectric function in 2D, and take it for insulators and semiconductors and the static limit.

In this section we look again into the \rpa~dielectric function we have previously introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:rpa_dielectric_function}.
The dielectric function in real-space and time domain, within \rpa, that we discussed in Section~\ref{sec:dielectric_function_quantum} writes

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dielectric_function_real_time_recalled}
    \varepsilon (\br,\br';t,t') = \delta(\br - \br') \delta(t-t') - \int  v_c(\br - \br'') \chi^0 (\br'',\br';t-t') \dr''  \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $v_c$ denotes the bare (without screening) Coulomb potential and $\chi^0$ is the \rpa~irreducible polarizability.
When Fourier transformed from the $(\br,t)$ domain to the $(\omega,\bq)$ domain, it has the form

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency_repeated}
    \varepsilon_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega) = \delta_{\bG \bG'} - v_c(\bq + \bG) \chi^0_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\bG$ and $\bG'$ are reciprocal lattice vectors, $\bq$ is a vector in the first \bz, and $\omega$ is the frequency.
This is nothing more than a recapitulation of what we have discussed in Section~\ref{sec:dielectric_function_quantum}.
The novelty in this chapter is that the Fourier transform is not in three dimensions, but in two dimensions while overlooking the $z$ dimension, as if $\br=(x,y)$.
Since in this work we deal with \bd~materials, we argue that performing a \bd~Fourier transform on the dielectric function is the right thing to do, because the material is infinite in the $xy$ plane, but finite along the $z$-direction.
Technically speaking, we are saying that the response to an off-plane varying external potential is local, so that $\varepsilon(\br,\br') = \varepsilon^{\BD}(\br_{||},\br_{||}') \delta(z-z')$, where $\varepsilon^{\BD}$ is a strictly \bd~dielectric function, where $\br_{||}$ and $\br_{||}'$ are in-plane vectors, whose Fourier transform is~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency_repeated}.

Within the strict \bd~framework for the dielectric response we develop here, we can ignore the $z$ coordinate altogether in the dielectric function.
This is evidently an approximation that needs to be justified.
We postpone doing so to Section~\ref{subsec:quasi_2D}, and let us see how far we can go with a pure \bd~picture.
In what follows, we drop the $\BD$ superscript in $\varepsilon$ for ease of notation.
But still, the reader should keep in mind that it is a \bd~quantity.

In what follows all the geometric quantities like position, reciprocal lattice vectors, and momentum are in-plane.
Therefore, the momentum-resolved Coulomb potential in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency_repeated} reads
\todo[inline]{Shorten the white space after writing the intro.}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:coulomb_momentum_repeated}
    v_c(\bq) =  \frac{e}{2  \epsz q} \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which depends on momentum as $\sim 1/q$, unlike its \gls{3d} counterpart that goes with $\sim 1/q^2$.
As we will explain in a moment, this small detail puts us in advantage over typical first-principle approaches.

Naturally, the polarizability $\chi^0_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega)$ is also the \bd~counterpart of Eq.~\eqref{eq:polarizability_momentum_frequency}, and reads

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:2D_polarizability}
    \chi^0_{\bG \bG'} (\bq,\omega) = \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{n,n'} \sum_{\bk \sigma} (f_{n,\bk} - f_{n',\bk + \bq}) \frac{\mel{n,\bk}{\ee^{-\ii(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br}}{n',\bk + \bq} \mel{n',\bk + \bq}{\ee^{\ii(\bq + \bG') \vdot \br}}{n,\bk}}{\epsilon_{n \bk} - \epsilon_{n' \bk + \bq} + \hw + \ii \hbar \alpha}\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent At the end, the expressions~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency_repeated} and~\eqref{eq:2D_polarizability} do not differ by much.
The resemblance is clear from their written forms, but the main contrast is in the implementation.
First, the sum over momentum $\bk$ is a sum over the \bd~\bz~of the material.
Furthermore, even though it is not explicitly evidentiated in the braket notation, the plane-wave matrix elements in the numerator amount to real-space integrals extending over the plane of the material, and are therefore integrals in \bd~space.
Such matrix elements have appeared before in Section~\ref{subsec:interaction_matrix_elements_and_screening}, when discussing the interaction matrix elements for the \bse~within an exact diagonalization formalism.
To make this chapter as self-contained as possible, we repeat the definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:plane_wave_matrix_element_definition} from that section, but now making it explicit that the integral is in two dimensions.
Henceforth, we can write

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:2D_plane_wave_matrix_element}
    I^{\bG}_{n \bk, n' \bk'} \equiv \bra{n,\bk} \ee^{-\ii (\bk - \bk' + \bG) \vdot \br} \ket{n',\bk'} = \int_{\area} \dr\, \psi^*_{n,\bk} (\br) \ee^{-\ii(\bk  - \bk' + \bG) \vdot \br} \psi_{n', \bk'} (\br)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent Note that even though the notation is similar to that of Ref.~\cite{Xatu}, the definition for $I^{\bG}_{n \bk, n' \bk'}$ is not exactly the same.
We will explain how to compute the matrix elements and the polarizability in practice in the next subsection.

\subsection{Noninteracting polarizability}
\label{subsec:non_interacting_polarizability}

Since we are interested in electrostatic screening for \bd~insulators and semiconductors (from now on, let us just say insulators to account for all \bd~gapped materials), and at very low temperatures, we can simplify the polarizability in Eq.~\eqref{eq:2D_polarizability} to

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:static_polarizability_repeated}
    \begin{aligned}
        \chi^0_{\bG\bG'}(\bq,\omega \to 0) = & \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\vv \cc,\bk \sigma} \Bigg[\frac{\langle \cc,\bk| \ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}|\vv,\bk+\bq\rangle \langle \vv,\bk+\bq| \ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG')\cdot\br}|\cc,\bk\rangle}{\epsilon_{\vv \bk+\bq}-\epsilon_{\cc \bk}} \Bigg] + \\
        & \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\vv \cc,\bk \sigma} \Bigg[\frac{\langle \vv,\bk| \ee^{-\ii(\bq+\bG)\cdot\br}|\cc,\bk+\bq\rangle\langle \cc,\bk+\bq| \ee^{\ii(\bq+\bG')\cdot\br}|\vv,\bk\rangle}{\epsilon_{\vv \bk} - \epsilon_{\cc \bk+\bq}}\Bigg] \,,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the sum over bands $n$ and $n'$ is now over valence bands $\vv$ and conduction bands $\cc$.
In the previous expression, if the system enjoys \gls{trs}, then the two terms on the right-hand side add up so that we can write the polarizability in a more compact form as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:static_polarizability_TRS}
    \chi^0_{\bG \bG'} (\bq) = \frac{2}{\area} \sum_{\vv \cc}  \sum_{\bk \sigma} \frac{I^{\bG}_{\cc \bk, \vv \bk + \bq}  \qty(I^{\bG'}_{\cc \bk, \vv \bk + \bq})^*}{\epsilon_{\vv \bk + \bq} - \epsilon_{\cc \bk} }\,
\end{equation}

\noindent We would like to take this opportunity to make a brief remark.
We have confirmed analytically that the expression~\eqref{eq:static_polarizability_repeated} derives from its more general version in~\eqref{eq:2D_polarizability}, which is an expression commonly found in textbooks~\cite{Cohen_Louie_2016}.
However, the expression in~\eqref{eq:static_polarizability_TRS} is not commonly found in textbooks, but it has made its appearance in the literature~\cite{BerkeleyGW,Yambo_2009}.
We stress that the prefactor of $2$ and the sum over spins $\sigma$ are key to get correct screening properties.
We prove in Appendix~\ref{app:polarizability_TRS_proof} that expression~\eqref{eq:static_polarizability_repeated} reduces to~\eqref{eq:static_polarizability_TRS} when \gls{trs} is present.
All systems considered in this thesis enjoy \gls{trs}, which can be broken, for instance, when a static magnetic field is applied.
As we have stated before in Section~\ref{subsec:maxwell_equations}, we do not study magnetized media.

We have gathered all the necessary knowledge to compute these matrix elements, and thus the matrix elements of the noninteracting polarizability $\chi^0_{\bG \bG'}(\bq)$.
As we said before, the material is in practice \bd~without any repeated off-plane images, unlike in first-principle approaches.
Therefore, our implementation is strictly \bd, and we ignore, for the moment, any information pertaining to the out-of-plane degrees of freedom.
This means that:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The sum over momentum in~\eqref{eq:static_polarizability_repeated} is a sum over momentum $\bk = (k_x,k_y)$ in the \bz, which is \bd.
    \item The $\bG$ and $\bG'$ vectors are \bd~vectors of the reciprocal lattice, which is \bd.
    \item The matrix element $I^{\bG}_{n \bk, n' \bk'}$, which is an integral in real space when written in terms of Bloch state wavefunctions, is an integral in the $xy$ plane.
          The symbol $\dr$ inside the integrals therefore means $\dd x \dd y$, if we do not say otherwise.
\end{itemize}

\noindent With this information in mind, we can work out the plane-wave matrix elements by replacing the Bloch wavefunctions by their expression in terms of localized orbitals, that in the lattice gauge read

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Bloch_state_LCAO_repeated}
    \psi_{n \bk} (\br) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \vdot \bR} \sum_{i \alpha} C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk} \phi_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_i)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent The plane-wave matrix elements between Bloch states have actually been calculated before in Section~\ref{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_effective_models}, at the time when we derived the \bse~applied to effective models.
At this stage, we compute them approximately using once again the point-like orbital approximation, but beyond the envelope function approximation as we did in Section~\ref{sec:bethe_salpeter_equation_effective_models}.
Let us then carry out the integral once more.
We have

\begin{align}
    \label{eq:lane_wave_matrix_element}
    I^{\bG}_{n \bk, n' \bk'} =& \int \dr\, \psi^*_{n,\bk} (\br)\, \ee^{-\ii(\bk' - \bk + \bG) \vdot \br} \psi_{n' \bk'} (\br) = \notag \\
    =& \frac{1}{N} \int \dr \sum_{\bR \bR'} \ee^{- \ii \bk \vdot \bR} \ei{\bk' \vdot \bR'} \sum_{i \alpha j \beta} (C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk})^* C_{j \beta}^{n' \bk'} \underbrace{\phi^*_{\alpha} (\br - \bR - \bt_i) \phi_{\beta} (\br - \bR' - \bt_j)}_{\approx \delta_{ij} \delta_{\alpha \beta} \delta_{\bR \bR'} \delta(\br - \bR - \bt_i)} \ee^{-\ii(\bk' - \bk + \bG) \vdot \br} = \notag \\
    =& \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\bR} \cancel{\ee^{-\ii (\bk - \bk') \vdot \bR}} \sum_{i \alpha} (C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk})^* C_{i \alpha}^{n' \bk} \cancel{\ee^{-\ii (\bk' - \bk) \vdot \bR}} \ee^{- \ii \bG \vdot \bR} \ee^{- \ii (\bk' - \bk)  \vdot \bt_i} \ee^{-\ii \bG  \vdot \bt_i} =  \notag \\
    =& \frac{1}{N} \left(\sum_{\bR} \ee^{-\ii \bG \vdot \bR} \right) \sum_{i \alpha} (C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk})^* C_{i \alpha}^{n' \bk'}  \ee^{-\ii (\bk' - \bk + \bG)  \vdot \bt_i} = \notag \\
    =& \sum_{i \alpha} (C_{i \alpha}^{n \bk})^* C_{i \alpha}^{n' \bk'}  \ee^{-\ii (\bk' - \bk + \bG)  \vdot \bt_i}\,, \qq{since} \sum_{\bR} \ee^{-\ii \bG \vdot \bR} = N \,,
\end{align}

\noindent where from the 2nd line to the 3rd line we did the point-like orbital approximation, and from the 4th line to the 5th line we used the fact that by definition $\ee^{-\ii \bG \vdot \bR} = 1$.

To summarize, to compute a single polarizability matrix element $\chi^0_{\bG \bG'} (\bq)$ we need:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The Bloch Hamiltonian $H(\bk)$ (regardless of the gauge) and to diagonalize it to obtain the band structure $\{\epsilon_{n \bk}\}$ in the set of points $\bk$ included in the sum and the eigenvectors $\{\bC^{n \bk}\}$ storing the tight-binding coefficients $C^{n \bk}_{i \alpha}$;
    \item The set of energies $\{\epsilon_{n \bk + \bq} \}$ and eigenvectors $\{\bC^{n \bk + \bq}\}$, where $\bq$ is the point in the \bz~we are computing the polarizability at;
    \item The plane-wave matrix elements of the form $I^{\bG}_{\cc \bk, \vv \bk + \bq} = \sum_{i \alpha} (C_{i \alpha}^{\cc \bk})^* C_{i \alpha}^{\vv \bk + \bq}  \ee^{-\ii (\bq + \bG)  \vdot \bt_i}$, as we know the position of the atoms in the motif $\bt_i$ from the beginning.
\end{itemize}

In our numerical framework, the Bloch Hamiltonian $H(\bk)$ is determined by the input model for the code.
The code accepts two types of input.
One way is by providing an output file from CRYSTAL~\cite{CRYSTAL_23}, which contains the Fock matrices $H(\bR)$ determining the Bloch Hamiltonian through Eq.~\eqref{eq:Bloch_Hamiltonian_definition}, as we saw in Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_mechanics_solids}.
Alternatively, the code accepts also \texttt{.model} files containing a tight-binding model for the material at hand.
Quite recently, the code was updated by G.~Janone with a utility that transforms an output file from Wannier90~\cite{Wannier90} into a \texttt{.model} file.
The corresponding version of the code can be found in~\url{https://github.com/GuiJanone/xatu-w90}.
This utility serves as an intermediary between Wannier90 and the Xatu code.

The Wannier model for the Hamiltonian has to be obtained through the method of maximally localized Wannier functions~\cite{Marzari_2012}, in order for the point-like orbital approximation to be as true as possible.
Then, the orbitals $\phi_{\alpha}$ can be perceived as Wannier functions (see footnote~\ref{fn:Wannier_functions} in Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_mechanics_solids}) as well, besides their interpretation as atomic orbitals.
In general, in a Wannier representation of the Hamiltonian, we have as many ``atomic positions'' $\bt_i$ as orbitals in total, but only one orbital per site within the motif of the unit cell.
On the contrary, in a tight-binding model, we have as many atomic positions $\bt_i$ as atoms in the motif, and generically more than one orbital per atom.
Either way, we do not consider the spatial structure of the orbitals.
Therefore, our approach applies equally to both cases.
Upon diagonalization of the Bloch Hamiltonian $H(\bk)$ at momenta $\bk$ and $\bk + \bq$, we obtain the energy dispersions $\{\epsilon_{n \bk}\}$ and $\{\epsilon_{n \bk + \bq}\}$ from the eigenvalues, and the tight-binding coefficients $\{\bC^{n \bk}\}$ and $\{\bC^{n \bk + \bq}\}$ from the eigenvectors.
In math notation and for an orthogonal basis, $H(\bk) \bC^{n \bk} = \epsilon_{n \bk} \bC^{n \bk}$.
Using this information to compute the polarizability matrix elements, we can compute the elements of the dielectric function, which is the subject of the next subsection.

\subsection{Dielectric matrix and its inversion}
\label{subsec:dielectric_matrix_inverse}

We are interested in the static limit of the dielectric function as per Eq.~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency_repeated}, for which we need the static polarizability worked out in the previous section.
The static dielectric function, within \rpa, writes

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\colorbox{lightgray}, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:static_symmetrized_dielectric_function}
    \varepsilon_{\bG \bG'} (\bq) = \delta_{\bG \bG'} - \sqrt{v_c(\bq + \bG)} \chi^0_{\bG \bG'} (\bq) \sqrt{v_c(\bq + \bG')}
\end{empheq}

\noindent if we adopt the symmetrized version of~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency_repeated}.
The reason why we implement the symmetrized version is that it eases the treatment of numerical divergences in the dielectric function and, subsequently, in the screened potential in the long-wavelength limit~\cite{Baldereschi_Tosatti_1978,Rasmussen_Schmidt_Winther_Thygesen_2016,Shishkin_Kresse_2006}.
Actually, for the head element $\varepsilon_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq \to \bzero)$, it is indifferent if we use the symmetrized version as per Eq.~\eqref{eq:static_symmetrized_dielectric_function}, or the initial one in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dielectric_function_momentum_frequency_repeated}.
For this element, we can simply set $\varepsilon_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq \to \bzero) = 1$, because the bare potential goes with $v_c(\bq) \sim 1/q$, while the polarizability goes with $\chi^0_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq \to \bzero) \sim q^2$, as we shall confirm in Subsection~\ref{subsec:non_interacting_polarizability_results} (see Fig.~\ref{fig:chi_00_hbndft_mesh}).
This feature of the head element of the polarizability is universal among insulators, either in two or three dimensions, as long as the material presents a bandgap~\cite{Thygesen_2017}.
This means that, when computing the matrix element $\varepsilon_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq)$ in the limit $\bq \to \bzero$, we face no numerical instabilities unlike in the \gls{3d} case, where $v_c(\bq) \sim 1/q^2$.

In \abinit~calculations for \bd~materials, it is typical to use a truncated Coulomb potential instead of the full one, in order to mitigate the interaction between repeated images, with increasing off-plane separation~\cite{Ismail-Beigi_2006,Huser_Olsen_Thygesen_2013,Rasmussen_Schmidt_Winther_Thygesen_2016}.
Only after performing a \bd~Fourier transform on the truncated Coulomb potential and taking the limit $q \to 0$, can one recover the $1/q$ behavior in the potential, removing any numerical instabilities when taking that limit.
However, it still depends on the off-plane separation between layers $L_{\perp}$.
But even in that case, the value of the static macroscopic dielectric function $\varepsilon_M$ at zero momentum is not exactly one~\cite{Huser_Olsen_Thygesen_2013}.
The results have to be converged by increasing $L_{\perp}$, and only in the limit of infinite separation $\varepsilon_M (0) = 1$ for a \bd~material.
Besides that, the \bd~macroscopic dielectric function is not given by the definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macroscopic_dielectric_function_definition}.
It can only be obtained after performing an off-plane average of the inverse \gls{3d} microscopic dielectric function~\cite{Huser_Olsen_Thygesen_2013,Jornada_PhD,Diana_PhD}, because the definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macroscopic_dielectric_function_definition} is not directly applicable.

With our framework, the macroscopic dielectric function we get from inverting the microscopic one as per Eq.~\eqref{eq:static_symmetrized_dielectric_function} is automatically an effective \bd~macroscopic dielectric function.
Also, we have $\varepsilon_{\bzero \bzero} (\bzero) = \varepsilon^{-1}_{\bzero \bzero} (\bzero) = 1$ by construction.
Regarding the numerical treatment of the wing terms $\varepsilon_{\bzero \bG'} (\bq)$ and $\varepsilon_{\bG \bzero} (\bq)$, it is required to look at the asymptotic behaviour of the matrix elements of the dielectric matrix and its inverse when $\bq \to \bzero$.
Only in that way can we know how to avoid divergences or undefined values for the screened potential.
This is critical for the computation of the excitons, due to how the screened Coulomb potential appears in the calculation of the interaction matrix elements (see Eqs.~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal} and~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_exchange_reciprocal}).
Issues like this are routine in the literature~\cite{BerkeleyGW,Louie_2000,Rasmussen_Schmidt_Winther_Thygesen_2016,Guandalini_Varsano_2023}.
To not break the flow of the text, we relegate the details to Appendix~\ref{app:regularization}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
The calculations are also much more efficient due to the reduced dimensionality of our approach.
To compute the dielectric matrix and invert it, we have no off-plane contribution to the reciprocal lattice vectors, so the number of matrix elements to compute, at each point $\bq \in \BZ$, scales with $N^2$ instead of $N^3$, if $N$ is the number of cells of the reciprocal lattice along the directions of the two primitive vectors.
Then, in two dimensions the number of linear combinations is $N^2$, and in three dimensions it is $N^3$.

After having computed the dielectric matrix, we need to invert it.
This poses a difficulty in the problem: there are infinite reciprocal lattice vectors; therefore, we need to apply a cutoff $G_c$ for the vectors $\bG$ and truncate the dielectric matrix to invert it in practice.
Doing so is routine in the literature~\cite{BerkeleyGW,GPAW_2025,Yambo_2009}.
Even if we have decided to follow the alternative approach for the \rpa~inverse dielectric function, given by~\cite{Dielectric_Genome}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:inverse_dielectric_function_alternative}
    \varepsilon^{-1}_{\bG \bG'} (\bq) = \delta_{\bG \bG'} + v_c(\bq + \bG) \chi^{\RPA}_{\bG \bG'} (\bq) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\chi^{\RPA}_{\bG \bG'} (\bq)$ is the reducible polarizability within \rpa, defined by the Dyson equation \eqref{eq:Dyson_equation_chi_RPA}.
In that equation, all quantities when written in momentum space are infinite matrices as well, so we would need to apply a cutoff for the vectors $\bG$ either way.
This is in great contrast with the jellium model considered by H.~Bruus and K.~Flensberg in Ref~\cite{Bruus_Flensberg_2004}, where there is no underlying crystalline structure, and as a consequence, all quantities like the Coulomb potential, the polarizability, and the dielectric function are simply scalars.
For now, let us assume that we can in principle compute the dielectric matrix and invert it without any problems.
In that way, the screened Coulomb potential in the static limit reads

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:screened_static_Coulomb_potential}
    W_{\bG \bG'} (\bq) = \sqrt{v_c(\bq + \bG)}  \varepsilon^{-1}_{\bG \bG'} (\bq) \sqrt{v_c(\bq + \bG')} \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent which is symmetrized as well by virtue of using the symmetrized dielectric function~\eqref{eq:static_symmetrized_dielectric_function}.
Looking at the dynamical expression of the polarizability, we can see that the frequency dependence comes in the denominator as $\hbar \omega - (\epsilon_{n' \bk+\bq} - \epsilon_{n \bk})$.
If the band gap is large enough when compared to the frequency, then we can approximate the screened Coulomb potential by its static limit and discard an integration in frequency in the interaction matrix elements.

To finish the discussion, we revisit the interaction matrix elements in the \gls{bse} for the excitons with the numerical approach.
Their expressions derived in Section~\ref{subsec:interaction_matrix_elements_and_screening} remain the same, apart from the prefactor that contains the area $\area$ instead of the volume $\vol$, since we follow a strict \bd\ approach.
For the direct term we have

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox, right={\,,}]{align}
    \label{eq:direct_term}
    D_{\vv\cc,\vv'\cc'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \qty(I^{\bG}_{\cc \bk+\bQ, \cc' \bk'+\bQ})^* W_{\bG \bG'}(\bk - \bk') I^{\bG'}_{\vv \bk, \vv' \bk'} \,,
\end{empheq}

\noindent while for the exchange term

\begin{empheq}[innerbox=\fbox,right={\,.}]{align}
    \label{eq:exchange_term}
    X_{\vv\cc,\vv'\cc'}(\bk,\bk',\bQ) = \frac{1}{\area} \sum_{\bG,\bG'} \qty(I^{\bG}_{\cc \bk+\bQ, \vv \bk})^* W_{\bG \bG'}(\bQ) I^{\bG'}_{\cc' \bk' + \bQ, \vv' \bk'}
\end{empheq}

\noindent The reader shall notice that in both expressions there is a sum over reciprocal lattice vectors.
In fact, it is a double sum over $\bG$ and $\bG'$.
This double sum accounts for local-field effects in the calculation of the excitons, which derives from the translational invariance being discrete and not continuous.
If we have enough $(\bG,\bG')$ matrix elements in the dielectric function, in principle we will not need as many terms in the sums above.
In practice, we have two cutoffs, one for the dielectric function $G_c$, and another one for the excitons $G^{X}_{c}$.

\section{Dielectric Function Revisited: Numerical Results}
\label{sec:dielectric_function_results}

We analyze in this section the numerical results obtained from the \bd~implementation of the dielectric function.
We will follow the same order as in the previous section; we focus first on the polarizability, then on the dielectric matrix and its inverse, and finally the excitons.
When discussing the dielectric function, we shall make the connection with the macroscopic dielectric function and take the small $\bq$ limit when relevant.
In principle, we should recover the Rytova--Keldysh model and get an estimate for the screening parameter $r_0$.
The material of choice is \gls{hbn} once more; but now, we work with a more realistic model as we shall describe, and look at it from a different perspective.

\subsection{hBN revisited: DFT band structure and Hamiltonian model}
\label{subsec:hBN_revisited}

In this section we describe our model Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn}, a \bd~material quite important in this thesis.
We introduced for the first time a minimal tight-binding model for \gls{hbn} in Section~\ref{subsubsec:tight_binding_method}, with only two parameters; one parameter for the difference in onsite energies between boron and nitrogen atoms, and the hopping parameter between first neighbors.
Then on chapter~\ref{ch:Optical_hBN_superlattice}, we used a model based on the initial one considered in chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_mechanics_solids} to study excitons and the excitonic optical response in a \gls{hbn} nanostructure.
We also used the Rytova--Keldysh model to account for the screening.
In this chapter, we work out a numerical model for \gls{hbn}.
For that, we need a more realistic model based on \abinit~methods.
After all, it is with \abinit~methods that we can fit the tight-binding band structure to the \abinit~one to get the tight-binding parameters.

Herein, the model Hamiltonian for \gls{hbn} was obtained through \dft~calculations (see Subsection~\ref{subsubsec:abinit_methods}) with the commercial package CRYSTAL~\cite{CRYSTAL_23}, which uses a framework of Gaussian basis functions.
In our calculations, we used the hybrid functional HSE06~\cite{Heyd_Scuseria_Ernzerhof_2003}, which mixes the exact \gls{hf} exchange functional and the Perdew--Burke--Ernzerhof functional~\cite{Perdew_1996} within the \gls{gga}\footnote{In typical \abinit~calculations within \dft, there is always an ambiguity in the functional one can choose to capture exchange and correlation effects. This is because no one knows the analytical expression for the exact exchange--correlation functional, if it even exists. We know however that it is a functional of the electron density $n(\br)$ by the Hohenberg--Kohn theorems, we just do not have an analytical expression for it. Nonetheless, exchange--correlation contributions to the ground-state energy can be computed numerically. The caveat is that it is very computationally demanding. What one typically does, is mixing the exact numerical functional with an approximated one, say at the \gls{lda} or \gls{gga} level. There is consensus in the community that using hybrid functionals allows better estimating the electronic bandgap than non-hybrid ones when compared with the experimental values~\cite{PhysRevMaterials.8.043803}.}.
For the sampling of $\bk$ points, we use a uniform $48\times48\times1$ Monkhorst--Pack $\bk$-grid~\cite{Monkhorst_Pack_1976}, with an interlayer distance of 500~\unit{\angstrom} and $a=2.51$~\unit{\angstrom} for the lattice constant.
The output file $\texttt{hBN\_base\_HSE06.outp}$ can be found in the official repository for the Xatu code~\cite{Xatu}; \url{https://github.com/xatu-code/xatu/tree/master/examples/material_models/DFT}.
This file contains the Fock matrices of the form $H(\bR)$ in a basis of localized Gaussian functions, sufficient in number to build the Bloch Hamiltonian $H(\bk) = \sum_{\bR} \ee^{\ii \bk \cdot \bR} H(\bR)$ while preserving basic symmetries.
Diagonalizing the Bloch Hamiltonian along the high-symmetry line $\Gamma-K-M-\Gamma$, we obtain the band structure displayed in Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_DFT_bands} below.
For this model, the bandgap is $\Delta = 6.08$ \unit{\electronvolt}, not very distant from the one reported in Section~\ref{subsec:band_gap_and_binding_energies}.
With this model Hamiltonian, namely the band structure and the eigenvectors of the Bloch Hamiltonian, we construct a numerical model for the screening properties of \gls{hbn}, needed later on to compute the excitons through the exact diagonalization method for the \bse, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:interaction_matrix_elements_and_screening}.
That is the subject of the sections to come.

\subsubsection{Local-field effects and convergence}
\label{subsubsec:inv_dielectric_matrix_convergence}

We study the convergence of the inverse dielectric matrix with the number of included reciprocal lattice vectors.
It is well known that including reciprocal lattice vectors in screening calculations is necessary due to local-field effects~\cite{Adler_1962,Wiser_1963}.
In the box below, we explain more precisely what we mean by local-field effects.

\begin{mybox}{Local-field effects}{box:local_field_effects}

As the name indicates, local-field effects pertain to the local details of the electric polarization, or equivalently of the electric field, that are often ignored when adopting phenomenological descriptions of dielectric properties of materials, often designated as macroscopic.
This is usually the case in Classical Electrodynamics, where the macroscopic Maxwell's equations~\eqref{eq:Maxwells_equations} reign.
Those equations are obtained from their microscopic counterparts, involving the microscopic electric and magnetic fields, after performing a spatial average of the rapidly varying local fields over distances big compared to the inter-atomic lattice spacing, but small compared to the overall size of the crystal~\cite{Jackson:electrodynamics,Landau_Electrodynamics_1984}\footnote{Considering the full equations including dynamics and time evolution, an average over long time scales is performed as well besides the spatial one, which is pointed out by J.~D.~Jackson in~\cite{Jackson:electrodynamics}, but that is irrelevant for the discussion.}.
More technically, applying a slowly varying external potential perturbation (weak enough so that we stay within the linear regime) of the form

\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\ext}(\bq,\omega) = \phi_0(\bq,\omega) \ei{(\bq \vdot \br - \omega t)}
\end{equation}

\noindent to the crystal, where $2\pi/\abs{\bq}$ is much smaller than its size.
The crystal responds and the total potential is the external one plus the induced one:

\begin{equation}
    \phi^{\tot} = \phi_{\ext} + \phi_{\ind} = \sum_{\bG} \phi(\bq,\bG,\omega)  \ei{[(\bq + \bG) \vdot \br - \omega t]}\,.
\end{equation}

It has all the $\bG$ Fourier components with wavevector $\bq + \bG$, where $\bG$ is a vector of the reciprocal lattice.
The bigger $\abs{\bG}$ is, the faster that component oscillates.
This potential is the microscopic one.
To extract the macroscopic or averaged one, we simply set $\bG = \bzero$, as $\exp{\ii \bq \vdot \br}$ varies slowly over the averaging region, while on the contrary the components $\exp{\ii (\bq + \bG) \vdot \br}$ vary rapidly over regions of the size of the unit cell, hence local-field effects are discarded on average (but does not mean that they do not affect the results, as we see in the results we obtain for the dielectric function after this box).
This discussion is adapted from~\cite{Adler_1962}.
\end{mybox}

This is a sensitive point---choosing an appropriate cutoff $G_c$ to compute the inverse dielectric matrix $\varepsilon^{-1}_{\bG \bG'} (\bq)$.
For the matrix elements to converge, we expect a high enough number of reciprocal lattice vectors is needed.
How high we will see.
We compute the matrix $\varepsilon^{-1}_{\bG \bG'} (\bq)$ for $\bq \neq 0$ with an increasing number of reciprocal lattice vectors included, and inspect four matrix elements: the head element, two elements in the body, a diagonal one and an off-diagonal one, and a wing element.
In Fig.~\ref{fig:hBN_inv_epsilon_convergence} below we showcase four matrix elements of the inverse dielectric matrix, one in each panel.
For each point in each curve, we applied a cutoff $G_c$ in integer multiples of $3 \,\text{\unit{\angstrom}}^{-1}$, although the horizontal axis shows the number of vectors included, and not the cutoff.
We can notice a convergence tendency with the number of reciprocal lattice vectors included, albeit quite slow.
Nonetheless, in the next subsection we study the dispersive behavior of the inverse dielectric matrix elements for a given cutoff $G_c$ assumed to be high enough.
The discussion will include an analysis on the macroscopic \bd~dielectric function, alongside its long-wavelength limit.
As promised at the end of the previous chapter, we will estimate $r_0$.

\subsection{Macroscopic dielectric function and the screening length $r_0$}
\label{subsec:macroscopic_dielectric_function_results}

In this section and with the results from the previous subsection, we compute the macroscopic dielectric function for \gls{hbn}, through the usual definition $\varepsilon_M (\bq) = \big(\varepsilon^{-1}_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq)\big)^{-1}$.
Our framework allows for directly obtaining the static \bd~effective dielectric function from this definition, which comes as a striking advantage when compared to typical \abinit~methods in three dimensions.
We display in Fig.~\ref{fig:epsilon2D_vs_q_hBN} the effective \bd~dielectric function for \gls{hbn} as a function of momentum $q=|\bq|$, where $\bq$ follows the same line as in Fig.~\ref{fig:inv_epsilon_vs_q_hBN}.
All the parameters from that figure carry over to the one below.
The numerically obtained dielectric function corresponds to the red curve, while the one in black is the dielectric function according to the Rytova--Keldysh model $\varepsilon_{\RK} (q) = 1 + r_0 q$, where the parameter $r_0$ was estimated from the low-$q$ behavior of the red curve.
With this model for \gls{hbn}, we obtained for the screening parameter $r_0 \approx 5.084$~\unit{\angstrom}.
This value is actually not close to the one we used in the previous chapter.
However, in the previous chapter the \gls{hbn} flake was supported by a quartz substrate, which affects the screening properties of the material~\cite{Trolle_Pedersen_Veniard_2017,}.
The value we used of $r_0 = 10$~\unit{\angstrom} has been used in previous works~\cite{Ferreira_2019,Ventura_2019}, showing good agreement with experiments with monolayer \gls{hbn} deposited on quartz~\cite{Cassabois_2019}.
In Ref.~\cite{Ventura_2019}, from the arguments of P.~Cudazzo, I.~V.~Tokatly, and A.~Rubio in Ref.~\cite{Cudazzo_2011}, the authors estimated $r_0 = 6.5$~\unit{\angstrom} for \gls{hbn}, knowing the material off-plane and in-plane bulk dielectric constants~\cite{Laturia_2018}.
Still in the same work, with the help of Refs.\cite{Trolle_Pedersen_Veniard_2017,Latini_2015}, the authors in Ref.~\cite{Ventura_2019} estimated $r_0 = 6.8$~\unit{\angstrom}.
Both values estimated in Ref.~\cite{Ventura_2019} are not that far from the one we get.

In Fig.~\ref{fig:epsilon2D_vs_q_hBN} one can notice as well a feature of the macroscopic dielectric function in the opposite limit of $q \to 0$.
When $q \to \infty$, the curve tends assimptotically to one, which is a universal characteristic shared among all \bd~insulators.
That is indeed what one sees in all the dielectric functions reported in the literature~\cite{Thygesen_2017,Latini_2015,Huser_Olsen_Thygesen_2013,Rasmussen_Schmidt_Winther_Thygesen_2016}, regardless of the material.
Physically, having $\varepsilon_M (q) \to 1$ when $q \to \infty$ means that, in the short-wavelength limit, the spatial variations of the external potential vary so fast (in space), that the system does not respond to the perturbation.
Therefore, test charges feel a total potential that coincides with the external potential---the internal charges do not realign in response to the external potential, which is completely unscreened.
Symbolically, $\phi^{\tot} = \phi^{\ind} + \phi^{\ext} \approx \phi^{\ext}$ for $q \to \infty$.
This is in sharp contrast with the prediction from the Rytova--Keldysh model, for which the external potential is overscreened, decaying with $\sim 1/q^2$ instead of $\sim 1/q$.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapters/Figures/Chapter_6/epsilon2D_vs_q_hBN}
    \mycaption[Effective 2D dielectric function for \gls{hbn}]{Macroscopic effective \bd~dielectric function of monolayer \gls{hbn}. We compare the full numerical $q$-dependence with the Rytova--Keldysh model, using the value for the screening parameter $r_0$ estimated by us.}
    \label{fig:epsilon2D_vs_q_hBN}
\end{figure}

During the cours of this work, we also found out that the parameter $r_0$ can actually be estimated directly from the head element of the dielectric function $\varepsilon_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq)$ by taking in it the $\bq \to \bzero$ limit.
This is not very surprising if we take into account that P.~Li and I.~Appelbaum, in Ref.~\cite{Li_Appelbaum_2019}, showed that the screening length can be calculated by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:r0_Li_Appelbaum}
    r_0 = \frac{e^2 \hbar^2}{4 \pi^2 \epsz m_0^2} \sum_{\vv \cc} \int \frac{|\mathbf{P}_{\cc \vv}(\bk)|^2}{(\epsilon_{\cc \bk} - \epsilon_{\vv \bk})^3}\, \dd \bk \,, 
\end{equation}

\noindent where the integral in momentum is in-plane, $m_0$ is the free electron mass, and $\mathbf{P}_{\cc \vv}(\bk) = \langle \cc, \bk |\mathbf{p}| \vv, \bk \rangle$ is the momentum matrix element.
This was proved, in the supplemental material of Ref.~\cite{Li_Appelbaum_2019}, by taking the static and $L_{\perp} \to \infty$ limits in the dynamical dielectric function of the respective bulk material (after taking the $q \to 0$ limit)\footnote{Nonetheless, it is not clear how aware the authors in Ref.~\cite{Li_Appelbaum_2019} were of the subtleties of writing dynamical response functions in terms of matrix elements of the momentum operator, as we discussed in Box~\ref{box:position_velocity_momentum_operators}}.
The expression in Eq.~\eqref{eq:r0_Li_Appelbaum} has been used before in the literature for exciton calculations in \bd~systems~\cite{Mauricio_2024}.

To settle the debate and get a better feeling for the validity of our calculations, let us compare our dielectric function directly with the one obtained from first-principles methods, instead of looking only at the small-$q$ limit behavior of the dielectric function.
We compare our results with those from S.~Latini, T.~Olsen, and K.~S.~Thygesen in Ref.~\cite{Latini_2015}, where the authors plot the effective \bd~dielectric function for \gls{hbn} and \ch{MoS2}, obtained from \abinit~methods.
Therein, the cutoff applied for the reciprocal lattice vectors is given in terms of energy, and it is $150$ \unit{\electronvolt}.
The cutoff for including local-field effects is usually given in terms of energy in the literature~\cite{BerkeleyGW,Latini_2015,Guandalini_Varsano_2023}, and the conversion from energy to wavevector is done through $E_c = \hbar^2 G_c^2/2 m_0$, where $m_0$ is the free electron mass.
When $E_c = 150$ \unit{\electronvolt}, we get $G_c \approx 6.27$ \unit{\angstrom^{-1}}.
In principle, this means that to compare results, we have to apply the same cutoff.
However, we will see that truncating the dielectric matrix at a smaller cutoff is sufficient to make comparisons.
For our model for \gls{hbn}, the smallest cutoff possible for including reciprocal lattice vectors other than the null one, is $G_c \approx 3.0$~\unit{\angstrom^{-1}}.
Only above $G_c = 5.0$ \unit{\angstrom^{-1}} are more reciprocal lattice vectors included.
We stress that we only have in-plane $\bG$ vectors.

We computed the dielectric function first for $G_c \approx 3.0$~\unit{\angstrom^{-1}}, and then for $G_c \approx 5.01$~\unit{\angstrom^{-1}}, choosing again the symmetry line $\Gamma-K$ for the momentum points $\bq$.
The results are displayed in Fig.~\ref{fig:epsilon2D_abinitio_vs_q_hBN} below.
It comes as a surprise for us, that the agreement between our results and theirs, represented by the dot-dashed red curve and the continuous black curve, respectively, is so good.
Not only the curves overlap in the small-$q$ limit, but they overlap as well for higher values of momentum $q$ inside the \bz~and further apart from the origin $\Gamma$.
Naturally, the agreement between the curves ceases for a given value of momentum $q$, which is related with the finite thickness of the material $\dd_{\perp}$.
When $q \dd_{\perp}  \lesssim 1$, we expect for our strict approach to agree with the \abinit~one, while for $q \dd_{\perp}  \gtrsim 1$ we expect for the two approaches to screening disagree.

Even though the effective \bd~dielectric function through \abinit~methods can only be obtained after performing an off-plane average on the inverse dielectric matrix, which can be a bit cumbersome, first-principles approaches have the advantage of naturally incorporating the finite thickness of the material.
Apparently, taking the finite thickness into account is not required to have a good estimate of the low-$q$ behavior of the dielectric function, and therefore, to have an estimate of $r_0$.
Not only that, but Fig.~\ref{fig:epsilon2D_abinitio_vs_q_hBN} highlights an interesting aspect of the low-$q$ dielectric properties of a \bd~material; the very low-$q$ dielectric response seems to not depend on the local-field effects.
We see this by comparing the red dot-dashed curve and the black curve with the blue one, which was obtained by applying a slightly higher cutoff of $G_c$.
On the other hand, one can conclude that local-field effects are crucial for higher $q$.
We have numerically verified, but do not present in this dissertation, that ignoring local-field totally highly overestimates the screening.
In math notation, we mean that $\varepsilon_{\bzero \bzero} (q) \gg \big(\varepsilon^{-1}_{\bzero \bzero} (q)\big)^{-1}$ for intermediate $q$.
If the cutoff is not high enough, then the dielectric response comes out overscreened.
For us, applying the lowest possible cutoff (above zero) is sufficient to replicate the \abinit~results, at least for $q \dd_{\perp}  \lesssim 1$.
By increasing more the cutoff, our dielectric function gets underestimated when compared to the \abinit~one, as it can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:epsilon2D_abinitio_vs_q_hBN}, when comparing our blue dashed curve and the \abinit~one in black.
At this point, we cannot say if the disagreement for higher $q$ is also in part due to the approximation of point-like orbital we used to compute the plane-wave matrix elements between Bloch states, which are typically computed exactly in \abinit~codes via fast Fourier transform algorithms~\cite{BerkeleyGW}.
At least for low momenta, this approximation does not set us back.

Now that we have successfully computed the dielectric function, it is now time to compute the excitons using the numerical model for screening.
Knowing the excitonic states and energies, just like in the previous chapter in Section~\ref{sec:exciton_optical_response}, we can compute the excitonic optical conductivity.
The excitons and the excitonic optical response are the subject of the next subsection.

\subsection{Excitonic states and conductivity}
\label{subsec:excitons_results}

To see the effect the numerical screening has on the excitons, we computed the excitons using the xatu code with the two different models for screening possible.
In both calculations, we used the same set of parameters, apart from the way the interaction matrix elements are computed.
We used a $60\times60$ $\bk$-grid to sample the \bz, included one valence band, $N_{\vv}=1$, and one conduction band, $N_{\cc}=1$, applied a cutoff for the reciprocal lattice vectors of $G_c = 3.0$ \unit{\angstrom^{-1}}, and for the regularization radius, we used a percentage of $1.3\%$.
This means for the regularization radius $q_0 = 1.3 k_0$, where $k_0$ is the norm of the \bz~momentum closest to the origin.
When using the Rytova--Keldysh model for screening, the direct interaction matrix element is computed through Eq.~\eqref{eq:direct_interaction_matrix_element_reciprocal_RK}, where for the screening length we used the value obtained by us of $r_0 = 5.084$~\unit{\angstrom}.
Using the numerical (inverse) dielectric function, the same interaction matrix element is computed through Eq.~\eqref{eq:interaction_matrix_element_direct_reciprocal}, but with the volume $\vol$ replaced by the area $\area$.

The numerical results for the excitons are displayed in Table~\ref{tab:hBN_DFT_HSE_06_exciton_spectrum} below, showing the excitonic levels of the first eigth excitons.
The values on the left were obtained using the Rytova--Keldysh dielectric function, while the ones on the right were obtained using the numerical dielectric matrix.
First, one can notice that for the Rytova--Keldysh calculation, the states are not as close to the expected degeneracies as they are for the numerical screening calculation.
For the Rytova--Keldysh calculation, this does not come as a surprise, since it is know that the binding energy for \gls{hbn} excitons are of the highest amongs all \bd~insulators.
This means that they are localized in real space, and therefore delocalized in momentum space.
Henceforth, one needs a high enough cutoff $G^{X}_c$ for the interaction matrix elements to recover the expected degeneracies and symmetries.
This fact, that we touched on at the end of Subsection~\ref{subsec:interaction_matrix_elements_and_screening}, has been discussed before in the literature by E.~Ridolfi et al.~in Ref.~\cite{Ridolfi_2015}, and verified again more recently in Ref.~\cite{Xatu}.

In Table~\ref{tab:hBN_DFT_HSE_06_exciton_spectrum}, we can also verify that using the Rytova--Keldysh model the excitonic states are higher in energy when compared to the ones obtained using $\varepsilon_{\bG,\bG'}$, by looking at the second and third columns.
If we consider only the the head term $W_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq)$ of the numerical screened potential and compare it with the Rytova--Keldysh potential $V_{\RK} (\bq)$, knowing that the Rytova--Keldysh model overestimates the screening, then the attraction between the electron and the hole is weaker than the one coming from $W_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq) = \varepsilon^{-1}_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq) v_c(\bq) = v_c(\bq)/\varepsilon_M (\bq)$, since $\varepsilon_M (\bq) < \varepsilon_{\RK} (\bq)$.
This means that the excitons obtained with $V_{\RK} (q)$ should be higher in energy than the ones from using $W_{\bG \bG'}(\bq)$.
In terms of binding energies, displayed in the fourth and fifth colums of the table, the excitons obtained with the Rytova--Keldysh model present lower binding energies when compared to the excitonic binding energies obtained with the numerical dielectric function.
In Fig.~\ref{fig:Coulomb_RK_W00_potential} we illustrate the $q$-space Coulomb attraction between the electron and the hole by plotting the bare Coulomb potential $v_c(q)$ in black, the Rytova--Keldysh potential $V_{\RK} (q)$ in blue, and the head element of the screened potential $W_{\bzero \bzero} (q)$ in red.
Since the Rytova--Keldysh dielectric function is obtained from $1/\varepsilon^{-1}_{\bzero \bzero} (\bq)$, in the long-wavelength limit the two screened potentials coincide.
However, for intermediate $q$, the Rytova--Keldysh potential underestimates the electrostatic Coulomb interaction, decaying to zero faster than $W_{\bzero \bzero} (q)$ and the Coulomb potential, as we have already pointed out in Subsection~\ref{subsec:macroscopic_dielectric_function_results}.
For high $q$, the head element $W_{\bzero \bzero} (q)$ of the screened potential and the unscreened potential $v_c(q)$ approach each other.

For this model for \gls{hbn}, with the electronic bandgap being of $\Delta = 6.08$~\unit{\electronvolt}, we obtain for the binding energy of the excitonic ground state $E_b = 2.47$~\unit{\electronvolt}.
Honestly, it is not very close to other values previously obtained in the literature using full \gls{bse} \abinit~methods.
For instance, in Ref.~\cite{Latini_2015}, the authors obtained a binding energy of $E_b = 2.05$~\unit{\electronvolt}, while in Ref.~\cite{Zhang_Louie_2022} a value of $E_b = 2.08$~\unit{\electronvolt} was reported.
However, in none of those works do the autors use a hybrid functional for the \dft~calculations as we do.
Even though with different Hamiltonian models one can get similar dielectric functions, the exciton properties of the material may depend more on model.
Even if it is the same material, having a different band structure, specially a different bandgap, may naturally lead to different exciton binding energies.

At last, we compare the optical conductivity of \gls{hbn} computed with both methods.
Unlike the \gls{bse} calculations, the optical conductivity is obtained always in the same way.
We use it just as it was implemented in the first version of the xatu code, in Ref.~\cite{Xatu}.
Therein, the conductivity is computed in terms of velocity matrix elements, instead of using the dipole operator approach from the previous chapter.
The expressions~\eqref{eq:non_interacting_Re_optical_conductivity} and~\eqref{eq:excitonic_Re_optical_conductivity} are the ones we use now to compute the conductivity at the \gls{ipa} and excitonic level.
In the last chapter, we focused solely on the excitonic conductivity.
On the contrary, as both noninteracting and interacting conductivities are implemented in the code, we plot both for the sake of completion.
The noninteracting one does not depend on how one computes the excitons, logically.

Before we comment on the conductivity obtained with the xatu code, for the sake of having a as self-contained discussion as possible, we brifly explain how the conuctivity is computed, in particular the velocity matrix elements.
We start by recalling the expression for the real part of the elements of the excitonic conductivity tensor from Chapter~\ref{ch:Excitons}, which reads

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:excitonic_Re_optical_conductivity_repeated}
    \Re{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}=\frac{\pi e^{2}\hbar}{\area}\sum_{\nu}\frac{1}{E_{\nu}} V^{\alpha}_{\nu} (V^{\beta}_{\nu})^{*} \delta(\hbar \omega - E_{\nu})\,,
\end{equation}

\noindent with the volume $\vol$ replaced by the area $\area$, as the material is \bd, with the interacting velocity matrix element for the excitonic state $\nu$ given in terms of the velocity matrix elements $v^{\alpha}_{\cc  \vv }(\bk) = \langle \cc,\bk |v^{\alpha}| \vv,\bk \rangle$\footnote{In the light of what was discussed in Box~\ref{box:position_velocity_momentum_operators} and to be more rigorous, we can say that this matrix element is an integral in real space extending over the whole material, considered finite and obeying periodic boundary conditions.} by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:excitonic_velocity_matrix_elements_repeated}
    V^{\alpha}_{\nu} = \langle \GS |v^{\alpha}| X_{\nu}\rangle = \sum_{\vv \cc, \bk} A^{\bQ}_{\vv \cc}(\bk) (\bk) v^{\alpha}_{\vv \cc}(\bk)\,.
\end{equation}

\noindent By virtue of the identity $\mathbf{v} = - \ii/\hbar [H_0,\br]$ (see Box~\ref{box:position_velocity_momentum_operators}), it is possible to evaluate the velocity matrix elements in a Bloch basis, and they are given by~\cite{Xatu,Esteve_2023,Esteve_PhD}

\begin{multline}
    \label{eq:velocity_matrix_elements_Bloch_basis}
    v^a_{vc} (\bk) = \sum_{i \alpha i' \alpha'} \left(C^{n\bk}_{i\alpha}\right)^* C^{n'\bk}_{i'\alpha'} \nabla_{\bk} H_{i\alpha i'\alpha'}(\bk)  \\
    +i \sum_{i \alpha i' \alpha'} \left(C^{n\bk}_{i\alpha}\right)^* C^{n'\bk}_{i'\alpha'} \left[ \epsilon_{n\bk} \xi_{i \alpha i' \alpha'}(\bk) - \epsilon_{n'\bk} \xi_{i \alpha i' \alpha'}^*(\bk)\right]
\end{multline}

\noindent where the quantity $\xi_{i \alpha i' \alpha'}(\bk) = \ii \mel{u_{i\alpha \bk}}{\nabla_\bk}{u_{i\alpha'\bk}}$ is the Berry connection (see footnote~\ref{fn:Berry_connection}), and evaluates to~\cite{Xatu,Esteve_2023,Esteve_PhD}

\begin{equation}
    \xi_{i \alpha i' \alpha'}(\bk) = \sum_{\bR} \ei{\bk \cdot \bR} \mel{i \alpha \bzero}{\hat{\br}}{i \alpha' \bR} + \ii \nabla_\bk S_{i \alpha i' \alpha '} (\bk) \,,
\end{equation}

\noindent where we recall that $S_{i \alpha i' \alpha '} (\bk) = \braket{i \alpha \bk}{i' \alpha' \bk}$ is a matrix element of the momentum-defined overlap matrix (see Eq.~\ref{eq:Bloch_overlap_definition}).
In the tight-binding approximation, or in the limit of maximally localized Wannier functions, only the intra-atomic position operator matrix elements are nonzero, and it is not hard to show that the matrix elements in Eq.~\ref{eq:velocity_matrix_elements_Bloch_basis} evaluate to

\begin{multline}
     \mel{n\bk}{\hat{v}}{n'\bk} = \sum_{i \alpha i' \alpha'} \left(C^{n\bk}_{i\alpha}\right)^* C^{n'\bk}_{i'\alpha'}\Bigg[ \frac{\partial H_{i \alpha i' \alpha'}(\bk)}{\partial k_a} \\
     +  \ii H_{i \alpha i' \alpha'}(\bk)(t^a_{i'} - t^a_{i})\Bigg] \,,
\end{multline}

\noindent which involves $k$-derivatives of the Bloch Hamiltonian matrix elements; typically, one needs a more refined, or dense, \bz~mesh to make the conductivity converge.
For the Dirac delta part, we use typically use a Lorentzian broadening of the form $\delta (x) \to \frac{1}{\pi} \frac{\Gamma}{x^2 + \Gamma^2}$, where $\Gamma$ is the broadening parameter.

For the sake of brevity, we did not rederive the expression for the noninteracting optical conductivity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:excitonic_Re_optical_conductivity_repeated} from the dielectric function, which is now \bd, as we did in Subsection~\eqref{subsec:non_interacting_conductivity}.
It is an instructive exercise to do so, but to do so, we need to have a caveat in mind.
The equivalence between the dielectric function and the optical conductivity from Chapter~\ref{ch:classical_electrodynamics} does not apply, because we implicitly assumed three dimensions for deriving it.
One can see that the same expression cannot be used, because the potential now goes with $\sim 1/(2\epsz q)$ instead of $\sim 1/(\epsz q^2)$.
Then, we need the \bd~counterpart of the same relation, which is derived in Appendix~\ref{app:2D_optical_conductivity}.
As one can see in that appendix, the permittivity--conductivity equivalence relation new reads $\varepsilon = 1 + \ii q \sigma / (2 \epsz \omega)$, instead of the old one $\varepsilon = 1 + \ii \sigma / (\epsz \omega)$.
The extra $q/2$ factor going with the conductivity compensates for the conversion of the Coulomb potential from $v^{\mathrm{3D}}_c(q) = e/(\epz q^2) $ to $ v^{\BD}_c(q) = e/(2\epz q)$.
